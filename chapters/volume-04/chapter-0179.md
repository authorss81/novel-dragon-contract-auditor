# Chapter 179: What the Blankness Is

Marek Kest worked that out at about the second hour of the fourth day of the third week of the first month of the year after, in a room in a city about four hundred and thirty miles up this river, with a roll of holdings open on a table and a woman of twenty-four standing at a window and a clerk of thirty-four sitting down because she has been on her feet for six days.

He had been carrying the plain meaning of it for about a month and it had not meant anything, and at about the second hour on that fourth day it meant one thing and it meant it plainly. Nobody in the history of this empire has ever been told that being made poor was the way they were made safe, and the reason nobody has ever been told is that it was done correctly, and the person who did it was the person it was done to, and she was the only one in the room who could.

It took about nine minutes. Everything after that took the rest of the week and most of him.

---

"There is an office," he said. "A human one. Old. Supposed to be temporary. Seven years and no more, and the person who holds it is chosen in a room with people in it and not inherited by anybody, and there is a thing in the middle of it that I have never seen written down anywhere and I am telling you second-hand and I am going to say so every time I say it."

"All right," said Marn Ottery.

"**A person who holds it has to have no enforceable inherited asset or right.** Not be poor. Hold nothing that anybody can put their hand on. And I have had that sentence in my head since the fourth week of the twelfth month of next year and I have not been able to make it mean anything, and it means one thing and it means it plainly, and it has meant it plainly the whole time."

"Say the plain thing."

"The test is not a test about being poor. **A family that holds nothing enforceable is not a poor family.** It is an eligible one."

---

The room was very quiet and there was a press in it with a rail across the front of it and about four hundred blanks in a box under a counter on the other side of the room that neither of them was looking at.

"Go on," said Tamsin Rook.

"That is the whole of the test and there is no other half of it and I have been reading it for a month as though there were a second half." He put his hand flat on the table and took it off again. "**I have been eligible since the day my mother's name came out of a file.** I did not know it. I was not told. There is nobody in this empire who was ever going to tell me, and that is not carelessness and it is not an enemy's doing. It is what she arranged, and she arranged it, and the arrangement is the thing that stopped me from ever being asked."

---

And he said the three things, in that order, and he has written in his own book that the order was the whole of it and that he tried it in the other two orders first and in both of the other two orders it came out as two things instead of one.

**The first thing: it was real, and it was a child.**

"A child is going to be put into a thing with a guild's name on it and a term on it and a house's name under the guild's, and I have read the form those things are done on and it is not a bad form and the guild is not lying on it." He was not reading anything. "And the person who stopped it was right about the guild. That is not in dispute and I am not going to pretend it is. **A woman with a pen made a family hold nothing so that a child could not be handed over.** She did it on a box, in about nine minutes, and she was not asked where the idea came from, and she did not say."

"And that is a kindness," said Tamsin Rook.

"It is a kindness and it is a wrong use of a form and it is the same act and there is no version of this in which you can have the first without the second, and I have been trying to have the first without the second since the fourth week of the twelfth month and I cannot do it and I do not think anybody can." He looked at the roll. "**A box is a machine.** She used a machine to do a thing a machine cannot do, and the machine did it, and the machine is still there, and it is still the same machine, and it is available to the next person who can reach it."

---

**The second thing: it did not close anything.**

"A disputed claim is a recorded claim. That is the definition at the back of that roll and I have read it out to two people in the last hour and neither of them has said a word." He turned the roll round. "**A claim that is recorded has got a name on it, and a name on a claim is a road.** A road does not care in the slightest whether the claim can be enforced, because a road is a thing a person can walk on and the walking is the whole of it."

"What does that buy," said Marn Ottery, "for the person it was done to."

"Everything she wanted and one thing she did not know she was buying." He had it in about four seconds and it had been in him for a month and he had not let it up. "**The whole of what she bought is that nothing can be given to me.** Not a duty, not a shilling, not a guild, not a house, not a term. Nothing. And that is a shield, and there is nothing in this empire that can get past it."

"And the cost."

"**The cost is that I am the cheapest person in this empire for anybody who wants a name in a column and nothing behind the name.** A man with money behind him can be sued and a man with a house behind him can be attached and a man with nothing behind him can be made to answer for nothing at all, because there is nothing to be liable for and because nobody can serve a person who has nothing to serve. She made me free. And a free man with a recorded claim in his name is not a safe man, he is a cheap man, and cheapness of that exact shape is what somebody has been looking for in this empire for a long time."

---

Nobody said the name of the thing that uses a road, and it was not said in that room, and it is not written here, and there is a reason for that which Tamsin Rook gave out loud and in the ordinary way and it is the reason the batch does not go near it.

"There is a historical question in this about something that was done to office-holders in an earlier century, and it is a real question and it has never been answered by any court that has asked it," she said. "**Naming a question is not the same as answering it.** I know that because there is a book in this city with about nine questions in it and not one of them has an answer at the back, and I have read all of them and I have wanted the answers since I was your age. And it is not this room and not this week and I am not going to let it into this room, because the moment it is in the room, it is the biggest thing in the room, and the biggest thing in the room is the thing nobody is looking at, which is a child who was in a room and was not asked."

---

**The third thing, and it is the one he has written down, with the day on it.**

"A family that can be given nothing cannot be served. A person who cannot be served cannot be told things. **A person who cannot be told cannot be asked. And a person who cannot be asked cannot say no.**"

"And there it is," said Tamsin Rook.

"There it is." He did not look at either of them. "She did not conceal it from me. That is not what happened and anybody who tells you that happened is telling you a comfortable story and it is not true. She made me unservable, and then she could not tell me, and the not-telling was the making, and the making was the not-telling. **The protection is the concealment and the concealment is the protection.** A person who cannot be asked anything cannot decline anything, and a person who cannot decline anything cannot refuse anything, and a person who cannot refuse anything is safe, and that is what it looks like from the inside, and I have been on the inside of it for about four months and I did not know and I would not have wanted to know and that is the last part I am not going to soften."

---

A girl of seventeen on a bank about four hundred and thirty miles down this river said half of it out loud, in a bay, in the second week of the eighth month of next year, and nobody in that bay followed the answer.

He has written that down too, with the day on it, and the words she used were these, as he has them, and he has not altered them and has said so in the margin:

*A person made blank of enforceable rights is not made safe. The blankness is a door with a person in it. And the person is nearly always the one who did the blanking, because she is the one who could.*

"That is about two thirds of what I have just said and she said it on a platform in the cold to about thirty people in a bay and nobody answered her, and she was paid forty-five pence a day to read things out loud and was not paid to think and was not thanked, and she did not follow the answer on purpose, and she gave the reason out loud, which was that a person who is paid to read is a person who can be told to stop, and she was not going to turn it into the other thing." He shut the roll. "**This is the answer being followed, and it does not improve.** She is a girl of seventeen and she was right. I am going to write her name in my own book and I am not going to write anything else of hers in it, because she has not been asked, and there is a column of that register with the office's name in it in every line, and I am not going to be a second one."

---

Nobody forgave anybody.

There was no forgiveness in that room and there was not going to be and everybody in it understood that inside about four minutes, and nobody said so out loud, because saying it out loud would have been a thing done to make the room easier.

Marek Kest did not say his mother was right. Tamsin Rook did not put her own work down. Marn Ottery did not say she would have done the same, and did not say she would not have. **Nobody said anything at all about a dead woman except one fact.** The fact is that she was a person who could be made to answer for nothing, and she did it to herself, on purpose, with a pen, in about nine minutes, and that is a thing a record can hold and a person cannot forgive.

And the witness on that line is dead, and the register has been saying in the office's name for a hundred and forty years that she was told, and the only person left in that room is in the third column under no heading at all, and she has been asked the fifth line of a printed form, once, in a kitchen, and it is the first question in a hundred and forty years that anybody was entitled to put to her, and nobody who was in that room is going to put another one to her.

---

And there is one more thing in that room, and it is not part of the three, and Marn Ottery brought it herself at about the fifth hour of the same day and would not leave it where it was.

She had the register out of the third gallery and she had it open on a table and her hand flat on the fifth column.

"I have been sitting with this since the eighth hour yesterday," she said. "I have got about four hundred lines in it in five columns and the fifth one is wrong in every one of them, and I have got a printed form four feet away with the fifth line on it, and the fifth line is a question, and the answer to it is standing in the four columns either side of it, and not one of the five has got a place to put it. **So I can correct it, and I want that said first.** Everybody in this room is about to hear the worst of it and the worst of it is that I can do it."

"Go on," said Marek Kest.

"One line at a time, by hand, in my own hand, with the day against it. That is all it is. There is no form for correcting a register and there does not need to be, because a register is a book and a book is corrected in a hand. **I could have the whole of it right in about eleven years of an afternoon a week.** I would be doing it in my own hand, and my hand would be comparable against my hand for the next forty years."

"That is the archivist's method," said Tamsin Rook.

"That is the archivist's method exactly, and she gave it to a stranger for nothing in a back room on a flood terrace and she said out loud that it was available to anybody with a pen and a quiet afternoon, and she was right, and I have been trying since yesterday to work out whether the fact that it is available to me is a reason to do it." She shut the register and did not open it again. "**And I am not going to do it, and I want the reason said out loud in a room.** I want it said once. I have been working on it since yesterday and it has taken me longer than the thing itself did."

"Because you are a party," said Marek Kest.

"Because I am a party and I am the one who would be doing it. **A person with nothing enforceable behind them can be made to answer for nothing.** The price of a correction made in my hand is that my name goes at the foot of about four hundred of them, and a woman with a name at the foot of four hundred of them is a woman who can be served about all of them, and I am the cheapest person in this building and I have been cheap for about a month and I found that out about four days ago."

"That is the wrong reason," said Tamsin Rook.

"It is the *first* reason and it is a real one and I am not going to pretend I did not think of it first." Marn Ottery looked at the shut register. "**The second reason is a man of thirty-eight on a bank about four hundred and thirty miles down this river.** He has been in a bay with about thirty people in it since the second month of next year and he refused to rule a fourth column in a company's own ledger in about nine seconds flat, and he gave as his reason that a column ruled in a man's own hand is a heading over a page in about four years put there by a man who did not stand in the room. I have never met him and I have never been in that bay. I found that out about a month ago from a woman of twenty-four and I have been carrying it since."

Nobody said anything.

"And the third reason is that the whole of what was done to him was done on a box, by a person, in a room, in about nine minutes, and I have just found out what the box was for and where it got to, and I am not going to be the next person in that line. **There is no version of me correcting that register in which I am not the next person in that line.** The only difference is that mine would be in ink and dated."

---

The counter opened at the fourth hour on the sixth day and stayed open until the sixth, and about a hundred blanks came out of the box under it over those two hours and went into other people's hands, and a girl of about twenty-five at the intake desk wrote a file number in ink and initialled it and was not thanked, and a man of fifty came to the counter and paid to have a thing copied and it was copied and read back to him twice, the second reading slower, and he said that was the first time anybody had done it that way and asked who had taught her and she said she was not going to say.

Nothing in that building stopped for about four hours on the third week of the first month of the year after, and that is not a figure of speech and it is the reason the finding took until about the fifth hour to arrive, and it is also the reason nothing that was found out in it is going to be in a hurry.

---

He wrote it in his own book that night in a room he had paid for out of his own money, and the book has no office on it, and he wrote the day on it, in his own hand, and he read it back twice, the second reading slower.

He did not cross anything out. There is nothing in that book to cross out, because he has not written any of it before, and the reason he did not write it before is in the first line of it.

He did not write his mother's name at the top of the page.

He wrote, as the heading of four pages, five words, and they are these, and the choice of them took him longer than anything else he has done in about four months:

*What the blankness is for.*

And then, underneath, in a different line and no smaller and no larger, a second heading, because one heading is a machine and a man of thirty-eight has spent four months watching a book-keeper on a bank refuse to rule a fourth column, and a heading is a heading over a column and a column is a machine and **a machine puts a name in a book so that the name can never be asked anything.**

> *What the blankness is for.*
> *Written by a person it was done to, who was not asked.*

---

There is a line about two inches long on the inside of his left wrist and it read nothing this week and it decided nothing this week and it does not know where a ford is, and he looked at it in a rented room at about the eleventh hour and it was the only thing about the whole of the four months that had not cost anybody anything, and it was not the price and it was not the protection and it was not the arrangement.

The wage was the price. The wage is seventeen shillings and a penny the week, and five journeys and about thirty days of it, and now six, and a clerk of thirty-four's four days of a shut counter, and a mason who opened a door for a wall and would leave if anybody thanked him, and a woman of sixty-three who wanted to know what she had written down, and a reader of seventeen four hundred and thirty miles away who is not thanked and is not going to start and has not been told any of this.

And a woman who is twenty and has a lane and a shop and a refused column and a brother's letter of the sixteenth of the eighth of last year that is still unanswered and is still hers to leave unanswered.

He did not send her a word. He has not sent her a word since the last week of the seventh month of next year and he was not going to start now, and the reason is not in the four pages and it is in the margin in four words, and the four words are: *a family finds out from itself.*

And the last thing he wrote that night, at about the eleventh hour, having read the whole of it back twice, was one line and it is the only line in four pages that is about anybody else, and it is about a person who is not in this matter and is not going to be told anything:

*She was the only one in the room who could, and she was the one it was done to, and there is no version of it in which those are two people.*
