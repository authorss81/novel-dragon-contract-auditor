# Chapter 185: Fourpence, and the Count of Columns

Nell Kest got off a cart in Auremar at about the fifth hour of the third week into the fourth week of the second month of the year after, with blue in the second knuckle of the third finger of her right hand and fourpence in her hand and a reason for coming that she had not put on paper, and she had been in the city about two hours when she found out that her brother had put something of hers on a board.

Nell Kest is twenty. She is a dye-house worker, which is what she is paid for, and she keeps the books of the eleven houses on a dyers' lane in Greyfen and of four other houses in three other districts, which is what she is not paid for and has done since she was seventeen.

She came to see him. She has not told him she is coming and she is not going to, and the reason is that if she told him she would have to give a reason, and the reason she has is about a column with no heading over it, and that is not a thing she does in a doorway.

---

The board was in a passage off the same building as the fourth counter, at the end of a run of about nine feet of stall, and there were nine other things on the other boards in the passage and none of them had anything to do with anything.

The first line on the card over the board said what it was. She read it twice, standing up, in the way she reads a levy roll, which is the way anybody who counts things for a living reads a thing with numbers in it and is not a thing anybody else does.

*These are nine lines written in the hand of the second person in a room.*

*They are the fourth column of one line of a register of witnesses kept in a house in a lane about four miles off the old river road. A register of witnesses has five columns.*

She read the second line about four times.

---

She bought a copy. Fourpence, the ordinary rate, in full, and the printer's man did not ask her name and did not ask what it was for and did not thank her, and there is no form anywhere that says she was thanked, and she did not expect to be and would have been embarrassed.

Then she read it on a step in the passage with her back to the wall, and the nine lines took her about a minute and a half and the fourth one stopped her.

> *A person who holds nothing cannot be given anything and that is the whole of it and it is the best of it.*

**That is our house.** She said it to herself on a step in a passage in a city about four hundred and thirty miles from the lane, in words, and nobody heard her, and she is not a person who says a thing like that out loud in a room, and a step in a passage is not a room.

And then the next thing, and it came about nine seconds later and it came in the wrong order, which is how the real ones come.

**"The house is to hold nothing." She is writing about a house and she is not writing about a building.**

She has kept the books of eleven houses on a lane for three years and there has not been one day in that time when she did not know that a house in a levy roll is a building and that a building can be sold. And a woman about four miles off a road wrote four hundred yards from a lane where eleven houses are standing *the house is to hold nothing* and meant something else entirely, and the something else is about the people in it, and it is her family, and it was written by somebody who was in a room and was not asked where the idea came from.

She put the copy inside her coat and she did not go and find her brother and she has not, and she will not, and the reason is at the end of this and it is not the reason anybody would expect.

---

What she did instead was turn the copy over and count on the back of it, because there was a step, and a pencil, and about nine minutes, and counting is the only thing she has ever been able to do with a feeling.

She did the eleven houses. It takes her about four minutes and she does it out of her head and she has done it every week since she was seventeen, and it is not the levy and it is not the days and it is the count she keeps for herself and has never shown anybody.

**Pieces out on the frames on the last day of the week, by house. Vat-hours. What the mill takes and what the mill does not take. And then the number that is not a number and that she has been keeping for three years without a heading over it anywhere, which is the number of days worked by a person in each house, and it is the number the fourth column has wanted since before she was seventeen, and she has it, and she has never written it down.**

A woman of about forty at the next board, who had been waiting to ask about a copy of a will and had given up waiting, watched her do the second one.

"How do you do that without writing it."

"You do it every week," said Nell Kest, "and after about two years it is not remembering. It is a place in your head with your hand on it, and if somebody asks you a number you do not think of a number, you put your hand on the place and the number is already there, and that is the whole of arithmetic. It is not that you are clever. It is that you have been somewhere the same number of times."

"That is a good answer," said the woman of about forty, "and I have been standing at this board for about a quarter of an hour and I have not got what I came for, and it is a copy of a will, and it costs fourpence, and I have fourpence."

"Then you should buy it before you go," said Nell Kest, "**because fourpence is the whole of what a copy costs and it is the whole of what anybody can find out, and there is a difference between those two and the difference is about four years.**"

The woman of about forty bought the copy of the will and went and did not say thank you and Nell Kest did not expect her to and turned the sentence over in her head on the walk to the cart and decided it was the best thing she knew about her own trade and also the most useless.

---

She went out of the passage and up Sallowgate, and about halfway along it she went past a counter with a queue out of the door and a board in the window with a price in it, and the price was a shilling a question and fourpence a copy and a small crowd standing in the cold to be told something they could have written down.

She stood behind about four people in the queue for about a minute and then did not join it.

She did the arithmetic standing at the back of the queue, in her head, in about four seconds, because that is what she does. A shilling buys somebody about four answers and a person is not allowed to ask the same thing twice, and fourpence buys one answer, and one is a document and the other is a conversation, and the only one of the two that can be shown to somebody else afterwards is the fourpence one.

**A shilling is what a person pays to be told. Fourpence is what a person pays so that it can be shown to a person they have not met. She has paid fourpence twice today and has not been told anything by anybody, and she is going to be able to show both of them to her brother, and that is the whole of what fourpence is for.**

And a person who cannot be told is not a person she can be rude to, and that is the next part of it, and she got to it about nine seconds later on a street about four hundred and thirty miles from a lane of eleven houses.

Then she counted.

That is what she is and there is no other thing she can do. Up Sallowgate, past the queue, she stopped at a counter where a woman sold printed forms of every kind of sheet a person in this empire can be entered on, and she paid for that as well, fourpence, and she was not thanked for that either, and the two fourpences together were a morning's work out of a wage she has not got a figure printed anywhere.

She asked for the sheet a witness is entered upon.

> *The first person says the thing in a room and the first person does not read it back afterwards.*
> *The day is entered in the first person's own hand and in nobody else's.*
> *The second person is entered in the room and the second person does not read it back to the person and does not show it to them.*
> *The second person writes what was said and writes nothing else.*
> *The second person asks the person, before the thing is said, whether she is a party to it, and the answer is written here.*

Five lines.

She counted them on the wall of the passage with the flat of her finger and she did not touch the paper, and she counted them again with her finger on the rail of the counter in case the first count had been about something else.

**Five. And a register of witnesses has five columns, and a book that is kept from a sheet is the shape of the sheet, because that is what a book is.**

And a thing she had been carrying for three years without knowing she was carrying it arrived at her on a step, and it is this: **a column with no heading over it is the same book with the machine taken out**, and she wrote that in the fourth book of an association of dyers in a town she can walk to, on a page, in her own hand, with the day on it, and it is the only true thing she has ever put in a book, and she has never once known there was another one.

---

She worked out the rest of it standing up, and it took about nine minutes, and she did it the way she does everything, which is by counting, and she has never in her life worked anything out any other way.

**A register of witnesses is a copy of that sheet, and the columns are the lines, in order. The first column is the first line, and the first line is the person who said the thing, and the name in the first column of that line is the person who said it, and it says Ilyra Kest.**

So her mother is in a register of witnesses about four miles off a road four hundred and thirty miles down a river, in the first column, and the first column has a name in it and that is the only thing anybody ever put there.

**The second column is the day. The third column is the second person. The fourth column is what was said, and that is what he printed. The fifth column is the question, and the question is the one nobody has ever asked.**

And then she stopped counting and started doing the other thing, which is the thing she is actually good at, which is the thing about a house in a book.

The third column is a person who is in a room and is not the one being written about.

A column with a heading over it is a machine for turning one thing into another kind of thing, and she has known that since she was seventeen and she has never been able to say where she got it. And a column whose whole content is a person who was in the room and is not the one on trial is **a column with a heading over it that says a job and not a name**, and every person in it for a hundred and forty years has been reduced to a function, and the function has a column, and the person does not.

And a dyer of twenty about four hundred and thirty miles from that house has spent three years refusing to write a name in a column with no heading over it, in a book about eleven houses, in a town she can walk to, and the reason she gave at the time is in the book in her own hand and it is the same reason.

**The reason is that a name in a column is a record of a person, and a record of a person can be asked about by anybody with a copy of the right page and four years, and a person cannot.**

---

There is one difference and she found it and it is the difference and it is why she could not put the book down.

In the fourth book of the dyers, the fourth column had a heading once. About sixty years ago somebody struck that heading through with a pen and not a rule, and ruled a line under it in a different ink, and left it. And six years ago a woman of about sixty-eight in a lane six hours away had looked at that and said no in writing and asked for her no to be entered and gave no name.

**Somebody took a machine out of that book and they knew they were doing it, and they left a line where the machine had been.**

The third column of a register of witnesses has no heading at all, and nobody took one out, because there was never one there to take out, and about nine hundred second persons have been written into it in nine hundred buildings for about a hundred and forty years without anybody noticing that a column should have had anything over it at all.

**And that is why the woman in it cannot be found by name. She is not hidden, and there is nothing over her and nothing under her, and what is in that column is a set of hands and not a name at all.** A person with no heading over her is not a person anybody can ask whether they were told.

And a hand is a person in a column just as much as a name is, and that is the whole of what she has got and it is not nothing. **A hand is findable, and it is findable without anybody publishing anything or serving anybody or saying a name out loud: a person with a copy of a book and about four years can put about four hundred hands in a book side by side and find the one that is hers.** That is not a secret and it is not a hiding place and it is not a thing anybody would think to look for.

And a dyer of twenty who has spent three years refusing to write a column like that has been refusing, for about a hundred and forty years of other people's ordinary afternoons, and she has never once known the other one existed, and she was not going to be told and she has not been told.

---

She got on the same cart at about the eighth hour and she did not go and find her brother and she is not going to.

There were four things she could have said to him and she went through all four of them on a cart road about four hundred and thirty miles long and she did not say any of them, and the reason she did not say them is not the four words he wrote in a margin in a book she has not read. The reason is that she has got a piece of paper, and a piece of paper is not a person, and **she is not going to be rude to her brother about a piece of paper**, and there is not a person in this empire she is angrier with and she has been turning the whole day over since the fifth hour looking for one and there is not one, and that is worse.

She got back to the counting room about eleven feet by fourteen off a dyers' lane in Greyfen, with a broken light in the window and a stove she does not light until the ninth month, and about forty books on shelves made for taller books, and she put her coat on the back of the door because that is what she does.

Then she did the only thing she is any good at, which is a count.

She took out a clean sheet and a pencil and she counted what every person in the eleven houses and the four other houses is owed and by whom, and the count is a page long and it is right, and it is the same work she has done every week since she was seventeen for nothing, and she did it on the night of the day she found out that her mother wrote nine lines in a room and did not read them back to her.

Nobody thanked her. She is not thanked in her own trade either and there has never been any expectation of it and she would not know what to do if there were.

And the copy was still inside her coat when she put the coat on the back of the door, and she did not take it out again, and she has not read the nine lines since, and there is a column in a book about four hundred and thirty miles away that has a woman of sixty-three in it under no heading at all, and the person who printed the fourth column knows that, and has not printed the third one, and has not said why, and is not going to be thanked for it.
