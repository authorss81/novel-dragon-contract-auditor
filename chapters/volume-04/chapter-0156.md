# Chapter 156: Four Hundred and Thirty Miles

They came down the road in the last week of the fifth month of next year and it took six days, and it was the second time they had been on it in as many months, and on the towpath there is nothing that makes it shorter, and people in three districts have offered to make it shorter and none of them can.

The old river road is about four hundred and thirty miles from the counter where the application was written to the bank where the application is about, and it is a towpath and a cart road and a bench in somebody else's load, and it is the same road in both directions, and it is not a metaphor and it is not a figure.

They went down it in the rain for two days and the third day was the day the arithmetic happened, on a bench at a lock, at about the fourth hour, in the dry, with the water up under the coping and a carrier eating something out of a paper.

"A day of my wage," said Marek Kest, "is a little over two shillings and fivepence."

"I have not the least idea what that is," said Tamsin Rook.

"**It is a week of seventeen shillings and a penny divided into seven.** It is not divided into working days, because nobody has ever divided it into working days, and the water office divides it into working days and the working days are forty-five pence, and forty-five pence and two shillings and fivepence do not agree with one another and I have known that for fourteen months and I have never been able to make them agree, and I have stopped trying, and I have not told anybody that I have stopped trying."

"Six days."

"Six days is a little over fourteen shillings. A week is seventeen and a penny and a day is a little over two and five, so six days leaves about two shillings and fivepence, which is one whole day, and a week is a week and a day is a day and there is no arrangement in this matter by which a day counts for a week." He did it twice on the back of a sheet and got the same figure both times and did not write it down. "Three journeys is twenty-one days, and a fourth will be about twenty-four, and twenty-four days is about three pounds. And there is no fund, and there is no form for a fund, and I have looked, and the looking took about four minutes and the answer was no in the way that most of the answers in this matter are no: not a refusal, just a place where the line would go."

"Three pounds of what."

"Of seventeen shillings and a penny the week. A year of that is about forty-four pounds. **Three pounds out of about forty-four pounds a year, and three pounds is very nearly what nineteen days on a bank cost me in the second month of last year, which is three pounds eleven and threepence, and it is in a weigh-house ledger a hundred and forty miles from here under the word *held*, and a clerk of fifty-one found that word in a box with *given* and *paid* and *advanced* in it and chose it, and I have never met her and I think about it about once a fortnight.**"

"And I am one of the four journeys."

"You are one of the four journeys and nobody pays your carriage either, and that is not a favour to anybody, it is that there is no office in this matter that pays the carriage of a person, and the only office that has ever come near it offered to pay carriage and not attendance and it has been inoperable for about sixteen months." He put the sheet away. "The girl on that bank is paid forty-five pence a day and she has a copy of an engagement in a satchel with a date on it of the first of the fourth month of next year and a term on it and the same notice as everybody else. The two of us walking this road are paid nothing at all. The difference between her and us is a sheet of paper with a date on it and a copy, and that is the whole of the difference, and I have been walking four hundred and thirty miles in both directions for fourteen months and I did not have one."

Tamsin Rook said, "You have an instrument with your name at the foot of it."

"I have three, and all three are on the backs of sheets, and one of them is a duty on a bank, and none of them is a wage, and none of them pays anybody for anything." He got up off the bench. "Go on. Your turn. You have a copyist's wage and it is on no page anybody in this matter has, and it is not going to be in a form either, and I have never once asked you what it is, and I am not going to start, and I would like it noticed that I have wanted to ask since the second month of last year."

---

The carrier at the lock was a man of about fifty and he had a boat and four boxes and a hand that had been broken twice in the same place. Marek Kest paid him ninepence to take a heavy paper up the road to a court on a flood terrace four hundred and thirty miles from a notary's table, and it was a copy of a line, and the line was in a clerk's own hand and had cost fourpence and had been paid for out of a column on the back of a counter that is shown to nobody.

He did not put the ninepence in the book with no heading.

He did not put it anywhere. The book with no heading at the top of it in that building is for work, and a man who has been fourteen months making about nine hundred and forty names findable has not got one line in it about what it costs him, and that is correct, and about four years ago he worked out that it is correct and has not been able to stop noticing.

The carrier took the money and looked at the paper and said, "That is a column."

"That is a column," said Marek Kest.

"I have carried hundreds of them in nineteen years," said the carrier, "and I have never once been asked what is in one, and I am not asking now, and I will say that I have read the heading and that is as far as I have got, and I would rather be a man who has read the heading than a man who has had a clerk explain it to him on a towpath, and if that is a thing you want to pay ninepence for you have picked the wrong carrier."

He put it in the inside of his coat and went.

---

**The line was a row of the register of runs.** A number of four figures and no letters. A device, described by a mark that is a mark and is not a name. **A column headed *by whose authority*, and a name in it, and no day, because a clerk in a city had refused to read a day out loud in a public room and had said out loud why.**

Tamsin Rook read the name in about four seconds.

She did not say anything for about nine minutes after that, and the nine minutes were not nothing to look at, and Marek Kest did not prompt her and did not leave the room.

---

They came down onto the Slade on the second day of the first week of the sixth month of next year, and it took the rest of that day, and there is about four hundred yards between the ford and the tool shed and nothing anywhere in this matter fixes the hired stone store at any distance from the ford at all, and nobody who wrote any of this down ever went and found out.

The north bay of a hired stone store holds about thirty and there were about thirty in it.

A girl of seventeen stood on the platform at the Reed Cut gate at about the seventh hour with a sheet in her hand and read a list of works out in plain words twice, the second reading slower, and there is a pail on that platform every evening and there has been for years, and a girl of eleven was on it with the pail.

Nobody gave her a reason. Nobody has ever given her a reason. **She asked four questions and three of them were about the work and were answered, and the fourth one was not a question anybody asked her and it was not answered either, it was stood with, and a person of seventeen stood next to a girl of eleven on a cold platform for about eleven minutes and did not tell her why she could not read, and did not tell her she was owed anything, and did not use her for anything, and was not thanked by her and did not ask to be.**

That is the whole of what happened to that girl and it is not a symbol and it is not in a book and it has no name and it is owed nothing at all.

---

A book with no heading at the top of it is on a bench in that bay and it has about nine hundred and forty names in it in nine hands over about sixty years and about four hundred of them are dead, and it is the fifth hand in about sixty years that is in it now and the page is a list of works with a name at the foot of it and a day against that name.

The man who is a clerk of the Inheritance Desk and holds no certification went to the bench at about the eighth hour and did not sit down and put his hand flat on the open page and said, "Nine hands."

"Nine hands," said Rennick Adley.

"About sixty years."

"About sixty years, and no heading, and no list of persons in it, and about four hundred of them dead, and the count of about ninety-one households and about sixty children under sixteen is on the back of a hand by a person who cannot read a paragraph." He turned back nine leaves and stopped. "And I have been told to look at a hand. And I have been at that counter for a fortnight and I have not got to it, and I am not going to get to it tonight, and I have looked at nine hands and one of them is going to be the one and I do not yet know which."

"You have not looked at nine hands," said Tamsin Rook. "You have looked at the tops of nine hands, which is a different thing, and I have been looking at hands for four years for a copyist's wage, and the tops are the same and the middles are not."

---

At about the ninth hour she was still at the bench, and about four people in that bay were still there, and the rain had come back.

"You have not asked me the thing," she said.

"I was not going to ask you the thing at a counter in a city," said Marek Kest, "and I am not going to ask you the thing at a bench in a bay, and if you want to say it you have about nine hundred and forty names in front of you and a bay with about thirty people in it and both of those are better than a counter and both of those are worse than a room with nobody in it."

"There is no room with nobody in it four hundred yards from this bay."

"No," said Tamsin Rook. "**There is not. And that is the whole of what this bay is, and I have been in four of them this year on account of a piece of paper, and I have not been in one room in fourteen months that nobody had a right to be in, and I did not notice that until this month and I have not worked out what to do about it and I am not going to say it again.**"

She turned back the leaves.

"And I am not going to ask you the thing either," she said. "Because you are a party now, and a party cannot speak for a body, and there is no person here who is not a party, and about four of the people in this bay said no in a doorway and are not on anything, and there is nobody in ninety-one households that a person can stand up and say a thing about a document to.

"That is not a fault in the bay. **That is a party being a party. And it took me three weeks to work out that the reason I could stand up in Auremar and put my name in a box and say what I am for is that I had never been one, and the reason I cannot do it here is that I am now one, and I did the thing and I lost the thing, and both halves of that are the price and I said the price out loud at a counter and nobody thanked me and I did not want to be thanked.**

"So I am going to do the other thing, and it is the thing I am actually for, and it takes about nine minutes, and it is a fact and not advice and you may check every word of it against that column of yours."
