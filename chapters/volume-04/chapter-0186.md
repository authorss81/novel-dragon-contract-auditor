# Chapter 186: Not a Substitute, and a Wage Stopped

A man with a paper came to the counting room off a dyers' lane in Greyfen in the third week of the third month of the year after, having come about something else first and said so, and he asked her three questions in about nine minutes, and every one of the three was fair, and the answer to the first two was yes.

The counting room is about eleven feet by fourteen. It has a bench, a window with a broken light, a stove she does not light until the ninth month, and about forty books on shelves that were made for taller books. It is not hers. The bench is not hers. The light is broken and has been since before she came into it.

---

The first question was whether she is of the claimant's family.

"Yes," said Nell Kest.

The second was whether she is of age.

"Yes," said Nell Kest. "I am twenty. I have been twenty since the ninth of the sixth and I can tell you the day if it is any use and it is not, because I have never once been asked a question in a room by a man with a paper and had it be a thing I could be wrong about."

The third was whether she is resident in the district.

And it is four lines long, and it is in a second hand, and she had it in front of her on the counter because he had put it down and let her read it, which was more than the last person to ask her anything about a column had done.

> *Resident in the district: having a place of custom or of work entered in the roll of households of the district. A roll of households lists the households in a district and does not list the rights, holdings or claims of any person in it, and nothing in a roll of households is a consent, and a person may be entered in a roll of households without being asked and without knowing, and may be put down in it as having a place of custom or of work by any person keeping the roll.*

---

She read that four times, and the fourth time was the one that took, and she read it a fifth time anyway because the man had let her and because it is free.

"I am not going to answer the third one," she said.

The man said, "I have to write something in the box."

"**Then write that I did not answer it, and write the day, and write that I said it out loud in a room with you in it, and do not write anything else and do not write why, because there is no box for a why and I have looked.** I am refusing to be served. That is the instrument. That is the only one there is in this empire and I have used it once and I am not a lawyer and I am not proud of it and I am using it now."

"I was told there was no need to ask."

"There is no need to ask," said Nell Kest. "**That is what the four lines in front of me say in the third one from the end. Nothing in a roll of households is a consent, and a person may be put down in it as having a place of custom or of work without being asked.** I have been in this room for three years and I have put about nine people I work beside into a book in my own hand, and I asked every one of them, and I wrote down that I had asked, and I put the day against it, and I did it because I wanted to be able to say in four years that I asked. That is my whole method and it takes an afternoon a week and it is the reason I sleep."

---

"And I want to say the other half of it out loud, because you have been decent enough to stand in my counting room and read me a thing, and because you will be asked what she said, and you will not be able to say."

"All right."

"**I am not a substitute for a thing you have not told me about.**"

She put the four lines down flat.

"That is the whole sentence and I have been working on it since the eighth hour and I have got it down to as few words as it will go and I have counted them, and I would like you to write them down, and I would like you to write the day against it as well, and I am not going to explain it to you and if you ask me to explain it I will not."

He wrote it down. He wrote the day against it. He did not ask her to explain it.

---

The rest of it took about four minutes and she gave it in the flat way she gives a count.

"I am twenty and I am a dye-house worker, which is what I am paid for, and I keep the books of the eleven houses on this lane and of four other houses in three other districts, which is what I am not paid for and have done since I was seventeen. I have one honest sentence about what the work has cost this family and I gave it to an assessor of about sixty-eight in a lane six hours from here and she wrote it down, and it is: *we got the levy off the mill and the shop lost its credit in the same month and both of those are true and one of them is why there is one job in this town and it is not mine.*

"And I am not going to be the second thing in this family that somebody decides is easier than telling her. **The first thing was a boy who was not asked anything in a room and the room was for something else.** He has been not asked for about four months. I have a letter of his that I have not answered since the sixteenth of the eighth of last year and it is mine to leave unanswered and it is still mine to leave unanswered this afternoon. And nobody in this empire is going to hand me a form to fill in as a way of not having the conversation, and if you have been sent here to do that then you have been sent to the wrong person and I would rather you knew it now than in four years."

---

The man did not answer two questions and she noticed both times and let them go, and she wants it written down that she let them go, because letting a thing go is a thing a person does and not doing it is also a thing a person does.

She asked him whether the hold has a name on it.

He said, "I do not have the hold."

She asked him whether the hold has a day on it.

"I have got a day at the top of the sheet," he said, "for when it is to be served, and not one anywhere else on it."

"Right," said Nell Kest. "**Then nobody in this room can tell me whether there is a name on the thing that is holding my wage, and the only person who could tell me is the person who is not allowed to, and that is not an accident either. It is the fourth line again.**"

---

And then the wage stopped, and it stopped in about nine days, and this is the whole of what that was.

The hold is in the schedule, in the second hand, and it is a form with a heading over it and no person under it, and the person who fills it in fills in the name of a thing.

**The thing is a household.**

The household is one room over a shop and there is one person in it and she is it. There is no name anywhere on the hold. There is a heading and a description and a day at the top of a sheet for when it is to be served, and the description is a household, and a household of one is a person, and there is no column on anything in this empire that says which person a household is.

Her wage at the dye-house stopped in the second week of the third month. Not reduced. Stopped. There is no form for a reduction and there is no form for a stop either; the word that is used is *held*, and it is the same word the district uses about a wall and the same word a book-keeper uses about a spring, and it means a person has been made to wait.

---

The factor came on the fourth day and asked her for the figure, and she is a person who has been putting figures in books for three years and has never once put one of her own in.

"No," said Nell Kest.

"I have to put something in the box."

"Put the word *held* in it." She put the pencil down. "**A wage is the whole of what there is. A figure in a document is a figure anybody can be shown, and in about four years there is going to be somebody with a copy of it and a reason and about nine hours, and they will not be asking whether I can live on it. They will be asking whether I could.** I have written down what nine people in this lane are owed about nine hundred times and I have never once written down what I am owed, and that is not modesty. It is that a number about a person is the easiest thing in this empire to take away and I am not handing it over for nothing and I am not handing it over for anything."

The factor wrote *held* in the box. He did not ask a second question and he was not thanked and there is no form anywhere that requires a person to be thanked.

---

The woman at the eleventh door came in on the fifth day, and she has cut plates out of a bench under a window on that lane for twenty-two years and she cannot read a paragraph and she is the person on this lane who says the true thing first.

"They have stopped your money."

"They have."

"And the levy." She had her hands in the sleeves of her apron and she was not looking at the bench. "The fourth column. The one you will not write names in. Is that what this is."

"I do not know," said Nell Kest, "and I want to be exact with you, because you have come down here on your own time and I am not going to give you a comfortable answer. **The form says a household. A household is a house, and this is one room over a shop, and there is one person in it. I do not know what it does when a household has four people in it and two of them are in a dye-house.**"

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only one I have and I have had it for nine days." She turned the four lines round on the counter so that the woman could see that there was writing on them, which is the thing Nell Kest does instead of saying a thing twice. "Look at the fourth line. *A person may be put down in it as having a place of custom or of work by any person keeping the roll.* Any person. It does not say a person may be asked first. It does not say anything at all about asking. And it does not say how many of us a household is allowed."

The woman of about sixty read it for about as long as it took her and then said, "Then I will tell you what I want and you will tell me if you can."

"Tell me."

"I want to be asked. Not before, not after, in a room, at the door, in words, out loud, where a person has to stand there while I say it. **I have been on this lane forty years and nobody has ever asked me one single thing and I have got very good at it and I am telling you that I would like to be asked.**"

Nell Kest looked at her for longer than she had meant to.

"I will not be able to do that for you," she said. "I have said it to one assessor in a lane six hours from here and she said it back to me and it is the truest thing anybody in this trade has said to me and it is four hours on a cart and it came to nothing, and what came of it is that I have a sentence in a book that says a person has to be able to say no in a room and not only in a book, and I have been carrying that sentence around this lane for about four months and I have not had one chance to use it. **I am going to use it today and I am going to use it on a form, and I am not going to pretend to you that it is the same as using it in your doorway, because it is not and it is much smaller.**"

The woman of about sixty said, "Smaller is not nothing," and went home, and she was not thanked, and there is no form anywhere that requires it.

---

And here is the thing she worked out on the bench that night, on her own, and it took her about nine minutes, and it is the finding and she did not expect it and it is not about the levy.

The first time they came for her they came with a debt.

That was in the ninth month of the year before last and it was a proper instrument with a seal on it and a word in it that somebody else had written, and a name on it that was not hers in the box where her name belonged, and a clause at the end that anybody could see and anybody could get out on, and a guild's challenge in the ordinary way, and a person came to the dye-house and read it out in a room with about nine people in it.

**The whole of that was arguable. Every word in it could be argued with, and I argued with it, and I was right about the exit clause, and I lost about the rest of it and I was not sorry about losing.**

And this time they have come with a description. A description does not have to be argued with. There is no instrument with my name on it that anybody can strike a word out of, and there is no seal, and there is no substituted signer, and there is no clause anybody can see.

**There is a heading over a form and the form says a household and the household is me, and there is nothing on it to be angry at, and that is why it is harder.**

---

She got up and took the fourth book off the shelf and put it on the bench and opened it at the fourth column, where it has been open more or less since the eleventh of the eighth of the year before last.

The six-year-old refusal is at the top of it, in an old hand, with no name in it, and under it somebody had written *Noted*, in an unknown hand, in a different ink, and that word is the last thing that has happened to that column in six years.

Under that is her own two days and her own name at the foot of her own line.

And under that, on the third day of the third month of the year after, a woman of twenty wrote a fourth thing, and it is four lines long, and it is the second refusal in that column that carries no name, and the first of the two is six years old and is not hers, and this is the first time anybody has ever asked her to put one there.

> *Asked of me, in this room, in the third week of the third month, whether I am of the claimant's family. I am. Asked whether I am of age. I am. Asked whether I am resident in the district, which is a roll of households and not a consent and does not list what anybody owns.*
>
> *I answered the first two out loud, because they are facts and facts are free. I did not answer the third, out loud, in a room, with the day on it, and my name is not in this column, and the reason is in the line below and the reason is that the whole of my work for three years has been to keep names out of a column that turns a person into a record, and I am not going to be the first person in this book who put one in and called it a duty.*
>
> *There is no why on the form they had and there is no why in this book and I am not going to supply one to either. There are four words and they are: a person, not a column. I have got to them myself and I have not seen my brother write them down anywhere, and if they are the same four words then he has written them somewhere, and I want that written down, and I want it written down here, in my own hand, by me, in a book that is not mine, in a column with no heading over it. That is the whole of it, and I am not going to be thanked, and there is no name on this line because there is no name in the column.*

She read it back once, which is what a person does with a thing they have written, and she did not read it back twice, and the reason is that there is nobody in the room and she is not doing it for anybody.

She put the book back on the shelf, in about forty books on shelves made for taller books, in a room about eleven feet by fourteen off a dyers' lane, and the stove was cold and she did not light it, and her wage is held, and there is a heading over the form that is holding it and nothing at all under the heading except a household with one person in it.
