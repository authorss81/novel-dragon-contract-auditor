# Chapter 168: What a Clerk Is In Charge Of

The Mosswake Roll Court is a low building on a flood terrace with a court room on the ground floor, a clerk's window at the back of it with a sill about four feet off the ground and about two feet wide, a board above the sill with a number of names on it in chalk, and an embankment corridor behind that with four presses in it.

Ivet Sarn has worked at the window for nine years.

She is thirty-eight. She refused a seventh column in a form with a reason and no why, and that entry is still in the minute book and it is the only thing in nine years she has entered in a book with a name on it that anybody has ever argued about. And there is a line in a sixth space at the bottom of one of those pages with a heading over it that is not the heading anybody expected, and the heading is the name of a person, and the person is her.

**Nobody had ever asked her what the presses were for.** She had been in charge of four of them for nine years.

---

At about the fourth hour a man of about fifty came up to the sill and asked for his name to be taken off the board.

She had a rag in her hand because the board is wiped every morning and she does it herself and there is no one else in the building who is allowed to.

"No," said Ivet Sarn.

"I have paid."

"You have paid and I am not saying you have not. "She did not put the rag down. "**That is a notice.** A notice is a thing a person can be shown, and a name that is not on a notice is a name that cannot be shown to anybody, and I have been at this sill nine years and I have watched about nine people ask me to make a name unfindable and not one of them had any idea what they were asking for, and every one of them asked because somebody had been unkind to them and not one of them asked because they had done anything."

"It is my name."

"It is your name and it is on a board and the board is nailed up there where the water can get at it, and it has been nailed up there since before I came, and it is the only thing in this district that a person can stand in front of and point at. "She wiped a part of it that had nothing on it. "If I take it off at the fourth hour on a day you have paid, then in about four years there is no board and no name and no day, and if you come back in about four years and ask me whether you were ever here, I will be able to tell you about the money. **That is all I will be able to tell you about.** You will have bought the only proof there was."

He stood at the sill for a while and then he said something that she wrote down afterwards in her own book with the day on it, and it is the reason she says the sentence about nine people in a corridor without any heat in it.

He said: "I did not think of it like that."

"No," said Ivet Sarn. "**Nobody thinks of it like that.** That is the entire difficulty and it has taken me nine years to be able to say it in one sentence and I am not going to say it more than once a day."

---

She had the key on her hip on a string, and the string had been shortened twice, and it had been shortened by herself, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that makes a person be in charge of a thing, and there never has been, and the way she came by it is that she was the only one left holding it.

The first press is a file of the sixth year. She has not opened it in this matter and she is not going to.

The second is a bound set of district sheets.

The third is a press of returned notices with a spike through them.

The fourth is a press in a wall with a shelf in it and a bound schedule in it, and the schedule has been in that press longer than anybody in that building has been alive, and nothing in that building's records says so.

"You have been in charge of a bound schedule for nine years," said Marek Kest.

"I have been the only one with a key for nine years and those are not the same thing and I want the first one off the table before anybody builds anything on it. "She was at the window and she had not come out from behind the sill. "**A person in charge of a thing is a person nobody appointed.** There is no form. I did not apply. Nobody sent me. I have a key on a string and that is the entire instrument, and if I had a form I would show it to you and there is nothing to show."

---

"You are not a party," said Tamsin Rook.

"I am not a party."

"You have a name in a heading in a minute book."

"That is a heading over a line in a page and I wrote it and it says what a person is and not what a person has done, and it is the only heading in nine years that anybody has argued about and they argued about it for about nine minutes and then somebody above me said it was a heading and not a column and that was the end of it. "She came out from behind the sill for the first time. "**A party is a person a book can be true about.** I am a person a book can be true about, and I get served, and I am asked questions, and I am not a party to anything, and I hold no box and no sheet and no fee and no term and no day at the end of it."

"You hold a wage."

"I hold a wage from a district and the wage is a line in somebody else's book and if the district stops the wage the line stops and the four presses stay. "She turned the key over. "**That is the whole of what I am.** I am the only position in this matter that is not a party, and I want that written down, and I am aware of how it sounds at about the seventh hour on the first day of the first week of the tenth month of next year in a corridor behind a building."

"Why does it sound like that," said the man of thirty-eight.

"Because a woman of about sixty came in about four years ago and asked me to put her late and I said no, and she asked why, and I said because I have to be able to say who I said it to. "Ivet Sarn put her hand on the wall beside the fourth press. "**And being able to say who I said it to is the only power I have ever had and I have had it for nine years and I have never once used it, and I have never once been asked to.**"

---

Here is the thing she said in the corridor, and there were four people in it, and she said it once and she would not say it again.

"A recorded custody entry is not a thing a court grants and not a thing a form produces. It is a thing a person writes down. "She had the form in her hand, the one a man of sixty-one or two had walked about nine miles to a lock to give away, written out in his own hand off a memory of about sixty years. "**Four lines.** The thing. The person in charge of it. The day it was taken. The signature of the person who took it."

"And the second of those four," said Marek Kest, "is you."

"The second of those four is a person, and there is no line on that form for a body, and a body is a word with a seal on it that cannot be instructed and cannot stop. "She held the form up so the lamp caught it. "**And here is the whole of it and I have had nine years and four presses to work it out and I worked it out in about nine minutes this morning while I was waiting for the two of you: a custody entry is a statement that somebody was in the room, and the whole of what it requires is a person who can be instructed and who can stop.** That is why a door has to be opened by a person and not by a body. Not strength. Not law. A person."

Nobody in the corridor said anything for a moment, and a barge went under the terrace.

"And the other half of that," said Tamsin Rook, "is that a person can stop it."

"**A person can stop it.** And a thing that depends on a person can be taken away by the person stopping. And a duty that stops is not a duty that survives stopping, and a person cannot be compelled to be in a room, and there is no form in this empire for compelling a person to be in a room, and I have looked for one in about nine years and I have not found it and I am not going to keep looking. "She put the form down on the sill. "So you can have this corridor in a fortnight's time and you cannot have it in a week's, and I am telling you that not as a threat. **I am telling you it because a person who is going to be asked to stand in a room ought to be told how long she has, and nobody in this matter has told anybody how long they have.**"

---

The witness was the difficulty and it took about nine minutes to get one.

"Neither of you can be a witness," said Ivet Sarn.

"I can be a witness," said Marek Kest.

"**You are a party.** You are on the sheet with three ruled boxes and your name is in the first one and a woman of twenty-four filled the third one at about a quarter past the second hour in the last week of the seventh month and neither of you signed anything. "She said it without any edge at all. "A party is served. A witness is asked. A person cannot be both in the same room about the same thing, and that is not a rule anybody made, it is what the two words mean, and I have been asking people things at a window for nine years and I have never once had a person who was both."

"Then who."

"**Somebody who has come to the window about something else.**"

There was a woman at the sill when they came out, and she had been at the sill for about half an hour, and she had a paper in her hand with a seal on it that was not this court's.

She was not asked her name and she did not give it, and she was not thanked and she did not expect to be.

"Is it about my paper," she said.

"No."

"Then what is it about."

"**It is about whether a person can be shown to have been in a room.**" Ivet Sarn said it in the plainest words she had. "There is a press in this corridor with a thing in it that has been in that press for longer than this building has had a court in it, and the law about a thing like that is that a person has to be able to say it was taken out and by whom and on what day, and there is no form for that, and the reason there is no form for that is that a form cannot be in a room."

The woman looked at the paper in her hand for a while.

"Will it take long."

"About nine minutes."

"Then I'll stand here. "She did not go in and she did not go away, and she held her paper against her coat, and she was in the corridor for about forty minutes and she did not hear one word of what was said in the room except one sentence, which Ivet Sarn said out loud on purpose.

"You are a witness," she said. "**It does not mean you are to blame for anything.** It means that in about four years if anybody comes along with a paper and a question, there is one person in this building who can say they were standing in this corridor, and that is the whole of it, and there is no other reason anybody will ever need you for."

"About four years," said the woman.

"About four years is what everything in this matter is. Everything anybody has done in this matter was done so that it could be found in about four years, and nobody who did any of it thought that was the point."

---

Then she made the entry.

Four lines, in her own hand, with the day on it, at about the eighth hour on the second day of the first week of the tenth month of next year, and a press opened by a person and a person standing in a corridor while it was open.

> *Thing: a bound schedule, in one volume, the schedule of a term of an office.*
> *Person in charge of it: Ivet Sarn, clerk, this court, by a key on a string and not by any appointment, nine years.*
> *Day it was taken: not taken. Opened, and the person in charge of it standing in the corridor while it was open.*
> *Signature of the person who took it: none, nothing was taken.*

She wrote it and she did not read it out, and when the man of thirty-eight said nothing about that she told him why, and the reason was not the reason the woman of twenty-eight gave about a lintel in a bay.

"**A lintel was a thing I did to a building and I did not want to be asked about it.** This is a thing I am going to be asked about for the rest of my working life and I am not writing it twice. "She put the pen down. "I will read it out to a person in about four years, in a room, with a witness in it, if anybody asks me. Not tonight and not to a man I met four hours ago."

The woman at the sill went home at about the ninth hour with her paper and no thanks and a thing she could say that nobody had told her before, and she has not come back and she does not have to, and there is no form anywhere that says she was ever here.

**A key on a string, shortened twice by the person who holds it.** Four presses. A file of the sixth year, shut. A register of duties whose fourth column reads *next review: none named* on every line it has, and whose fifth heading is *Copy sold*, and a sixth space at the bottom of every page with nothing whatever over it. And a woman of thirty-eight who has been in charge of a thing for nine years by accident, and who is not a party, and who is the only person in this matter anybody can stop.
