# Chapter 0162: What It Costs to Get Here

Sefa Ild is twenty-eight and she has kept the north bay of a hired stone store off the Slade since the second month of next year, and the bay is about thirty-one foot by nineteen and holds about thirty, and she is the only person in ninety-one households who is paid for standing in a room.

On the first day of the second week of the eighth month of next year she worked out, on the back of a sheet, what it costs to get about forty-one people to a place on a day that nobody is paid to bring them to, and then she read it out in the bay, and then she was asked whether she would take the works on, and she said no, and the reason she gave was not the one anybody expected.

She is not a foreman. She has said so twice in that bay to people who were not asking and she has never once been asked, and being asked now, at about the fifth hour, by a woman of fifty-one standing in front of half a bay, was a different thing and she treated it as a different thing.

"Take what on," she said.

"The second season. "Halla Wray had the slate in her hand and was not looking at it. "The asking. The walking. The reading. All of it, for a season, with a day against it and a name at the foot."

"I do not want a foreman," said Sefa Ild, "and I have said so twice."

"You have."

"I do not want it now either. "She took the slate out of the foreman's hand, which nobody in that bay had ever seen anybody do, and she did it without heat and she put it back. "I am going to say why, and it is going to be about the arithmetic, and you are going to find it strange, and it is not strange, it is the only interesting thing anybody has asked me this month."

---

The arithmetic is this.

The Company's whole money is four shillings a year and all of it is the rent of the building. The sum that reaches her out of that is smaller than the four shillings and she has never once asked what it is and there is no line in any book in that building with her name against it except the one she has written herself in the bay's own ledger.

"So the money for a review," she said, "is nothing, and the money for a season is nothing, and I have been standing in this room for about four months for the smaller half of nothing."

"Forty-five pence a day," said Tavi Venn. "For the reading. Four days a week."

"And the walking is not the reading. "Sefa Ild put her hand flat on the bench in the middle. "**A review a person has to walk to is a review of the people who can walk.** That is the first line of the whole of this and it took me seconds and I have been wrong about it for four months."

Nobody said anything.

"You have all been thinking about the bay. Thirty-odd of you fit in here and about nine of you cannot read a paragraph and the other half of you have walked to this room before and you think the question is the bay. "She looked round it. "**The question is not the bay.** The question is that about nine of the about forty-one who said yes with a day against them did not turn up on the day, and nobody knows which nine, and there is no column for it and nobody is paid to find out, and the bay was full and the nine were not in it, and both of those are true at the same time and only one of them is a thing anybody can see."

Rennick Adley had the ledger open on his knee by then, on the bench in the middle, the third column with no heading over it.

"Can I put that in here," he said.

"You can put anything in the third column. That is what the third column is for. It has had no heading over it since the sixth year and every one of the four hundred and something people in this book with no heading has gone into it, and about nine hundred and forty names are in that book and about four hundred of them are dead."

"That is not what I meant."

"I know what you meant. "She put her thumb on the page. "**Write it in the third column.** Nobody will look it up in nine years under anything in particular, and somebody will want to know why a bay was full and nine people were missing, and there will be one page in this district that says so, and it will say so with no heading over it, and a person will have to read about nine hundred and forty names to find the day."

He wrote it. She did not read it out, and he did not read it out, and that is the whole of the arrangement between the two of them and it has been the arrangement since the second month.

---

"Put the slate down for a minute," said Tavi Venn.

It went down on the bench. She got the satchel up off her shoulder and took a leaf out of the back of the book in it, which is where she keeps what she cannot read to whom, and she put it on the bench next to the chalk and turned it so the room could see the writing was upside down to them.

"That is a day against nine lines in my own hand," she said. "It is the only record anybody on this bank has made in four months that is a record, and I made it, and I am seventeen, and I am paid forty-five pence a day to read things out loud and I am not paid for the writing, and if I stop then it goes in a satchel into a house and it stays there."

"Or it does not," said Doreth Ferrow.

"**Or it does not.** That is the whole of what a person is as a record and it took me about four minutes and I have not said it out loud before and I am saying it now because there are two objects on this bench and both of them are rubbish and they are rubbish in opposite ways."

She put her finger on the slate without touching it.

"That is a record a person can destroy before anybody has finished looking at it and nobody is going to be able to say afterwards that it was not there. **This is a record that a person can carry away in a satchel, and the person can be seventeen, and the person can be told to stop, and being told to stop is the ordinary thing that happens to a reader and not a special thing that happens to somebody who keeps a record.**"

"And the third column," said Sefa Ild.

"And the third column is in a book in a hired store with no office on it that a company owns, and the company has four shillings a year and all of it is the rent, and in nine years the company may not be there. "Tavi Venn shut the satchel. "**So the slate can be washed and the leaf can go in a house and the book can lose its building, and there is not one of those three things anybody on this bank can be paid to prevent, and every one of them is a person, and the price you read out a minute ago is the price of that.**"

Nobody in that bay had put those three things next to each other before.

---

Here is the rest of the price, and she read it out, and she read it out in the order it is paid and not in the order it is convenient.

"One. **Four shillings a year, all of it the rent of this building, and the smaller half of it for standing in a room.** That is the money. There is no other money and there is no fund and there is no form for a fund.

"Two. A reader's wage for two days, which is forty-five pence a day, and the walking is not that wage and nobody is going to pay it and nobody has offered.

"Three. **About nine people who cannot read a paragraph.** A day a person comes to is a day they have to be told what the day is, and the only person on this bank who can tell them is a girl of seventeen and she is paid by the day and not by the walk, and if she stops then those same people do not know that a review happened, and a review that nobody knows happened is a thing that did not happen, and there is no form anywhere that says a thing did not happen.

"Four. **About four people who cannot read a paragraph were never asked in the first place.**"

The bay did not move.

"I am not going to name them," said Sefa Ild. "I want that written down with whatever this is. **I am not going to name them because naming them is asking them, and they have not been asked once, and if I put their names in a list to explain a cost to a room then they have been asked something by a woman writing a price list, and that is the thing this whole matter is supposed to be a different shape from.**"

"Then how do you count them," said Doreth Ferrow, from the bench against the north wall.

"You do not. You write four and you write *not asked* against the four, and people know it, and that is a number of people and not a list."

"Write it," said Doreth Ferrow.

"It is written."

---

"Five," said Sefa Ild.

"Five is the cart," said Tavi Venn.

"Five is the cart and five is why there is no cart. "The woman who keeps that bay went and stood in the frame of the west door, which shuts now, and put her shoulder against the jamb. "There is one cart on this bank and it belongs to a man of thirty-one who is on the list first, and on the day of the review he has to come to this bay as a person who is on a list and not as the man who brought the cart, and if he brings the cart then he is working on the day of the review and he is not attending it, and if he does not bring the cart then about eleven people from up the bank do not come in."

"Nobody thought of that," said Halla Wray.

"**Nobody thought of it because a man with a cart is a man with a cart and a man on a list is a man on a list, and this is the whole of what this matter is about and I have been standing in this room for four months.**"

"Six," she said, from the door. "That bench."

Doreth Ferrow said, "That bench has been here since before the war."

"That bench came with the building and it is against the north wall and it is about two feet higher than the bench in the middle and a woman of sixty-four cannot get onto it, and two of us got her onto it on the day and it took the two of us a while, and on the day of the review there were nine of us in this room and we need two of us for the bench, and that is two of the nine not counting, and on the day after the review there is no review and the bench is a bench."

She stopped in the door and said the last of it with her back to the room, which is a thing she does and which the chair of sixty-four noticed and has not said anything about.

"**That is the whole of the price.** About four shillings and ninety minutes a day and one cart that cannot come and one bench that will not take a person, and four people nobody asked and about nine who cannot read and about nine who did not come. And not one of those nine lines is a line anybody can be given money for, and the two lines that could be — the reader's wage and the rent — are the two lines we have."

---

They were quiet for a while.

"Sefa," said Halla Wray. "I am asking you again and I am going to be told no and I want it on the record that I asked and not that I put you in a corner of a room to do it."

"You have."

"Then say no to the work and not to me, because there is a difference and you know there is a difference."

"I said no to the work. "Sefa Ild came back off the door frame. "**I want to be exact, because I have been inexact about one thing in this bay for four months and I am not going to be inexact about it twice.** I do not want to be a foreman. That has never changed and it is not what I am refusing this afternoon. What I am refusing this afternoon is the arithmetic, and I have put the arithmetic in the room and the room can have it or not have it, and I am not going to be the one who holds it, because the moment I hold it somebody will write a name at the foot of it and there will be a day against that name and I will be the person who is in charge of a season nobody agreed to."

"Would that be bad."

"**It would be the same bad thing as a door that does not shut, and it would be bad in the same way, and I worked it out with a wedge in a morning and I am not going to work it out again with a list.**"

Somebody started to. She put her hand up, the way the chair of sixty-four does it, and it stopped.

"Do not," she said. "**I am not doing it for the room.** I did it because the door was making a noise and I am doing this because somebody has to be able to say the price out loud in a room where about nine people cannot read a paragraph, and that is not the same job and I am not going to be thanked for it and if I am thanked for it I will have been bought."

And then, because the foreman was still standing there with the slate in her hand:

"Write it in the third column," said Sefa Ild. "**All of it, the four and the nine and the nine and the cart and the bench, and the day, and put my name at the bottom of it, because somebody is going to want to know who worked this out and I would rather it was me than a person who copied it.**"

Rennick Adley wrote it in the third column, with the day on it, and Sefa Ild's name at the foot of it, and did not read it out, and nobody read it out, and that is the ninth line in a column with no heading over it in a book in a hired stone store off the Slade.

The slate went back on the nail over the bench in the middle. It is not a record and it is not a book and in about four years it will be washed, and a woman of about thirty-one at the first house is on it as a description, at her own insistence, twice, and she has not been asked for a name again and is not going to be.
