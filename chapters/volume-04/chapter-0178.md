# Chapter 178: A Column Against Its Form

Marn Ottery came back up the old river road in six days in the first week of the first month of the year after and walked every one of them, and she got to the fourth counter of the Notaries' Table in Auremar on the sixth day at about the fourth hour, and the counter was open, and a girl of about twenty-five was at it, and the girl said the counter had been shut for four days and that nobody had told her anything and that she had been at it every day anyway.

That is the whole of what she got for it, and she has said the price out loud twice, in a lane and on a towpath, and the second time she said it to a man of thirty-eight who had walked the same road five times and who told her that a day of that wage is a little over two shillings and fivepence, and she said, "Then I have spent more than you have and I have never once been in a cart, and I would like that entered somewhere."

It is not entered anywhere.

A copyist's trade is not writing a thing out again. It is putting a thing that is in front of her onto a thing that is not, and being exact about it, and knowing which of the two she has done.

---

She went and found the register at about the eighth hour on the sixth day and it took her about nine minutes and she has told both of the two other people in the building the size of the looking.

It is on a shelf in the third gallery, which is not a gallery any more and has a table in it and a stool, and it is a witness register. It has five columns.

The first is a name. The second is a day. The third is the second person. The fourth is what was said. **And the fifth has a heading over it, and the fifth is full, and in every line of the fifth, from the first page to the last, the same thing is written, and it is the name of this office.**

About four feet away, on the same table, under a stone that keeps the damp out, is the form it is kept from. It is a printed sheet and it has five lines on it and the fifth line is this:

> *The second person asks the person, before the thing is said, whether she is a party to it, and the answer is written here.*

"I have looked at that form about nine hundred times in eleven years," Marn Ottery said. "I have never once looked at the register against it. I did not know it was on the same table. **I am a copyist. Comparing a column against its form is the whole of what I am**, and I have been doing it in this building for eleven years about nine hundred times a year for other people's forms. This one is mine and it is four feet away and I have never turned my head."

---

Aurelia Pell put that method into the world for nothing in a back room on a flood terrace in the third week of the ninth month of next year, and she is not in Auremar and is not in this building and does not know that any of this has happened and is not a friend of anybody in this matter and has not been thanked, because nobody in this building knows her name.

Tamsin Rook knew it. Tamsin Rook was at the counter in the third gallery with a roll under her arm at about the ninth hour and she had been told about the fifth line at about the eighth hour and she had been told it in a lane about four miles off a road, and she said one sentence and it was the only sentence she said about it, and she said it the way she says things that are facts.

"**That is the same method as the archivist's and it is running the other way.** A person can be put into a column by hand, and a person can be left out of one by hand, a hundred and forty years over, in about nine hundred buildings, and nobody has to want anything at all."

"That is two findings," said Marn Ottery. "The first is the one in the lane. The second one is new and it is yours and you have given it to me in a gallery and I am going to write it down and I am not going to tell you whose hand it is in, and you will find that out in about four years with a pen and a table and about nine days of your own afternoon, and that is the correct way round and I am not going to be thanked for it either."

"I am not going to be thanked," said Tamsin Rook. "My third line forbids it and my fourth line says a fact is not advice, and neither of those has anything to do with this and I have said them about nine times in four months and I would like somebody to notice that I have got a use for them and it is not this."

---

Then she went and looked for her own name.

She did not decide to. She was standing in a gallery with a register open and a hundred and forty years of the same shape in her head, and she said out loud, in the ordinary way, to nobody, that a person who has spent eleven years putting names into columns knows exactly which column she is in and exactly which hand is going to be in it, and that if she is going to say out loud that a fifth column is a copy of a heading and not an answer then the first person she has to say it to is herself.

It is a roll of persons who have been parties to instruments. It is a district roll and it is not the register of runs that is on the high shelf in this building and is not a copy of it, and the two are about four bays apart and nobody has ever compared them either.

There is a column headed for the person.

Her name is in it. It is in a hand that is not her hand and it is not a bad hand, and it is the hand of somebody who was doing an ordinary day's work on an ordinary day, and it is correct.

**It is the first name in that column that she did not write herself.**

---

"There are two names in the day-book at the fourth counter," she said. "There are two in a clerk's own book on the floor below me and they went in in the last week of the seventh month of next year on my own motion and the clerk in that case was a woman of twenty-four and neither of them signed anything. **And I have just found the third, and the third is me, and I did not put it there.** There is a form in this empire in which a person becomes a party without anybody asking them, and I have never once seen it filled in wrong, and that is the part I cannot get past."

"You did not put it there."

"No. Somebody did, in the ordinary way, and correctly, and about four days after I filled in the third of three ruled boxes on the back of a blank because a form came back with a reason in it and no box for a why and a person has to be in the box." She shut the roll. "**That is how it is done. Not by a villain and not by an order. By a clerk, on an ordinary day, filling in the next space**, because the next space is there."

---

And then the woman came to the counter, at about the tenth hour, with a paper.

She was not named and she was not thanked and her name was not taken, because a copy does not need the buyer's name on it and the fee board at that counter carries four things and none of them is time and none of them is a name.

There was a seal on the paper and the seal was not this building's.

Nobody read it out. Nobody asked whose it was. **A woman with a paper and a seal on it has been in this room before in another building about four hundred and thirty miles from here, and she was not asked her name then either.** The two women are not the same woman and the same fact is not a door.

She bought a copy of a record of property and debt for a house of the name Kest. Fourpence. In full.

---

Marn Ottery served her, because that is the counter and it is the counter's work and there is nothing in this empire that lets a clerk of thirty-four decide which of the records on the shelves behind her a person at a counter may not have a copy of.

And she said out loud, in the room, in the ordinary way, what she was doing, because the price of a copy is not the fourpence.

"**A copy of a thing is a fact about the copying.** I am going to hand you four pages with a thing written on them that has been copied about four times by about four people, and it will tell you what the record says, and it will not tell you one single thing about whether any of it can be enforced, and there is no part of that record that can be enforced against anybody at all, and both of those are true at the same time and they are the whole of what is on the page."

"I did not come for that," said the woman.

"Then I do not know what you came for," said Marn Ottery, "and I am not going to ask, and it is not my counter's business and I have been at it eleven years and I have never once been the person who is told what a copy is for, and I have said what I said because the alternative is handing four pages to a stranger in silence and I have handed about nine hundred of them in silence and I have never slept particularly well for it and I have slept perfectly well for the four hundred of them I did not."

The woman paid the fourpence and took the pages and put them inside her coat, and she said, "Thank you," and Marn Ottery said, "That is fourpence and not a thank you, and there is a difference and I am not being cold, I am being exact."

"**I know the difference. I have been in a building for years where everybody knows the difference and where nobody says it out loud.**"

And she went, and she was not at the counter again, and nobody knows who sent her.

---

Marek Kest was in the room the whole time.

He could not have stopped it and he could not have refused it, and the reason is not a difficulty in this matter and it is the ordinary reason: **fourpence is not his to withhold.** He bought a question at a shilling at that table in the second week of the fifth month of next year and it was his because he had paid for it. This was fourpence and the fourpence came out of a stranger's hand and the stranger had a right to it, and there is no form in this empire for a man to object to a person buying a copy of a record about his own family, and he looked at it for about four minutes and then he put his hands in his pockets and stood there.

"You could have said her name," said Marn Ottery.

"I could have said her name to a man in a gallery and it would have been a question about a seal and it would have been the right question and it would have gone into a room and there is no form anywhere for taking a question back out of a record." He did not take his hands out. "**A person in this empire gets to be private by nobody being able to say in a room what she is after.** That is the whole of the mechanism and it is the only privacy there is. My mother knew that and she used it, and I am not going to be the man who takes it off the door because he thinks he has a better use for it."

---

At about the tenth hour and a half she went and got the roll of holdings for that district, because a counter that has just sold a copy of a record ought to be able to say what is in it, and she had not been able to say what was in it and that was not a thing she was prepared to leave alone for another day.

The roll is arranged by street and not by year. That is the ordinary way of a roll of houses and it is the reason a person can look up a house in about four minutes and get it, and it is also the reason that no line in the roll carries a year, and the absence of a year on a line in that roll is not a gap in a record. It is a roll of houses.

The Kest house is on the fourth page. It is a line about four inches long and it is a house and a holding and a state, and the state is **disputed**, and the word is the office's word and it has a definition at the back of the roll, and the definition is in the ordinary way and it is one sentence long.

> *Disputed: where a claim is entered and the assent of the claimant is not entered, or is entered and does not carry a day.*

"It does not say whether it can be enforced," said Marn Ottery.

"It does not say," said Tamsin Rook, who had come down to the shelf without being asked and was standing behind her.

"It does not say, and the definition is a definition of how to write the word down and not a statement about a person, and I have written that word about four hundred times in eleven years and I have never once been asked what I thought it meant, and I have never once gone and found out." She put her finger on the line without touching it, which is a thing a person does in a gallery and not in a press and she was aware of the difference. "**A claim that is disputed is a claim that is recorded, and a claim that is recorded has got a name on it.** There is no column in this roll, and there is none in that definition, and there is none in any form in this building, that says whether the person whose name it is can be given anything."

"You have just described a door," said Tamsin Rook.

"I have just described a column with nothing in it and a row with a name in it and about four hundred years of people in this building copying one out of the other and never once putting the two together." She shut it. "**That is not a door. That is worse than a door**, and I have been on this side of a counter for eleven years and I am telling you it as a fact and not as advice, and the difference between those two is a fact and not advice, and I have said that phrase more times than I can count in four months and this is one of them."

---

At about the eleventh hour the clerk of thirty-four wrote four lines in the day's book of her own counter, with the day on it, in her own hand, about a roll and a column and a form on the same table, and she read it back twice, the second reading slower, and there was nobody in the gallery to hear the second reading and she did it anyway.

And then she said one sentence out loud to a woman of twenty-four and a man of thirty-eight, at the top of the stairs, on her way out, and it is the only thing she said on the way out and she said it in the ordinary way and it is a fact and not advice.

"**A person has come to a counter and asked for a record, and a record is what a person asks for when a person is going to do something with it.** In about four years there is going to be a hearing, and a hearing is a room, and a room is where records are asked for, and I have been selling copies out of this counter for eleven years and I have never once asked a single buyer what for."

---

And the fourth counter was open at about the fourth hour on the sixth day and it had been open for about four hours, and the girl of twenty-five was at it, and she had written a file number in ink and initialled it and had not been thanked, and the box of forms under the counter had about four hundred in it and the two names in the day-book had not been un-entered and were not going to be, and a man of thirty-eight had a copy of ten entries in the inside of a coat that can be read and cannot be leaned on, and a woman of sixty-three about four miles off a road had been read her own words twice and had asked for them a third time and had not got them, and the fifth line of a printed form had been done once.

Marn Ottery put her rag in the pocket of her apron and looked at the box of forms under her own counter and thought, in the ordinary way, that she had never in eleven years counted what was in it and had no reason to and would not be doing it now.
