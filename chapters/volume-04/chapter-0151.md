# Chapter 151: The Number of a Run

The first week of the fifth month of next year they were on a towpath four hundred and thirty miles from the bank, and it took six days, and there is no fund in this matter for the carriage of two people and there never has been, so the six days came out of a wage of seventeen shillings and a penny the week, and the whole of the six days came out of it in bunks and baked food and the price of a dry place to sit, and nobody paid either of them and nobody was asked to.

**The Notaries' Table in Auremar is not a court and does not look like one.** It is a long room on the ground floor of a building with a plate-glass front, and it has eleven counters in it, and at the back of each counter there is a rack of blanks in a pigeonhole, and above each counter there is a board with the fee of the four things that office will do.

It does not answer a question. It takes one — a question, from a person, in a form, on payment — and it answers it in about eleven weeks, in a room upstairs, by a person nobody at the counter can name in advance.

**The fourth counter is the one for scale instruments**, and it has been the fourth for a hundred and forty years, and the reason it is the fourth and not the first is that the three before it are for estates, for marriages, and for a thing nobody at this table will explain to a stranger. It is at the end of a passage. You go past the estates counter and past a door that has been propped open with a quill since somebody's time. The counter itself is a slab of oak with a rail on it, and there is a box under it about two feet by one, and a register on a shelf so high that a clerk has to be given a chair to reach it.

**The clerk is Marn Ottery. She is thirty-four and she has been at that counter for eleven years and she came to it from the second counter, where estates are, and she has never once been a lawyer and has never wanted to be one.**

The man who came up to the counter in the second week of the fifth month of next year put a piece of paper on the slab.

There was a number on it in his own hand. Four figures and no letters. Nothing else on the paper, and no name, and no day, and he had copied the number out of something and the copying had cost him nothing and the original was four hundred and thirty miles away on a shelf.

Tamsin Rook was four feet behind him, which is the distance a person stands when they are not a party to a thing and want to be able to hear all of it. She was in the room because a paper said she had to be in any room he was in, and she was not going to say that out loud at a counter, and he was not going to say it either, and the arrangement was the arrangement and had been for sixteen months and neither of them said it in a corridor on the first day of the road and neither of them was going to say it here.

"Four figures," said Marn Ottery.

"Four figures."

"Where is it from."

"It is a number of a run."

She did not write it down at that point. She looked at him for about four seconds longer than a person needs to be looked at.

"Say the rest of it," she said, "and I will tell you whether I have heard it before, and I am going to tell you the answer whether I have or not, and you are going to pay for the form whatever I say, and it is a shilling whether you fill it in or not."

"I have a number of a run," said Marek Kest, "and a number of a run is not a document, and I have come four hundred and thirty miles to find out what it is a number of."

"That is a question and it is a shilling and I am not taking it," said Marn Ottery.

She put the paper down square with the edge of the slab.

"Here is what I am not going to do. I am not going to be a door for you. I have been the door in this room for eleven years and I have one rule about it and the rule is that I do not find things out for people who walk in with a piece of paper and a face that has decided what is in it." She turned the fee board round a few degrees with one finger, which she did not need to do. "And the second thing, which is the same thing. There is a register on that shelf with the runs in it. It is the only place in this city that the numbers exist. If I go and get it down and turn to your four figures and read you a row, then in about four years you will tell somebody that a clerk of the fourth counter will look in the register for anybody who asks nicely, and there is no charge on that and it cannot be got back, and about nine people a year would find out about it the hard way."

"Then what is the price," said Tamsin Rook.

"A shilling and about eleven weeks," said Marn Ottery, "and a form, and your name at the top of it, and the answer to a person who asked. That is the only way that book gets looked at for anybody, and it has been the only way for a hundred and forty years."

---

She looked it up on her own time over two days and she told him so, and he believed her, and she did not care whether he believed her.

The register of runs is a fat book about four inches thick and it is ruled in five columns and the columns are the same five columns that have been in it since the book was opened. First: the number. Second: the day. Third: the device, described by a mark that is a mark and is not a name. Fourth: what the impression was put to. Fifth: **by whose authority.**

"A run number is not a document," she said. "It is a number in a run. Every impression off one device takes the next number, so that a missing number is a missing instrument, and that is the whole of the idea and it is a good idea and it has worked for a hundred and forty years. There is no run number anywhere in this empire that means a thing on its own. It means a gap, or it means nothing, or it means a row in a book in a room four hundred yards from where you are standing."

"And the row."

She had the book open on a stool and a chair and a space on the floor for a second knee, and she turned round to it and put her finger on a line and did not read the line.

"Two things and I am going to say them in the order I found them and not the order you want them. First. **Your four figures are a run of impressions off one device, and the run has about thirty rows in it that are not scale instruments at all.**"

Marek Kest did not say anything.

"Not one of the rows in that run says what the impression was put to in a way that would let anybody tell. The fourth column has about a hundred and forty years of practice in it and about thirty of the entries are a striking-out of a name out of a roll, because you take a name off a roll with an impression and the impression takes a number like anything else. **And there is no column in that book which says which is which, and there is not going to be one, and the reason there is not going to be one is that the fourth counter does not get asked.**"

"And the fifth column."

"**By whose authority.** That is a heading, and I want you to hear it as a heading and not as a charge, because it is a heading and it has been a heading for a hundred and forty years and it is the only reason that register has ever been allowed to keep a person's name in it at all. A name under that heading is an office being discharged. It is not an admission of anything. Anybody who tells you different has not read the heading."

"What's the name."

"I am not going to read you the name today," said Marn Ottery, "and I am going to tell you why, and you are not going to like it either, and both of those are the same conversation."

She closed the book and put her hand flat on it.

"**The day is the only part of that row anybody could use.** The name is a name and a name in a column with a heading over it admits nothing. The device is a mark and a mark is a mark. The fourth column says what the impression was put to and I have just told you it does not say it in a way anybody could use. **The day is the whole of it. And a clerk who reads a day aloud in a public room has put it into a second book, and there is no charge for that anywhere in this building, and it cannot be got back.**"

"I have a number."

"You have a number and I have given you a row, and the row is the truth and the row is worth nothing on its own, and that is the finding and it took me two days and it is the whole of what two days buys." She squared the book against the edge of the stool. "You can have the day. **Fourpence, in person, at this counter, out of the book, in your own hand, and about eleven weeks, because the answer to a question is given by a person nobody at this counter can name in advance and I do not know who will be standing here when it is ready.** Or you can have it never. Those are the two prices. One of them is free and it is the one you have already paid."

Tamsin Rook said, "The free one is not free."

"No," said Marn Ottery. "It never is. That is the second time in about four months I have wanted to argue that with a person, and the first time was with a woman of sixty-four in a bay four hundred and thirty miles from here in the fourth month of last year, and I did not manage it then either."

She put the register back on the high shelf and had to be given the chair again to do it.

---

The man put a shilling on the slab.

She looked at it and did not take it.

"That is for a question," she said. "You have not got a question. You have got a number and you have found out that it is a number in a run, and there is nothing at the end of a run that this table can certify for you, and the three things you want are a thing another court has to do and a thing an office of custody has to do and a thing that costs money nobody has."

"Then what is the question I can ask," said Marek Kest.

Marn Ottery picked the shilling up and turned it over once and set it back down on its edge.

"**You can ask me what a table may hold.** That is on nothing, it is not on the board, and I will answer it in full and I will not charge you for it, and I am doing it for a reason I am going to tell you at the end of it and the reason is not that I am generous."

"And the question I wanted."

"The question you wanted is the third of the four things on that board, and the third of the four is *what a party is*, and you cannot ask me what a party is until you can say who the party is, and you cannot say who the party is without a box, and I have not got a box for you and I have told you twice today that I am not going to look in that register for a person who has not got one." She pushed the shilling an inch towards him with one finger. "**And the reason I will answer the other question for nothing is that you are going to find out what a table may hold and then you are going to try to take something out of it, and I would rather tell you the price of that at a counter than find out about it in a corridor.**"

He picked the shilling up and put it in his coat and said, "What do I do with the number."

"You bring it back in about eleven weeks and you ask the question you can afford, and I will put it in the book, and then you will be a person who asked, and being a person who asked is not free and is not on the board and is the only thing this room has ever given anybody."

---

And there is one more thing about that room, and she said it to the empty counter at about the sixth hour on the second day, and it is not in any file, because nobody said it in a form.

**The board over that counter has four things on it and none of them is time, and every one of the four is a price a person could see before they committed themselves, and she has been proud of that board for eleven years the way you are proud of a thing you have never had tested.** A shilling is a shilling and everybody objects to a shilling. The four pence for a copy is the same. A person who can pay can find out what she is buying, and a person who cannot pay can find out nothing, and the whole of the design of that counter is that the price is on the wall and the price is the same for everybody and anybody may see the price before deciding.

And the fourth line of the form was free.

Not fourpence. Not a shilling. **Free.** It is the only line on that form with no charge against it anywhere in that building, and it is the one that changes what a person is, and it is free because nobody has ever had to charge for it, because nobody has ever been in a position to be charged.

She got the book of runs down off the shelf again at about the sixth hour because she could not leave it, and she looked at the five columns for about nine minutes, and she counted the rows in the run that were not instruments, and she wrote the number out twice on a sheet of foolscap and did not enter it in anything.

**Four things are on the fee board and none of them is what is in the book**, and that is not a fault in the board.

That is what a fee board is for.
