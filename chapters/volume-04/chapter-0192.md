# Chapter 192: What The Fifth Line Is For

The first sitting was on the fourth day of the second week of the fifth month of the year after, at the second hour, in a room with a rail in it, and the whole of what happened in the first nine minutes of it was that a record was asked for and produced, and the record was a record of property and debt for a house in a lane of eleven houses about four hundred and thirty miles down this river, and the copy of it had been bought at a counter in this city in the second week of the first month of the year after at fourpence, and the seal on the paper the buyer produced was not the seal of the table that issued the copy.

Marn Ottery is thirty-four. She has been at the fourth counter of the Notaries' Table eleven years and she is not a lawyer. She is a party to an instrument about a dead woman's name and she has no form anywhere for taking a party back out of a box, and she is the cheapest person in the building she works in and she says that out loud.

She was not on the bench and she was not on the rail. She was in the room because a counter is a thing a room asks about, and because being asked is the one thing that has never happened to her and she had a fortnight to think about whether she wanted it.

There were about thirty people in it. Two of them were there because of instruments and not because of the matter: a woman of twenty-four was at the back of the room on the ground that a court order made in a form in four days requires her in any room Marek Kest is in, which is a paper and not a preference and has been produced in a room with a rail in it about this many times this year, and she was not a witness and was not asked anything and could not be examined, and she has not been in any of those rooms for the asking.

---

The record came up out of a press in a building four hundred yards from the room, in the ordinary way, wrapped, with a day on the wrapper, and it was produced by a person who is not in this and is not named in this.

Four things about it were said out loud in a room and every one of them is a fact and none of them is advice.

The seal on the paper the buyer produced is not the seal of this table and it was not read out. The buyer's name was not asked for and is not kept at that counter, because a counter does not take the name of a person for a copy. And the copy was issued in full, at the price at which copies are issued, to a person, on a day, in the ordinary way.

And then the fourth thing, which is the one that is hers.

"There is a certification at the foot of the request for it," said Marn Ottery, "**and I wrote it in about nine seconds on the seventh day of the first week of the second month, and I said the price out loud in a room with three people in it before I wrote anything.** The price was that a document with my hand at the foot of it is findable in about four years by anybody with a copy of the right page, and she has known that since she said it and has not improved on it and is not going to."

"Was there a charge for it," somebody said.

"There is no charge for it."

"Why not."

"Because the fee board at that counter has four things on it and none of them is time, **and there is no line on that board for a certification of that counter's own book, because a table cannot charge a person for telling the truth about its own book.** Nobody has ever charged anybody for it at that counter and I am not starting and there is no form that requires me to."

---

Then a sheet was handed in and put on the table face up, and it is printed, and it is a sheet of the kind of thing anybody may have for nothing.

> *The first person says the thing in a room and the first person does not read it back afterwards.*
> *The day is entered in the first person's own hand and in nobody else's.*
> *The second person is entered in the room and the second person does not read it back to the person and does not show it to them.*
> *The second person writes what was said and writes nothing else.*
> *The second person asks the person, before the thing is said, whether she is a party to it, and the answer is written here.*

Five lines. It is the sheet a witness is entered upon. It is in nine hundred buildings in this empire, and a stack of them is on a table in this room about nine inches deep, and not one of them has ever been looked at by anybody for any reason.

Nobody had ever asked.

**There is no post in this empire whose job is to stand in front of a heading and ask**, and that is not an oversight, and it is the reason a form printed in nine hundred buildings does what it does in nine hundred buildings. And on the fourth day of the second week of the fifth month of the year after, in a room with a rail in it, somebody asked.

The question was put to the clerk of thirty-four who had produced the record, and it was put by a person with no office in this matter and no reason that could be printed, and it was four words long and it was *what is the fifth line for.*

---

She said the price out loud first.

That is the whole of it and there is nothing else to say about it. She had been asked questions in a room four times in eleven years and she has answered three of them, and the first three did not require a price, and this one did, because the answer is a thing she did and the thing she did is findable.

"I am a party to an instrument about a dead woman's name," said Marn Ottery. "**From the day I was served I can be served anything about it for the rest of my life, and there is no form anywhere for taking a party back out of a box, and that is the eighth time I have said it out loud in a room.** Four of the eight were about me. One was about a person who is not in this room and cannot be told. One was about nobody in particular. One was about neither of us. And this one is about a sheet of printed matter, and I am not going to say the number again, and I am going to say the number anyway, because a number is findable and a person who says a number can be held to it, and the number is about four years and a hand of mine and a sheet of paper with my name at the foot of it in a file four hundred and thirty miles from here."

Nobody thanked her and she had said in advance that nobody was to and there is no form anywhere that requires a person to be thanked.

---

Then she answered the question, and she answered it in the ordinary way, and the ordinary way took about nine minutes and it is the whole of the chapter, and Marek Kest was in the room for all of it and said the four words and then nothing else at all, and she had noticed a man who does not speak standing in a room in the second month of the year after and had said nothing about it then either.

"The fifth line is a question," she said. "**It is the only instrument in this empire that can hand a person the rest of what they wrote.** Not a copy. Not a register. Not a court. A question, asked in a room, out loud, by somebody who is entitled to ask it, before the thing is said, and written down with the answer on it."

"Who is entitled to ask it."

"The second person. That is what the line says. The second person asks the person, before the thing is said, whether she is a party to it."

"And who is the second person."

"The person in the room who is not the person being written about," said Marn Ottery. "That is the whole definition and it is in a printed sheet in about nine hundred buildings and it has been in about nine hundred buildings for about a hundred and forty years and it is the reason a register kept from that sheet has five columns and the third of them has nothing over it and what is in it is a hand and not a name."

---

Somebody said, "Then it is a form anybody could use."

"No," said Marn Ottery. "**It is a form nobody has ever been entitled to use.** That is the whole of it, and it took her about a hundred and forty years of books to see and she saw it in about nine minutes in a kitchen.

"**A second person cannot be served.** Being served is what makes a person a party. A party is the only person in this empire who can be told what a thing is going to be used for, and the only person a court can hand a question to is a party. So the fifth line asks a question of the one person in the room who by definition cannot be told, and it has been dead for about a hundred and forty years, and nobody took it out, and nobody ever found out it was dead."

"And it was done once."

"It was done once. In pencil, in a kitchen, in about nine minutes, in the first month of the year after." She did not raise her voice. "I did it. I am a party, and a party is the only person entitled to ask it, and I said the price out loud before I asked it, and I asked a woman of sixty-three in her own kitchen whether she was a party to a thing that was about to be said in a room, and she said no, and I wrote the no in pencil on her kitchen table, and a girl of nineteen wrote it out fair afterwards, and there is a sheet of paper in that kitchen with that woman's name on it and it is not the office's name."

"And it will not be done again."

"It will not be done again on that woman. **There is no form in this empire for asking a person the same question twice**, and if there were one then the second asking would be about the first, and she would be asked in a room whether she remembers, and a person remembering a thing and a person reading a thing back are two different acts and only one of them is evidence. She is sixty-three and she is owed one question and she has had it and nobody is going to ask her again and nobody has sent anybody and nobody is going to."

---

Then two things were asked of her and she refused both of them in the ordinary way, and she has said in advance that she would, and one of the two was refused in a room and the other was refused in a room and neither refusal is a refusal to be served and both of them are the same instrument.

They asked her what the certification had cost her. That is the first refusal, and it is the same one she made at the counter and it is the sentence *a wage is the whole of what there is* said by a woman of twenty about four hundred and thirty miles down this river in a different month about a different figure, and Marn Ottery has not heard that sentence and would have agreed with it.

They asked her for the name of the woman in the third column. That is the second refusal, and she gave the reason in one sentence and it is the reason and not a speech.

"That is a person who is in a book in a house about four miles off a road and whose name is not in it, and if I say the name in this room then a person in a book becomes a name in a room, and a name in a room is a thing that can be asked about, and there is no form anywhere for that, **and the man who printed the nine lines of that woman's own hand at fourpence a copy did not print her**, and she is not going to be the person who says it out loud, and he did not print her and she has not said it and neither of them is going to."

---

The clerk who took the minute was about twenty-five and had been in the building two years and wrote down, without being asked, that no form in this matter had ever been put to a person and been asked about, and that the reason a form printed in about nine hundred buildings had done what it did for about a hundred and forty years was that nobody had ever stood in front of a heading and asked what a word on it meant.

She read that back to Marn Ottery before she wrote it, which is not the practice in that room and she said so.

"You are not the practice in this room," she said, "and I have been in this room for two years and I have never once been the practice in this room, and I do not know who is, and I would like that in the minute as well."

It went in with the day on it.

---

Then she went out into the corridor and stood against the wall with her back to it for a while, and the girl of about twenty-five came out of the room about four minutes afterwards with the minute under her arm and the ink still on the side of her right hand, and stood about six feet away, and did not come closer, and Marn Ottery let her stand there.

"You have written that you were not the practice in this room," the girl said.

"I did not write it. You wrote it."

"I wrote it twice and I am going to write it a third time if you turn round, so you may as well hear it." She shifted the minute. "**Nobody has ever asked anything in that room. I have been in this building two years and there is a list of things asked in it and it has a hundred and forty lines on it and every one of the lines is a question about what a person wants to do, and there is not one line about what a piece of paper means.** And you have been at that counter eleven years and you are the person who answers them."

"Yes."

"And you told them."

"They asked."

"**Then that is what it is for.**" The girl put the minute down against her hip. "I am going to be asked about it. I am going to be asked why I wrote two lines in a minute that were not about the matter, and I have not got an answer, and if somebody asks me I am going to say that a clerk of thirty-four told the truth in a room and I wrote it down, and I am not going to say her name, because a counter does not take the name of a person for anything, and I have worked that out myself in nine minutes and I have not got anybody to tell it to."

"Then tell it to me," said Marn Ottery, "and I will not be able to do one thing with it, and that is the correct arrangement and not a disappointment."

**And she told it to her, standing in a corridor, in about four minutes, out loud, twice, the second time slower, and the girl of twenty-five wrote it down with the day on it in her own book, and neither of them thanked the other and there is no form anywhere that requires it.**

---

Nobody thanked her. She is not thanked in her own trade either and there has never been any expectation of it and she would not know what to do if there were. She is a party and a party is the cheapest person in a building and she says that out loud and she has said it four times now in four rooms and it has never once been a way of asking for anything.

What she took away from the room was a sheet of printed matter with five lines on it, and she took it because it was handed in as an exhibit and exhibits go back to the person who brought them, and it went into the counter's pigeonhole and out of the pigeonhole and into a book with no office on it, on a page with the day on it, and the day on the page is the day she was asked a question she had never been asked.

**She has written down in her own book, in her own hand, with the day on it, what she said in that room and who was in it and that she was the person who gave the answer.** She has also written down that a form printed in nine hundred buildings asked nothing of anybody for about a hundred and forty years, and that on one day in the fifth month of the year after it was asked what it was for, and that the answer took nine minutes, and that the answer is that it is the only instrument in this empire that can hand a person the rest of what they wrote, and that it has been used once, and that the once was in a kitchen, and that the price of the once was said out loud before the asking and was about four years, and that nobody thanked her and there is no form that requires it.

And a book with no office on it is a thing a form cannot be answered with, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that makes a person produce a thing that is not in a book with a name on the spine, and that is the only kind of record she has ever had, and it has cost her nine seconds and it is worth nothing and she is going to keep it.
