# Chapter 176: What She Wrote

She wanted to know what she had written down, and there was no way on earth for anybody to tell her, and she had known that for about nine years and had not said it out loud to anybody until a man of thirty-eight stood in her kitchen and asked her the wrong question first.

Bess Tarrant is sixty-three. She keeps a house at the end of a lane about four miles off the old river road. She cannot read a paragraph and has never been able to and was not taught at a school that anybody in this lane has ever heard of. She has a bundle of kindling under her arm most days because a man at the top of the lane cuts it and sells it at about nine places on this side of the water and hers is the one nearest the end.

She is not a device and she is not owed anything and she does not know that anything is happening.

---

"You are the one who was standing in that doorway," she said. "With the lamp."

"I was."

"You have not given me your name and I have not asked you for it, and that is a thing I am not going to start now, because you have come about a man with a trowel and not about me and I would rather be a house than be a question."

He said, "I am sorry. My name is Marek Kest."

"Well, that is the second time somebody has said that name in this lane in about nine years and I have never once seen the person."

---

"I want to know what I wrote," she said.

He had a lamp on the table and he had a sheet of paper on the table with four lines on it that he had written out of the register in his own hand about two hours before, and he had not said anything about it for about nine seconds, which is about nine seconds longer than a person ought to be allowed.

"You wrote nine lines," he said.

"That is not what I asked you."

"I know what you asked me. I am telling you what is written and I am not able to tell you what it says, and I have been standing here working out how to say that in a sentence for about nine seconds, and the sentence is this." He put the sheet down. "**I can copy a thing out of a room. I cannot read a thing out of a room, because I am not a reader, and I have refused to pretend to be one at a counter in a city four hundred and thirty miles up this river in front of a man with a paper in his hand.** I write. You want somebody to read."

"Then go and get one."

"I cannot get you one in a day. There is one coming."

---

"I am not going to have it brought here," he said, and that was the second wrong thing he said that day, and he knew it was wrong while he was saying it.

"Why not."

"Because it is a page out of a bound book and it is staying in the building it is in, and I have been four feet inside that building and I did not take it and I am not going to go back for it, and the reason is about nine months old and it is the first thing anybody ever taught me that I have kept." He said it slower. "A gap in a copy is a fact about the copying. A gap in an original is a different sentence. A gap in an original is a fact about a removal. **I am not going to be the man who writes that sentence about a book with a woman's name in it, and I would rather walk back up a lane than do it, and the lane is about four hundred yards.**"

Bess Tarrant looked at him for a while.

"You have thought about that before," she said. "That is not a thing a man says the first time he has been in a room."

"Twice," he said. "And the second time is the one I am doing now."

---

So she went up the lane herself, and she went at about the fourth hour with her coat on and a shawl over it, and she did not take kindling and she did not take a basket, and she had gone past that door about once every four days for about nine years without going through it and she went through it.

He followed her. He did not go first. **A person who opens a house has to be the one who goes through the door, and a person who goes through a door first is a person who has been in the room before anybody else in it, and there is no form in this empire that makes that the right way round and there is no form in this empire that makes it the wrong way round either.**

There was a lamp already burning. There was a woman sitting on the bench.

---

The woman was thirty-four and she had come down about four hundred and thirty miles of road to be in a room and she had a sheet of copyist's paper in her hand and a wet sleeve, because it had begun to rain in the second week of the twelfth month of next year and the lane does not take rain well.

"I am the one who reads," she said, before anybody had asked her anything. "**I am a copyist and not a reader and I want that said at the top so that nobody in this room has to spend nine minutes working out what I am.** And I am a party to an instrument about a dead woman's name, and I came down here because a letter is a thing a person can be shown and I said that out loud to the man who sent it before I decided to come, and a counter with nobody at it does not open and there is nobody to cover and no form for covering, and I have had four days of that counter shut and I am telling the room the number because the number is the price."

"You could have written to me," said Bess Tarrant.

"I could have written to a man who cannot read you what you wrote, and I would not have known whether he had done it, and neither would you," said Marn Ottery.

---

She read it.

Not off the sheet. Off the book, on the bench, with the lamp on it and her hand on the page, and she read the fourth column of that line, which is in a woman's hand of about nine years old, and she read it once and then she read it again, and the second reading was slower.

Nobody in the room had decided that. It was the way the woman of thirty-four had been reading things out loud at a counter for eleven years, about nine hundred times, and the way a girl of seventeen had been reading things out loud on a platform in the cold for about a season, and neither of them has ever explained it to anybody and both of them have been asked and one of them has said out loud where she got hers and the other one has not.

**The first reading is for the words and the second reading is for the people who did not have them the first time.**

"It is nine lines," said Marn Ottery. "I am going to read all nine and then stop, and then I am going to read them again, and I am not going to do anything else, and I am not going to tell you what any of them means, because I do not know and neither does he."

---

Nine lines, in a woman's hand of about nine years old, and this is the whole of what was said in that room by a person who was not asked where the idea came from and did not say:

> *The house is to hold nothing.*
> *Nothing is to be entered against it and nothing is to be taken out of it.*
> *The boy is to be put down as holding nothing and to be left so.*
> *A person who holds nothing cannot be given anything and that is the whole of it and it is the best of it.*
> *She was not asked where it came from and she did not say and I have written that down because I did not think of it at the time and I have thought of it since and about nine years is a long time to have thought of it.*
> *The woman is to be put down as having said it and not as having asked it.*
> *It is to be done on a box and not on a paper, because a paper goes to a house and a box goes to a roll.*
> *Nobody is to be asked the family anything.*
> *That is all of it. I have not put anything else down and I was not told to leave anything else off.*

---

The second reading was slower and Bess Tarrant heard the whole of it and the whole of it was about a boy of nine or ten and a woman and a box.

Then she said, "That is not what I remember."

"That is correct," said Marn Ottery. "**A person remembering a thing and a person reading a thing back are two different acts and only one of them is evidence.** You wrote it in your own hand nine years ago and you were in a room and you were not shown it afterwards. What you remember is what a person remembers, and it is not wrong, and it is not a copy of what is on that page, and there is no form in this empire that will make the two of them agree and there is no form that will make them agree at all."

"I remember a woman standing up," said Bess Tarrant. "I do not remember sitting down. I have not thought about it since the day."

---

"You said you wanted to know what you wrote," said Marek Kest.

"I do want to know what I wrote."

"Then you have just had it read to you twice, and I am not going to read it a third time, and the reason I am not is that you are not going to be able to remember the words and the third time would only make the thing feel more certain than it is, and I would rather you had it twice and were unsure than three times and were sure." He put his hand flat on the bench, on the lime, about a foot from the book. "I want to ask you something and I am going to ask it badly."

"Everybody in this matter has asked me something badly for about four months and I have said yes to all of them."

"The woman whose name is in the first column of that line. The first column is not in your hand. The second column is empty. That is the only line in the whole of that book with no day in it and I have not worked out why and I am not going to guess at it in front of you."

"Her name is Kest," said Bess Tarrant. "**Ilyra Kest.** I know that much because it is the first column and I copied it out when I was keeping the book, and I have never once said it out loud in this lane and I have said it twice today.**

"And the boy in the fourth column is a Kest as well, because that is what I remember and I remember it because I was the one writing it and I have thought about it for about nine years, which is a long time to have thought about a boy of nine or ten who was not asked anything in a room and who was not told what the room was for." She looked at him. "**And that is your mother. And you are a Kest. And you are thirty-odd and I have not seen you before this week and I would not have known you from the boy.**"

"Yes," he said.

"Then I am sorry about the ninth line, and I am not going to say I am sorry about the other eight, because I wrote them and I have had about nine years and I have never once been asked whether they were right, and I would like to be asked."

---

He did the law in her kitchen, afterwards, with the lamp on the table and the kindling on the hearth, and it took him about four minutes and he was not good at it and he said so twice.

"There is an office in this empire that a human being can hold for a while, for a fixed number of years, not more than seven, and it is an old office and it is supposed to be temporary and I have never seen the compact it comes out of and I am telling you about it second-hand, from my mother and from a clerk of thirty-four, and I am not a lawyer and I hold nothing." He put his hands flat on her table. "**A person who wants it has to hold no enforceable inherited asset or right. Not be poor. Hold nothing that anybody can enforce.**"

"And a house that holds nothing."

"A family that holds nothing. And it does not matter how they came by it. The test is not a test about being poor. **A family that holds nothing enforceable is not a poor family, it is an eligible one, and a person out of it is an eligible candidate, and the test was written to be passed by a person with nothing.**"

She said, "Then she passed you on purpose."

"She passed me on purpose," said Marek Kest, "and she passed me on purpose about nine years before you wrote that down, and I have been eligible since the day her name came out of a file, and I did not know, and I was not told, and there is nobody in this empire who was ever going to tell me, and that is not an accident, and it is not anybody's carelessness. **It is the fourth line of what is written in that book. Nobody is to be asked the family anything. A family that can be given nothing cannot be served, and a person who cannot be served cannot be asked, and that is not a side of it. That is the whole of it, and she wrote it, and she wrote it as the reason it was worth doing.**"

The lamp guttered and neither of them did anything about it.

"I have not got one word to say about my mother," he said. "I want that written down in the room, in those words, because in about four years there is going to be somebody with a pen who is going to want to know what I thought, and he is going to find the four words and they are going to be the true ones."

---

"Did I do it wrong," said Bess Tarrant.

That was the other want. It was under the first one and it had been under it for about nine years and it came out about nine seconds after the first one stopped.

"I do not know," said Marn Ottery. "And that is the whole of the answer and I am not going to soften it, because you are asking me whether a form was filled in correctly about nine years ago and I am a clerk of thirty-four who has been at a counter for eleven years and I will tell you exactly what I am able to tell you. **Nobody knows. There is no right way and no wrong way, because the sheet that was printed for this has five lines on it and the fifth of them has never once been done by anybody, in about a hundred and forty years, and so there is nothing to compare your nine lines to except about nine hundred copies of the same nine lines in the same hand.**"

"Then how is anybody meant to know."

"You are not," said Marn Ottery. "**You are meant to write it down in your own hand, which you did, and to not be shown it afterwards, which is the fourth line, and which is correct, and which is the cruellest sentence anybody ever printed in about nine hundred buildings.**"

---

At about the seventh hour Bess Tarrant asked the third question, and she asked it standing up with her shawl on, in the doorway, with the lamp behind her, and it is the only thing anybody in that room did not have an answer to that was not a fact.

"Will somebody tell her," she said. "That I wrote it down. That it is there. **I have been in this house about four feet in about nine years and I have never gone in, and if I had gone in then somebody would have had to tell me that there was a book, and there was a book, and I did not know, and that is about nine years of me not knowing, and I am not asking to be thanked for it and I am not asking to be told it was right.** I am asking whether anybody is going to go and say to her, wherever she is, that it was written down and it is still there."

Nobody answered for a while. The rain had come on properly and it was coming on the roof of a house that has no roof on three sides of it, and it made a noise like a person counting.

"A person in a house that is shut has no address," said Marek Kest at last. "**A person who is not told has not been erased. Those are two different things, and I have known the second one for about four months and I have never once been able to say it out loud without the first one coming with it, and I am saying both of them now and the first one is the harder and I am not going to leave it out to make the second one easier.**"

"That is a very cruel thing to say to a woman of sixty-three in her own doorway," said Bess Tarrant.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

And she said, "Well, it is about four miles back down the lane and it is raining and I am sixty-three, and I have a bundle of kindling to fetch on the way, and if you are going to stand there looking at me then stand there and if you are not then go and get the wood."

He got the wood.

And nobody thanked anybody, and nobody said the woman's name out loud again that day, and a man of thirty-four read a woman's own words back to her twice in a house about four miles off a road, the second reading slower, and neither of them knew where the habit came from and one of them had said out loud in a corridor four hundred and thirty miles away that she knew, and the other one had not been told, and neither of them is going to find out from this.
