# Chapter 171: A Day at the End of a Notice

There is no form in this empire that will let a person put a day at the end of a notice.

That is not a complaint. It is a thing she worked out on her own, at about the fifth hour on the second day of the second week of the eleventh month of next year, standing at the end of a corridor about thirty feet long with a rag in her hand, and it took her about nine minutes, and she has had nine years to be ready to work it out.

Ivet Sarn is a clerk of thirty-eight. She is at a window about four feet off the ground and about two feet wide at the back of a low building on a flood terrace, and above the sill there is a board with a number of names on it in chalk which she wipes every morning herself, and behind the building there is an embankment corridor with four presses in it, and she has the key to the corridor on a string on her hip and the string has been shortened twice and both shortenings were hers.

She is in charge of the four presses. Nobody appointed her. There is no form that makes a person be in charge of a thing and there never has been, and the way she came by it is that she was the only one left holding a key.

---

The notice has been on a nail at the end of the corridor for about nine days.

It is a list of works. It has jobs against days. It has a name at the foot of it.

And the name at the foot of it is not a person. It is the name of a district, and there is no day at the end of it, and the thing at the end of it is the only part of the shape that is missing, and it is missing on purpose by somebody who has never had to look at it.

A woman of twenty-four worked that out in this corridor in the last week of the tenth month of next year and said it out loud with two other people in it, and it was correct, and it has been in a book with no office on it with the day on it ever since. **A list of works gets to be the only sentence anybody in this empire can act on the same day because a person's name is at the foot of it. Take the name off and it is a list of jobs with nobody's promise at the end of it, and that does not stop. It goes on.**

So she stood at the end of the corridor and looked at it for a while, and then she went and found the chalk, because the board over her own window is wiped every morning and there is chalk in this building because there has always been chalk in this building.

---

"What are you doing."

He had come up the corridor with a barrow and a toolbag and he was about forty and he worked for the district works and he was not a party to anything and he was not a witness to anything and he was a man who had come to do a day's work.

"I am putting the day at the end of it," said Ivet Sarn.

"That is our notice."

"It is a notice on a wall in a corridor behind a building and I am the clerk of that building, and there is a nail in it that came out of this wall, and I have been in this corridor nine years and nobody has ever told me I was not to write on a wall."

He put the barrow down. He looked at the notice and then at her and then at the notice again, and the looking took about four seconds and it was not a stupid look.

"**The district will not recognise a day written by a clerk at a window.**"

"You do not have to recognise it," she said. "That is the whole of it and it has taken me nine minutes and you have had about four seconds of it. The day is on the notice. **A notice with a day on it is a notice with an end, and the end is the fourth of this month, and after the fourth it is your district's problem and not mine.**"

"It is our building we are clearing."

"It is a press in a wall in my corridor and the press is not going anywhere, because a wall is not a thing anybody moves, and the thing in it is going into a cart, and I have been in charge of that thing for nine years."

He said, "Who told you to write that."

And she said, in the plainest words she has, and she said them slowly, because he is a man with a barrow and not a lawyer and he is owed the plain version:

"Nobody. **There is no form that makes a person be in charge of a thing and there never has been, and I did not apply, and nobody sent me, and I have a key on a string and that is the entire instrument, and if I had a form I would show it to you and there is nothing to show.** A thing nobody told me to do is the only kind of thing I am allowed to do, because every other kind is somebody's duty and a duty can be told to stop and a thing nobody told me to do cannot."

---

She wrote the day at the end of the notice in chalk, at the bottom, under the district's name, in a hand of about sixty.

The fourth of the eleventh month of next year.

Then she went back to her own window and got her own book out, which is a hard-backed book with no office on it that she has kept for nine years, and she wrote the same day at the foot of a page in ink, and she read it back twice, the second reading slower, and there was nobody in the building at that hour who heard either reading.

**The chalk on the notice is the half that anybody can act on the same day, and it can be washed in about nine seconds by any person who wants a list of works with no end in it.** The ink in her own book is the half that is findable, and it is in a book that nobody can wash, and there is a person in this building who can say she wrote it and when.

Both halves. And there is no form that asks for both.

---

The man with the barrow did not go away for a while.

"I have four men," he said. "They are on it. They are on it because it is a list of works and a man on a list of works is working, and if I take a day off the end of it then either I have not got four men or I have got a day of nobody's."

"Then you have a day of nobody's," said Ivet Sarn, "and that is a true sentence and it is about half of what I have just done to you, and the other half is that a duty with a day at the end of it is a duty somebody has to be in a room for, and a man on a list of works is a man who is working and not a man who is in a room, and I have not got to that yet and I intend to."

He looked at her for a moment longer.

"Nobody thanked me for the nine days I have spent on this notice," he said, "and I am not expecting it, and I would like it noticed that I said the day out loud to you before you wrote it, which I did not have to do."

"It is noticed."

"Good." He took the barrow up. "I am not going to say whether the district will do anything about it. I am a man with a barrow. **If it does not, it does not, and that is not a reason for me to take the day off the chalk.**"

He put two fingers in his mouth and whistled up the corridor, and a man of about twenty came down it with a barrow of his own and a coil of rope on his shoulder, and he had not been asked to come and he was not asked why he had.

"Hold that end," said the first man, and then he said, to her, without any ceremony at all, "**Say the day again for him.**"

She said the day again. The second man looked at the chalk and then at the notice and then at the chalk.

"That is a day," he said.

"It is a day," said Ivet Sarn.

"Then I am not going to be here on it." He put the barrow down and coiled the rope again, which he had not needed to do. "**I will be here on the day before it, because the day before it I am working. And on the day itself I am either working or I am standing in a corridor not working, and if I am standing in a corridor not working then I am late, and I do not get paid for being late and I do not get paid for standing in corridors.**"

"That is a true sentence," she said, "and it is the first one anybody has said to me about this that I had not worked out, and I worked it out in about nine minutes and you have done it in four seconds, and I would like you to know that I am not being sarcastic and I am not asking you to do anything about it."

"I know you are not," said the man. "I have been on a list of works since I was your age and a list of works is a list of works, and if a day comes off the end of it then the day comes off the end of my wage, and the wage is a day and the day is me." He shouldered the rope. "**Put it on the wall, though. Because in about four days somebody is going to come down here with a piece of paper that says the fourth and I am going to have to decide, and if the wall says the fourth then that is one more piece of paper saying it, and that is how it gets to be a fact instead of a rumour.**"

And that was the whole of his interest in the matter and he took the barrow up the corridor with him, and neither of the two men was thanked, and the second one was not thanked harder than the first.

---

She had a rag in her hand the whole time and had not used it, because the board over her own window is wiped every morning and she had wiped it at about the sixth hour and had been holding the rag since, and a person holding a rag for three hours is a person who is waiting to be asked to do something and is pretending not to be.

She went back to the window and put the rag on the sill and looked at the board.

There were about nine names on it. **A name on a board is the only thing in this district that a person can stand in front of and point at, and there is no other one, and a man of about fifty asked her to take his off it in the first week of the tenth month of next year and she refused him and he said the sentence that has made it possible for her to say a true thing in one line ever since: *I did not think of it like that.***

Her own name is not on the board. There is no form that would put it there and she has looked, and there is no form that would take it off either, and both of those are the same absence and it is the only absence in this building she is not frightened of.

---

Here is the other half, and she worked it out at about the seventh hour with nobody in the corridor, and she said it out loud to the wall because there was nobody else and because saying a thing out loud is the only way she has ever found of being sure she has said it.

**A person who puts a day at the end of a duty is a person who has accepted it.**

There is no form for accepting one either. She has checked. There is a form for a thing being put to a district and there is a form for a thing being taken out of a district's hands and there is a form for a person refusing, and the refusal is the one with a reason in it and no box for a why, and she has read one of those and it is in a file in this building with a name at the foot of it that is not hers.

And there is no form for un-accepting. Nobody in nine years has ever been asked whether they will be in a corridor on a day, and she is the person who has just written one, and she has done it about four days before the day, and if the day arrives and she is not in the corridor then there is a notice in a corridor with a day on it that nobody stood at the end of.

**Nobody in this matter has told anybody how long they have.** She said that in this corridor in the last week of the tenth month of next year to two people and a witness, and it was true then, and it is truer now, and now there is a day on it in two places and neither place can be washed and one of them can.

---

The first press is a file of the sixth year. She has not opened it in this matter and she is not going to.

The second is a bound set of district sheets.

The third is a press of returned notices with a spike through them.

The fourth is a press in a wall with a shelf in it, and a bound schedule in it that has been in that press longer than anybody in that building has been alive, and nothing in that building's records says so.

She went to the fourth one at about the eighth hour and did not open it. She put her hand flat on the door of it the way a person does to a thing they are in charge of and are not going to open.

"There is a form in a coat in this matter," she said to it, "with four lines on it, given away by a man who walked to a lock to give it away, and I have filled it in once, and the day on it is my day and not the day of the thing, and I did not read it out and I am not going to read this one out either."

**A lintel was a thing I did to a building. This is a thing I am going to be asked about for the rest of my working life and I am not writing it twice.**

And then she said the part of it she had not said out loud, including to herself, which is that she has been in charge of four presses for nine years and has never once been asked what any of them is for, and that she worked out what this one is for in the first week of the tenth month of next year in about nine minutes while she was waiting for two people, and that she has told nobody what it cost her to work it out, and that it cost her the whole of the thing she had been for nine years.

---

A person in charge of a thing is not a party to it.

She said that sentence to nobody at about the ninth hour and then did not say it again, because she has said it once already in a corridor with four people in it and it is a thing a person says once.

A party is a person a book can be true about, and it is served, and it is asked questions, and it holds a box and a sheet and a fee and a term and a day at the end of it. She is a person a book can be true about, and she is served, and she is asked, and she holds none of those things, and **that is the only reason she is the one person in this matter who can be told to stop, and the only reason it costs anything to tell her to stop is that she would be the one who has to go and look.**

She is going to lose the fourth press on the fourth of this month, or she is not, and nobody has told her which and she has not asked, and she has not refused, and she is not going to say in a corridor in front of a man with a barrow that she has not refused.

She wrote the day on the notice. That is not a refusal and it is not an acceptance of the losing. It is a third thing and there is no form for it.

---

Nobody thanked her.

She said in advance, in the corridor, to a man with a barrow and to a wall, that nobody was to, and nobody did, and the finding on that is the same finding as always: **a woman who says in advance that she is not going to be thanked is almost always believed and almost never taken at her word, and those are the same finding.**

And a notice on a nail at the end of an embankment corridor behind a low building on a flood terrace, in the second week of the eleventh month of next year, has jobs against days and a name at the foot of it and **a day at the end of it in chalk**, and the same day in ink in a hard-backed book with no office on it, and the fourth is the fourth.

A clerk of thirty-eight is going to be in that corridor on the fourth because she wrote the day and not because anybody told her to, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that can put her there, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that can keep her out, and she has four days to think about the second of those.
