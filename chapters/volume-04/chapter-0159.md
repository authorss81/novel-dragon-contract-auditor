# Chapter 159: The Second Question on the Back of a Form

The rail at the Reed Cut gate is a piece of iron with a curve in it that somebody's grandfather set into the platform so that a boat has something to hold against while it is being worked, and there is a schedule nailed to it, and there is a list of works nailed beside it, and neither of them has a date on it that anybody in this matter has ever read out.

The schedule is on good paper and it is about eleven inches by nine and it has been folded once across the middle and it carries the water office's device, which is not a name, and a memorandum number, which is a number of a book, and a foreman's name at the foot of it, which is the only person's name on the whole sheet.

It is on four nails and there are the holes of an earlier set in the rail, and the four old nails are still in the rail, and nobody has pulled anything down.

Tavi Venn, who is seventeen and has been on the Reed Cut bank since she was old enough to stand on a platform, stood on that platform at about the seventh hour in the second week of the seventh month of next year and read both sheets out in plain words, twice, the second reading slower.

There is a pail on that platform every evening and there has been for years. There was a girl of eleven on it with the pail.

She asked four questions. Three of them were about the work and were answered. The fourth was not a question anybody asked her and it was not answered, it was stood with, and she stood next to the girl of eleven on a cold platform for about eleven minutes and did not tell her why she could not read, and did not tell her that anybody owed her anything, and did not use her for anything at all.

The girl with the pail is a girl with a pail. She is owed nothing by anybody on that bank and she was used for nothing on that bank, and the whole of what happened to her this evening is that somebody stood next to her while a thing was read out loud. There is a woman of about seventy-one at the fourth house at the north of the ford who cannot read a paragraph and has asked in writing to be asked at her own door four times a year, and she is being asked, and the two of those women have never been in a room together and it is nobody's doing that they have not: it is that nobody has ever put them in one. There is a girl of eleven on this bank called Rosamund Crane who reads, and she was not on that platform, and nobody at that gate has ever mixed the three of them up, and the reason is that only one of the three has ever asked anybody for anything.

---

And then, on the same platform, with the whole of the evening's crowd on it, she asked a question.

She asked it once. She did not explain it. She did not apologise for it and she did not say that anybody could go home.

*And who is the cheapest person in this matter to put in a box.*

Then she read the schedule again, the second reading slower, and the fourth nail held.

---

A person who is not a lawyer working out a thing out loud in a room, and putting it on the back of a form, is how most of what anybody knows about this matter was got, and none of it was got in a room with a desk in it. A clerk of thirty-four wrote nine lines on the back of a blank in a city four hundred and thirty miles up a road and gave them to a carrier at fourpence and did not put them in a file. A clerk of thirty-four ruled three boxes on the back of another blank. A clerk of a court on a flood terrace wrote a finding in a column with her hand against every line of it and no heading over it. And the room is worse than theirs, because theirs had a stove.

She wrote it on the back of a leaf in her own satchel book, where she keeps what she cannot read to whom, in her own hand, with the day against each line, and the leaf went into the book and the book went into the satchel, and it is findable, and about four years from now a person with fourpence can have a copy of it out of a book with no office on it.

She said the thing about the pen at the seventh hour and she said it to a man of thirty-eight who has a wage of seventeen shillings and a penny the week, and he did not laugh, and about four people on that platform looked at her, and she has been looked at by about nine hundred people since the second of the fifth month and has never once minded being looked at and has minded being thanked about four times.

---

She asked it again in the bay at about the eighth hour, because a foreman of fifty-one at the back had not heard it and said so, and she asked it again the same words and did not explain it twice.

The answer took nine minutes and four people had it between them and nobody wrote it down at the time, and it is the finding of the third week of the sixth month of next year and it is on the back of a leaf in a satchel and in a book with no heading in about nine hands, and it is this.

A body cannot be made a party. **A body cannot be instructed and a body cannot stop, and it does not know it is doing anything, and it will go on doing it after every single person in it is dead, and that is why a company is the right place to keep a book and the wrong place in the world to keep a duty.** That is a keeper's three parts and it is about eighteen months old and about nine hundred rooms have had it.

A person with a wage can be made to answer for a thing.

**A person with nothing enforceable behind them can be made to answer for nothing at all, because there is nothing to be liable for, and because nobody can serve a person who has nothing to serve.**

"So it is a person with nothing," said the book-keeper of thirty-eight.

"It is the cheapest person in the matter, and there are two kinds of nothing, and the second kind is the one that matters and nobody has named it in a room yet." Tavi Venn put her hand flat on the bench. "**Not *has no money*. Has nothing that anybody can be made to answer for. No house. No share. No claim that anybody can enforce. Nothing with a name on it that a court can take off a person and give to another person.**"

"A person with a shop is not the cheapest person in this matter," said the woman with the shop, who had come down off the platform to ask it and had not gone back up. "That is me. I have got a shop and the shop has lost its credit and I have been eleven months not knowing what I am, and you have told me what I am this morning and I would rather you had not."

"**A shop is a thing somebody can be made to answer for.** I am not being unkind. I am saying that a shop is a road into a person and a wage is a road into a person, and both of those roads are open to anybody who wants to go down them, and they are open now and they are open tomorrow and they are open to about nine men in three gangs who have asked in writing for the name of the man who instructs them and have not been answered, and one of them has said out loud that asking may have cost him the contract."

---

"The second half," said the chair of sixty-four, from the bench against the north wall.

"It is about four sentences and I have been carrying it for nine days and I want it said in a room and not on a form."

"Say it."

"**A person who is made blank of enforceable rights is not made safe. That is the first sentence and it is the whole of what this morning was about, and everybody who has ever been made blank has been told the other one, by somebody who meant it kindly.**

"The blankness is a door with a person in it. And the person is usually the one who did the blanking. **Not usually. Nearly always. Because the person who can make a family blank is the person in the family who is allowed to do it, and there is about one of those in a house and it is not a coincidence that it is nearly always a woman, and I am not going to say anything more about that than that sentence.**"

A bay of about thirty did not make a sound.

"**And here is the part that is the answer. A family with nothing enforceable can be put to by nobody. That is true, it is real, and it has kept at least one family out of one office in one district for about twenty years. And a family with nothing enforceable can also be named as the road by which somebody else is put to, and that is also true, and it is the same piece of paper doing both at once, at the same hour, with the same hand at the foot of it.**

"So the person who arranged the first one is the only person who can say which of the two was meant. **And she is nearly always the person who arranged it, because she is the one who could.**"

Nobody in that bay said the word *eligibility*. It is in a book in a city four hundred and thirty miles up a road, it has been in books like that book for a very long time, and no clerk who works at that counter has ever had cause to use it, and that is not because of anything anybody has done in this bay.

---

And the man who is a clerk of the Inheritance Desk, and holds no certification, and is entered in no register of persons who read, and has a bar discharged on the twelfth of the ninth month of last year, and has three pounds eleven and threepence of his own wage gone nineteen days at a time out of a weigh-house ledger under the word *held*,

stood in the middle of that bay and did the arithmetic, and it took him about four seconds, and he did not say it out loud.

He said, "That is a category."

"That is a category," said Tavi Venn.

And she did not follow it, and she has been asked since why she did not follow it and she has said the same thing both times, which is that she had been asked to read a thing out loud, and she is paid forty-five pence a day to read things out loud, and a person who is paid to read is a person who can be told to stop, and this is the first arrangement in this matter in which the person who asks is also the person who can be told no, and she was not going to be the person who turned it into the other thing.

She used it once already, in the fourth month, on a woman of about seventy-one at the fourth house at the north of the ford, at her own door, four times a year, exactly as she asked in writing, and it is the whole of the power she has ever had, and it is a door and a knock and it is not a wall and she has never once said it was a wall.

---

"A carter of thirty-one stood up at the back," she said afterwards, "and asked a question, and it was a bad question, and it was the only question anybody asked all morning, and I am going to say it because he is on the list first and he is owed a hearing and there is no form in this empire for compelling a person to be brought to a room and the review is about the first week of the eighth month of next year and a season is about ninety days and it began on the second of the fifth."

"What did he ask."

"He asked whether the review is a day a person comes to, or a day the work stops." Tavi Venn shut her satchel. "And it is the correct question and there are about nine people in this bay who have been not asking it for four months, and about nineteen people behind that bank have already answered it by not coming, and one of them is the woman of about seventy-one and she has said the same reason twice and she has said she is not going to say it a third time."

"And what did you tell him."

"I told him the truth, which is that nobody in this bay knows yet, and that a clerk in a city has an answer coming in the first week of the eighth month of next year about a completely different thing, and that the two of those are in the same week, and that there is no form in this empire that puts two dates in one box."

---

And a chair of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph had the question read to her at about the ninth hour, twice, the second reading slower, and asked for it to be read again a third time because of one word in it.

"Which word."

"*Cheapest.*" She had the satchel book brought to her and had the leaf turned to and had it read to her again, and then she said: "Put the finding in the book."

"Not the question," said Rennick Adley.

"Put the finding in. All of it, in your hand, with the day on it, and the four of you who worked it out can read it back and the two words *cheapest* and *party* come out of it."

"Why those two."

"Because a book is a place a thing goes to be looked up, and I am not putting a judgement in a book, and a judgement in a book is a machine with a heading over it, and I have been a chair for nineteen years and that is the only thing I have learned that I am sure of."

---

And nobody thanked the reader, and she was not thanked, and she is not going to start.

**A reader who is paid can be told to stop.** That is the whole of what the first season bought, and it is not a wall and it is not justice and it is a list with a name at the foot of it and a day against that name and a door in the west end that shuts.

And a guarantee under the fire-and-water device stands offered and unanswered on a bank about four hundred and forty foot long, at no charge, answerable at any time afterwards by a person answering in its terms, and there is no such person and there is not going to be one, and about sixty people under sixteen are inside it, and inside a guild's blank list, and inside a rotation they have not been asked about, and have been told about none of it.

And the Lowcross bill is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and it is unpaid and nobody is liable, and there is no line for a bridge in that fund and there has not been one in nineteen years, and about a penny a day is the real cost of it, and four hundred and eleven people live on the far side and the district's return for four weeks says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings, and those are two figures and they are never the same.

And the review is about the first week of the eighth month of next year and nobody on that bank is going to be paid to walk to it.
