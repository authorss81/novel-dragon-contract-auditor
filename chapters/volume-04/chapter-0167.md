# Chapter 167: A Correction on a Table

She came into the back room with a box on her hip and a lamp in her hand and put the box on the table and did not say good evening and did not apologise for that either.

The back room is off a corridor behind a building on a flood terrace. It has a table in it and a bench and a chair with a broken front leg and a window about two feet wide with a pane out of it, and the light in it in the third week of the ninth month of next year came off the water and not off the sky.

Aurelia Pell. Human. A provincial archivist, and her office is four days' cart travel from here, and she is on the round. She came on the ordinary round with a box and a list of eleven stops and this is the fourth, and she has four days at this stop and three of them are for lifting and copying and one is for the cart.

She is Pell Vey's daughter. **That is a fact about her and not a fact about this room, and the two people who came into this room in the fourth hour did not know it, and she did not tell them, and they did not ask, and if they had asked she would have answered in one word and then put the box down.**

The same surname is not a door into anything. It has never been. She has been in a building four hundred and thirty miles from her father's for four days and she has not gone and looked at him and she is not going to.

---

The disagreement was nine years old and it had been copied forward every year since by somebody in a hurry.

Her office holds a copy of this district's roll. The roll is here. They are the same roll up to a point and after a point they are not, and the point is a page with fifty-one entries on it, and forty-nine of the fifty-one agree, and two do not, and the two that do not are in a column headed for the person who did a work.

The copy says a company. The roll says a person.

"I have had this in a box on a cart for four days," she said, "and I have got it out at a table, and I am going to look at the two lines and then I am going to do one thing and it will take nine minutes, and then you can both get out of my light."

---

She had a pen. She had a bottle. She had a blotter made of about four sheets of paper folded and it was grey.

She read the two lines out loud, both of them, twice, the second reading slower, and she did not do that because of anything that had been done on a bank four hundred and thirty miles down a river. She did it because her office has a rule and the rule is that a person reading two versions of the same thing out loud finds the disagreement about nine seconds before a person reading them silently does, and the rule was written by somebody in her office about forty years ago and it is the only thing in nine years that has never once been wrong.

Line one, the copy:

> *West ditching, second week, carried out by an unincorporated company, water office credits received.*

Line two, the roll:

> *West ditching, second week. Tarn Belwick, forty-one, of the house at the end of the lane. Money to be paid to the man on the list and not to any body.*

"That is nine years old," she said. "And the man is alive, and I have found him, because I got out of the cart at this stop and went and asked a woman of about sixty in a lane whether there is a man called Tarn Belwick in it, and there is, and he is forty-four now and he did not know he was in a roll and he does not want to be in one and he told me so in front of his wife."

---

"Then why are you correcting it," said Marek Kest.

"Because he did the work. "She uncapped the bottle. "That is the whole of the reason and I have known it for four days and I have not said it in a room before and I am going to say it once and then I am going to do the work."

"That is not a reason anybody would—"

"It is the only reason there is. "She looked up. "A roll is a copy of a copy of a thing, and every copy in it is a person copying, and the reason a roll can be corrected at all is that a person is sitting at a table with a pen. A person can put a name into a column and a person can take a name out of a column and it is the same hand both times and it is the same table both times and there is no instrument anywhere that stops it. And there is nothing in this empire that stops anybody doing it. That is not a flaw in the record. That is what a record is."

The lamp moved. The water moved.

"And I am going to say the rest of it because you have got the face of a man who has just understood something and I would rather you had it in the right order. "She dipped the pen. "**A person can be put into a column of a roll by hand and a person can be taken out of it by hand, and there is no form for stopping it, and there is no office whose job it is to notice, and it will be exactly as true in nine years as it is this evening, and that is the reason nobody has ever been able to find out who did a particular one.** Not because it was well done. Because it was ordinary."

---

She wrote it in the margin of the copy, in her own hand, with the day against it, and then she wrote the source of the correction under the day, and then she signed it, and then she blotted it and did not blow on it.

> *West ditching, second week. Entry in the county copy corrected to the person named in the district roll. Source: district roll, page fifty-one, the ninth year, entry two. The county copy carried a body in a column headed for the person. A body is not a party, cannot be instructed, cannot stop, and cannot be paid. Corrected by hand by the undersigned, provincial record office, on the fourth day of the fourth week of the ninth month of next year. A. Pell.*

"There," she said. "That is the whole of the act and it took nine minutes and a quiet afternoon and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a person may not do it, and if there were one I would have had to fill it in, and there is not one, and I have been looking for about nine years."

She put the pen down and did not pick it up again.

---

"You have not put a question in it," said Tamsin Rook.

"I have not put a question in it."

"You have put *a body is not a party* in it, in a roll, in a county record, with your name at the foot of it, in a hand anybody can compare against your hand for the next forty years."

"Yes. "She said it without any weight at all. "And I have done it nine times in about nine years in nine counties and every one of them is a line in a margin with a day on it and my name on it, and that is the whole of my method, and it is the only thing I have ever been proud of, and it is not a crime and it does not make me a good person, and I would like both of those understood before anybody in this room says anything kind about it."

---

Marek Kest had been standing by the door with his hands behind him for about four minutes.

"A question," he said.

"Ask one. You have got one. Everybody who comes into a back room with a lamp on has got one, and I have been in nine counties and it is always the same shape and it is always about somebody's family. "She got the box off the table and set it on the floor. "Ask it and I will tell you whether I can answer it from a roll. That is the only thing I can do. I am not a person who can do anything else and I am not going to pretend to be one for four hours."

He asked it.

She answered it, in about nine minutes, with the roll open, and the answer was that a person can be put into a column of a roll by hand and a person can be taken out of it by hand, and that the handwriting can be compared for as long as there is a hand to compare, and that nobody compares it because comparing is work and the work is not anybody's job, and that there are about nine hundred and forty names in a book in that district and about four hundred of them are dead and the rest of them are alive and about two hundred and forty people in that district have never known there is a sixth space at the bottom of the page with nothing over it.

He asked the second one.

"No," said Aurelia Pell.

"You did not hear it."

"I heard it and I have decided in about four seconds that I am not answering it, and I am going to tell you why, and the why is the same shape as the first one and you will not like it either. "She shut the roll. "A provincial archivist who answers a stranger's question about a roll is a provincial archivist who has put a stranger's question into a roll. And there is no form anywhere for taking a question back out of a record. There is one for taking a person's name out of a box at a notary's table and there is none at all for taking a question out of a page, and I have looked, and I looked for about nine years, and I am telling you the size of the looking."

"You have not met me before today."

"I have not met you before today and I am not going to. "She picked the pen up and put it in the box, and closed the box, and did it in that order. "I am going to say my name once, because a person who will not give their name is a person asking for something, and I am not asking for anything, and I have four days of lifting left and a cart to catch and no interest in this."

"Once," said Tamsin Rook.

"Once. It is the name on the correction. You can read it there, which is a thing I have just done on purpose, and you can compare it against my hand for the next forty years, and that is more than anybody else in this matter has ever offered me. "She got the box up off the floor. "**And I am not going to be thanked and I would like it said in advance so that nobody starts, because a thing done for nothing and thanked for is bought on the way out, and I have watched nine people get bought that way in nine years.**"

---

Nobody thanked her.

"Can I see the card," said Tamsin Rook.

"You can see the card."

She got it out of the box and put it on the table, and it was a strip of card about the length of a hand with eleven entries on it in her own hand and the days against them, and ten of the eleven were buildings and one was a man.

"**The first is a courthouse and the third is a courthouse and the fifth is a store and the seventh is a courthouse, and eight of these eleven are rooms with a door and a person in charge of it who cannot say no, because a person in charge of a store cannot say no to the person who lifts.**" She put her thumb on the tenth. "This one is a man in a house at the end of a lane and he can say no, and he did, to his face, in front of his wife, and I wrote his no down because I have to, and then I corrected his name into the column where it belongs because the column is the column."

"So that is the hard stop."

"**That is the only stop on that card anybody can turn me away from, and the other ten are buildings, and a roll is kept in buildings, and that is the whole of why this country can be corrected in nine minutes by anybody and cannot be corrected at all in a house where a man says no.**" She put the card back in the box and shut it. "You have been four days on a road about four hundred and thirty miles long to get to a room with a table in it. So have I, and I came in a cart, and my office paid for the cart, and I have never once been on that towpath and I have never once been to a lock, and I am telling you that because you have been looking at me as though I were a door and I am a cart."

---

She went out into the corridor with the box on her hip and the lamp in her hand, and she was gone in about nine minutes, and the three of them were in a back room on a flood terrace with a roll open on a table and a margin in it in a hand anybody can compare.

And the whole of what she had given them was not an answer. It was a method, and a day, and a name at the foot of a line in a county record nine days' cart travel from here.

**A person can be put into a column of a roll by hand and taken out of it by hand, and the handwriting can be compared for as long as there is a hand, and nobody compares it, and it will be exactly as true in nine years as it is this evening, and nobody has ever found out who did a particular one because it was ordinary.**

**And a question in a margin, with a day on it, and a name at the foot of it, and a body named in a column that is headed for a person, and the person is alive and is forty-four and does not want to be in a roll and told a stranger so in front of his wife, and the stranger wrote it down anyway, in the correct column, in the correct hand, and that is the whole of what the record is for.**

And nobody in this matter is now an ally of anybody, and the same surname is not a door into anything, and a daughter and a father are in this river district on the same day and neither of them has looked for the other.
