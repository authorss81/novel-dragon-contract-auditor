# Chapter 160: Two Names That Are Not the Clerk

They came up the road in the third week of the seventh month of next year and it took six days, and it is the third time, and the road is the same road, and people in three districts have offered to shorten it and none of them can.

The application had been in a bag in a coat for about seven weeks and nobody had opened it and it was not sealed because it was never sealed, and on the last week of the seventh month of next year it went back onto a slab of oak at the end of a passage, past the estates counter, past a door that has been propped open with a quill since somebody's time.

---

"Three boxes," said Marn Ottery.

"Three boxes," said Marek Kest.

"One and two are yours and they are dated and I have read them and they are true, and the second one says the run number and the row and the fourpence and the fact that there is no provision for anybody else and there never has been, and that is a correct description of this office and I have signed underneath it because somebody had to."

Box one: his name, and the room he sits in.

Box two: the run, the row, the fourpence, the eleven weeks, the absence of a provision.

Box three: **the person the instrument touches.** A name in a copyist's hand that everybody in three galleries can tell from anybody else's, and a day, and nothing under it, and no signature, because a party does not sign the box that makes her a party, she fills it, and everybody who has ever done it anywhere in this empire has filled it and about half of them have signed it and neither of those is a rule.

"No," said Marn Ottery. "**You are not signing box one and neither of you is signing box three, and I am not going to ask either of you to, and I want that said out loud in this room because in about four years somebody is going to write a sentence about this form and get it wrong.**"

---

She read it back.

She read it twice, the second reading slower.

She has read a thing back twice, the second reading slower, about nine hundred times in eleven years at that counter and she has never once said where she got it, and **a girl of seventeen four hundred and thirty miles down this river has been doing it in a bay for about a season, out loud, in the cold, and I have been asked twice in four days where I got it and I have not told anybody twice, and neither of those two things is going to change because of this morning, and that is what a rule is.**

Nobody thanked anybody for it. Nobody is going to.

---

Then she got the day-book out from under the till and opened it at the second hour of the last week of the seventh month of next year and wrote in it, in her own hand:

> *Application, three ruled boxes. Put up by M. Kest, no office named, no register, no certification, bar discharged. Third box filled by T. Rook, copyist, of this city and of the county of the lower district, address as box one.*

And the day. And her name at the foot of it.

"That is the first name I have entered in that book in eleven years," she said, closing it. "The one above it is nineteen years old, it is a refusal, it was put there by a man who is dead, and I have read it about four hundred times and I am not going to tell you what it says."

"You are entering ours," said Tamsin Rook, "and ours is not a refusal."

"No. Ours is an application, and an application is the only thing I have ever entered here that was not somebody else's paper. In eleven years this office has certified what an instrument is, and what a book is and what is written in it, and what a party is, and what a document does, and it has refused two questions, and it has sold more than a hundred thousand blanks at a shilling apiece in a hundred and forty years, and the four hundred that came back to the counter are under it, and it has never found out who brings them. And it has never once had a person walk up to this counter and ask to be a party to something."

---

"I am not going to fill the third box, and I am also not going to un-fill it, and I want the reason said out loud and in this order, because there are two of you and only one of me and one of you is a party and one of you is not, and the reason is the same in both directions and I would like it entered while I am saying it."

"Go on."

"**A box at this table gets filled with a person who has been asked.** That is the whole of the rule and there is no second half of it. Not: a person who has been asked whether they will answer. **Asked.** A person asked, and who answered, and the answer and the day are both in it somewhere.

"And where nobody has been asked, the box stays empty. **And an empty box on a form at this counter is the only honest thing that has ever been on one, and I am not going to fill one out of a clerk's own common sense at about the fourth hour of the last week of the seventh month of next year.**

"And I am going to say the other half of that out loud as well, because I have been right about it for about nine minutes and I have just broken it. **The woman who filled that box was not asked by this office. She asked herself. And there is no box on that sheet for a person who was not asked, and there is no line for it, and the reason there is no line for it is that this table has never once had a person walk up to it and ask to be a party, and a design that has never been asked for has never had to hold one.** And I have had a form on this counter with three ruled boxes and a strip of paper gummed over the only line in it that mattered, and I am not going to put a person's name in a box out of my own head, and I am not going to take one out of one either, and that is the whole of what a clerk may do at about the fourth hour and it is very little and it is all of it."

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds.

"You could fill it," said Marek Kest. "There are about four hundred forms in that box under you and about a hundred and forty of them have the answer on them and eleven of them have a second name as well, underneath, where a person signs."

"**I could, and every one of the eleven had no more right to than I have, and not one of them was asked, and I have had three weeks to find one of them who was, and I have not found him. And the woman standing at this counter is the twelfth, and I entered her, and I did not find her, and I have not got anything here that is better than that except a wage and a slab of oak.**"

---

"And now the price of it," said Marn Ottery, "and I am going to say it before either of you can, because I have watched about nine hundred people find this out at about four seconds each and the four seconds is the part that stays with me."

"The price is that the two of you are now findable about this instrument. Not about the bank. Not about the water. About this, in a book in a building in this city with a date on it and my name at the foot of it, and a copy at fourpence, and in about four years anybody with fourpence can read it. And there is no form in this empire for un-writing a name out of a box, and I have looked, and I looked in about four minutes standing at this counter while you were both at the estates counter, and I did not look in every place, and I am telling you the size of the looking."

"That is the price and it is the whole of it," said Tamsin Rook.

"That is the first of it." Marn Ottery put both hands flat on the oak. "**The second is that the price of a door is the door. I have been the door in this room for eleven years and I have never once asked what the door was for, and I have said that out loud to an empty counter twice in about three weeks, and I have found out, and I am going to tell you what it was for, because a clerk of thirty-four is not going to be the only person in this building who has been keeping a thing to herself.**

"It was for this.

"Not for the shilling. Not for the fourpence. For the person who walks up a counter with a piece of paper and a face that has decided what is in it, and who goes away and comes back, and who is on a road four hundred and thirty miles long about eleven weeks later with a coat that has cost him three pounds out of about forty-four pounds a year, and there is no fund for it and there is no form for it. A door is not a thing you want anybody to notice. A door is a thing that has to be there, and the price of a door is that you can never shut it, and I have not shut this one since the day I got here, and I have been the reason that nothing wrong has happened in this room for eleven years, and I have been the reason and I have been the price and those are the same person and there was never a version of this where I got to be one without the other.

"So do not thank me. I want it said first, before anybody starts, and I have said it, and I have found out in three weeks what eleven years of that counter was for and it was for a form with three boxes on it and it cost me fourpence that I paid out of my own column and did not put in the fee book, and I am not going to be thanked for fourpence."

---

And then she told them the day.

"I can name it," she said. "The first week of the eighth month of next year. That is when the answer to the question bought in the second week of the fifth month comes, because the room upstairs says about eleven weeks and they have been right about that for a hundred and forty years and they have never once been early. It will come to this counter and I will take it and I will read it out, and if you are not here I will put it in the press and you can have it for fourpence whenever you like for the rest of your life.

"And here is the thing I have been not saying since the second week of the fifth month, and I am saying it now and it is the last thing I am going to say at this counter for a while.

"**That is the same week that a bank four hundred and thirty miles down this river has to decide something about about forty-one people.**

"**And there is no form in this empire that puts two dates in one box.** I have looked for one. It is not a matter of money and it is not a matter of a court and it is not a matter of a House or a guild or a dragon, and a woman of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph fixed a day about nine weeks ago for the purpose of asking a body whether it will accept a duty that stops, and the day was fixed, and about forty-one people answered yes with a day against them, and about nineteen said no with reasons and are not on anything, and about four people who cannot read a paragraph were never asked and never will be.

"And the answer to your question comes down a road in the same week that the review of that comes round, and a person cannot be in two places and a form cannot be in two boxes, and people in three districts have offered me a way to shorten a road and not one of them can do it, and one of the nine is right about the money and none of the nine is right about the rest.

"That is the finding and it is eleven days old and I have not put it anywhere, and I am not going to, and I would like somebody to find out what to do about it and I would like it not to be me."

---

At about the sixth hour they went, down the passage past the estates counter and out into the evening, and she watched them as far as the propped door, and it is the fourth time she has watched a person go as far as that door and it is the first time she has watched two.

And then she stood at the counter alone for a good while and did not go and do anything else, which is not how she works.

---

And here is what the last week of the seventh month of next year leaves standing, in the plainest words there are, and none of it is a question and none of it is answered by anybody in this book yet.

A form with three ruled boxes on a slab of oak at the end of a passage, and **two of them filled with the names of two people who are not the clerk**, in two hands, in about sixty words between them, and one of those hands is a copyist's hand that everybody in three galleries can tell from anybody else's.

**And the answer at the foot of the sheet with a line for the day on it and nothing for anything else, and the answer not yet come, and the space not filled, and an unfilled space on a form at that counter is worth more than a filled one.**

A copy at fourpence in a bag in a city four hundred and thirty miles down a river. A day-book in a building with a plate-glass front with two names in it and a date and a clerk's name at the foot of it, which is the first time in eleven years a person's name has gone into that book on her own motion, and the first time in nineteen years that it was not a refusal.

A strip of printed paper about two inches by one, gummed to the foot of about nine hundred forms a year, with the only line on the sheet that changes what a person is printed on it, and about six hundred of a slip in a press in the back room that says the fourth line is not to be completed by this office, and no knife on the counter.

A register of runs on a high shelf with about thirty rows in one run that are not instruments at all, and a column headed *by whose authority*, and one name in it, and no day, because a clerk would not read a day aloud in a public room and said out loud why.

A copy of that line, on ninepence of a carrier's time, at a lock, in the inside of a coat, going up a towpath to a clerk's window at a court on a flood terrace who has worked out the same thing by accident and has told nobody, about a foreman four miles away who threw a heading away twice and was not told.

And a door in the west end of a bay that shuts, and a wedge off a sleeper, and one day in a hard-backed ledger with three columns and no heading over the third of them.

And a question, asked by a person of seventeen who is paid forty-five pence a day to read out loud to people who cannot read it, written on the back of a leaf in a satchel with the day on it: and who is the cheapest person in this matter to put in a box. And nine minutes in a bay, and a man standing in it, and nobody followed it, and a woman of twenty-four who would like to know what it is like for about eleven weeks and has said so in a city four hundred and thirty miles up a road and has not said so to anybody who can write it down.

And a guarantee standing offered and unanswered at no charge on a bank about four hundred and forty foot long with about sixty children under sixteen inside it, and a bill at Lowcross of nineteen pounds three and fourpence that nobody is liable for, and about nineteen people behind that bank who are on nothing, and about four who were never asked, and a reader who is not thanked and is not going to start.

**And two dates in one week, four hundred and thirty miles apart, and no form in this empire that puts them in one box.**
