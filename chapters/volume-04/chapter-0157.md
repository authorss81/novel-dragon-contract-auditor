# Chapter 157: A Name in a Hand

She recognised the hand in about four seconds and then had about nine minutes of work to do, and the four seconds were free and the nine minutes were the whole of it, and she has said since that the four seconds were worth nothing and the nine minutes were worth a wage, and that a person who is paid a copyist's rate for four years to tell whose hand a thing is in has been given a skill and has never once been told what it is for, and that she found out what it was for at a bench in a hired bay in the third week of the sixth month of next year, and that it was for a room with about nine people in it.

Nobody asked her. She had not intended to. She had the column of foolscap propped against a beer jug on a bench and about four people still in the bay and the rain going on the roof, and the column is five ruled lines in a clerk's own hand, and the name is in the fifth one.

"Read it again," said Marek Kest.

"No."

"That is my coat's contents and it cost ninepence to a carrier at a lock."

"It is a copy of a line out of a register that a clerk of thirty-four copied out on her own time over two days and would not read a day out of aloud in a public room, and she has told nobody in this empire that she did it, and you have just said her name in a bay and there are about four people in it." Tamsin Rook put her finger on the fifth line without touching the ink. "I have read the name once, out loud, and I am not going to read it again, and that is not caution and it is not a rule. It is that a name read twice in a room is a name the room has, and people in this bay can now find that name in a city four hundred and thirty miles up a road, and I have spent fourteen months making people findable and I know exactly what that costs and it is not nothing."

"Then say what the hand is."

She turned the sheet about so that the four of them could see the shape of it and did not put her own name to it and did not name the man whose it was either, and that is the first thing and it is deliberate and she said so.

"**It is a hand that has been writing for about nineteen years and has never once had to hurry, and it is a hand that was taught in this city, and there is a handful of people in three galleries who can tell it and I am one of them and that is a skill and it is not a power and nobody has ever asked me for it.**"

---

"Right. Nine minutes. You may check every word of this against that column and I have got the column here, and it is a fact and not advice, and I would like somebody to write it down in a book with no heading, and not in that one, because that one is for work and a person who puts a judgement in a book that four hundred dead people are written in is doing a thing I would not do."

Nobody wrote it down.

"One. The column is headed *by whose authority*. That is a heading and it has been a heading for a hundred and forty years and it is the only reason that register has ever been allowed to keep a person's name in it at all, and a name under that heading is an office being discharged and is not an admission of anything."

"That is what he says," said Marek Kest.

"**It is what he has said out loud three times in three forms, and he has been right three times, and I have read all three, and I am not going to say the fourth thing I have got and I am not going to be asked to.**" She did not look at him. "So: that sentence is true. And it is also what a register of that kind has been for a hundred and forty years. Both of those are true at once and the second one is not the first one and the second one is the one nobody has ever said."

"Go on."

"Two. About thirty rows in that run are not instruments. About thirty rows in a register of scale instruments are a name being taken off a roll, because you take a name off a roll with an impression and an impression takes a number. And there is no column in that book which says which is which, and there is not going to be one, because the fourth counter does not get asked, and I have now got two separate people in this matter who have said the same four words in two different trades and neither of them knew about the other."

"A clerk of a court on the flood terrace wrote a thing like it in the sixth month of last year," said Rennick Adley from the other end of the bench. He had not said anything for eleven minutes. "And she wrote that a foreman four miles from here arrived at the same thing by accident about nine months before that and threw a heading away twice, and nobody told the foreman, and the clerk said that that is the whole of what is wrong with the last two years of her own life. And I have read that. It is in a column with her hand against every line of it."

"And nobody has told the foreman. I am not going to say whether anybody should and I am not going to say whose life it is about. I am saying that two trades, about four hundred and thirty miles apart, in about eleven months, arrived at the same thing by accident, and neither of them knows the other exists, and that is the whole of what is wrong with both of them and neither of them can find out and neither of them is going to be told."

---

"Three," said Tamsin Rook, and she turned the sheet about again and put her thumb under the heading so that nobody could see the name, and it was about the fifth hour of the second hour of the afternoon and half the bay had come in and stayed.

"**A heading over a column is a machine.** A column with no heading over it is the same book with the machine taken out. That is not a figure of speech and I am not being clever, it is the finding and it is a clerk's finding and it took her eleven months and a press and a box under a counter.

"And the machine does one thing. **It takes a sentence and it puts a name at the end of it.** A name under a heading is a name that has been put in a book to discharge somebody's office. And then three things are true at once: the book is true, the office is discharged, and the person named has admitted nothing.

"**And a person who has admitted nothing can be asked about nothing.**"

That is the part they all came back to afterwards and there is no room in this matter where four people come back to a sentence.

"So the column is not a protection for the man whose hand it is, and it has never been, and he knows that, and it is the only defence he has got and he is right to use it. And the column is a machine for putting a name in a book so that the name can never be asked anything, and that is not a side effect. That is what it is for. And the reason it has worked for a hundred and forty years is that the name is in the book, and the office is discharged, and nobody has any standing to say to a man that a name in a book is a person."

"Then what is it for, finally," said a foreman of fifty-one from the door.

"**For making a person in a book into a person nobody has to answer for.** And the cheaper the person is, the better it works."

---

She said the fourth thing at about the fourth minute of the ninth and she said it to the room and not to the column and not to Marek Kest, and the whole bay heard all of it, and it is on no page and it is findable on a bench in a hired bay four hundred yards from a ford and it is worth nothing at law.

"Then here is the answer to the question nobody in this matter has asked, and I have asked it, and I am going to be asked why I asked it, and it is because I read to about nine people a week who cannot read a paragraph and I am paid forty-five pence a day for it and I have a copy of the engagement in a satchel and I have a term and a notice.

"**A blank heir is not a safe person. A blank heir is a cheap person.**

"**A body cannot be made a party, cannot be instructed and cannot stop, and a person with a wage can be made to answer for a thing, and a person with nothing behind them can be made to answer for nothing at all, because there is nothing to be liable for and because nobody can serve a person who has nothing to serve.** That is the whole of it. That is why a box at a notary's table wants a person and cannot do without one, and it is not because the form is badly designed, it is because a box with a wage in it can be held and a box with nothing in it cannot.

"**And the cheapest party in this matter is a man of thirty-eight with a discharged bar and three pounds eleven and threepence of his own wage gone nineteen days at a time, and he is in this bay, and it is not because he is honest and it is not because he is nobody's, it is because there is nothing behind him to be liable for and nobody can serve a person who has nothing to serve.**

"And that is not a discovery about him. That is a discovery about the price of him. And somebody worked that out, a long time ago, and built it into a machine, and the machine is a strip of paper about two inches by one gummed to the foot of a form so that the only line on the form that matters cannot be got at without damaging the sheet, and the only names that office has ever got out of the whole of it are eleven, and it got them by asking a person to stand in front of a body, and every one of the eleven was in a hurry and every one of the eleven was going to be sent for if they had not been."

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds and then the foreman of fifty-one said, "Then the gum is the offer."

"**The gum is the offer,**" said Tamsin Rook. "**And there is no box anywhere in this empire for a no, and a thing nobody is offered is a thing nobody can do, and eleven is not a small number and it is not a conspiracy and there is nobody to be angry at, and that is what makes it a hundred and forty years long.**"

---

And then she stopped, and the foreman of fifty-one asked her the obvious thing, which is whether she had wanted the room to know, and she answered it, and this is the part of it that is hers and not the column's and that nobody thanked her for and nobody offered to.

"I have been paid a copyist's wage for four years to be able to tell whose hand a thing is in," she said. "And in fourteen months of this I have never once been on the other end of a line of paper of my own, and I have said so at a counter in a city and it was believed in about eleven seconds, and it was true when I said it and it has stopped being true at about a quarter past the second hour of the fourth week of the fifth month of next year, and I did not tell a single person in this bay that I had said it, and I am not going to now.

"So I wanted them to know. And that is the whole of the wanting and I am not going to dress it up as a service to anybody, and I have watched four people this year do exactly that with something they wanted and call it a finding.

"And I cannot tell you why I wanted it. **And I am not going to pretend to a room that I can.**"

"That is the best thing anybody has said in this bay," said Doreth Ferrow, who cannot read a paragraph and had it read to her twice, the second reading slower.

"It is not a finding at all," said Tamsin Rook, "and I would like the two words *cheapest* and *party* left out of anything anybody writes down, and I would like that asked of the chair, because the chair is the person in this matter who has never once put a judgement in a book."

"I will not put a judgement in a book," said the chair of sixty-four. "I will put the finding in it. **The finding is that a name in a heading cannot be asked anything, and that this bay now knows it, and that a clerk at a court on a flood terrace four hundred and thirty miles down this river knew it eleven months ago and told nobody, and that nobody has told anybody in either place, and that I have been a chair for nineteen years and this is the first finding I have had handed to me by a person who got it from a stranger's handwriting.**"

---

And what was left standing at the end of the third week of the sixth month of next year:

A copy of one line of a register of runs, in a clerk's own hand, with a number of four figures and no letters in the first ruled space, a device that is a mark and is not a name in the third, and a name in the fifth, under a heading that is a heading and not a charge, and no day in it, because a clerk of thirty-four refused to read a day aloud in a public room and said out loud why.

A finding, in about nine minutes, in a bay, that a heading over a column is a machine and that a column with no heading over it is the same book with the machine taken out, and that two trades about four hundred and thirty miles apart arrived at it by accident in about eleven months and neither of them knows the other exists.

And the question, asked by a person of seventeen who is paid forty-five pence a day to read out loud to people who cannot read it, asked in a bay and not in a form, with no instrument and no answer: who is the cheapest person in this matter to put in a box.

**And a man standing in the bay, who is it.**
