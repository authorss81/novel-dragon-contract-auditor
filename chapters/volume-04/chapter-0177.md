# Chapter 177: The Only Person Who Can Ask

The fifth line of a form that has been printed for about a hundred and forty years was done at about the second hour of the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after, in a house at the end of a lane about four miles off the old river road, and it took about nine minutes, and nothing whatever happened.

Marn Ottery said that out loud to herself on the way there, at about the seventh hour of the previous day, on the last stretch of a road she had not been on in eleven years, and she said it because she wanted to be certain that she was not going to be disappointed and because a person who is disappointed is a person who was expecting something, and a clerk of thirty-four at a counter in a city about four hundred and thirty miles up this river has learned in eleven years that the whole of her trade is not being disappointed by a document.

She is thirty-four. She has been at the fourth counter of the Notaries' Table for eleven years and she is not a lawyer and she cannot leave the counter and does most of the work of about four hundred people who can.

**She came down because a letter is a thing a person can be shown.** She said that out loud to the man who sent it before she had decided to come, and she said it in a room, in words, and she has not gone back on it and she is not going to.

The price of coming: a counter with nobody at it does not open. There is nobody to cover it and there is no form for covering it. **She has had four days at that counter shut and about four days of her own wage**, and a day of her wage is between fourpence and a shilling for a reading and rather more for a day. She is not going to work the total out in a lane, because a total is a thing a clerk does once at a desk and not four times in fields.

She has not told the fourth counter she is going to be back. She wrote a note and left it with a girl of about twenty-five who does the intake and who wrote a file number in ink and initialled it four times in one year for a man who never once thanked her.

---

She had copied the form out at the counter at about the eleventh hour on the last day before she left, the whole of it, five lines and a heading, because that is what she does. The five lines are on a sheet of copyist's paper in her satchel. She does not look at them once before she leaves, and the reason is that she is a copyist, and a copyist does not work off her own hand. A copyist works off the original.

> *Five. The second person asks the person, before the thing is said, whether she is a party to it, and the answer is written here.*

"I am not going to do this from the paper," she said. "If I do it from the paper then the paper is the form and the form is in a lane and I will have been the office. **I am going to do it off the top sheet, with my hand on the book, and the room is going to be this room.**"

---

The room was a kitchen with a table, two chairs and a bench, a hearth with a bundle of kindling on it, a lamp, and a window about two hands wide with a shutter that does not shut.

Bess Tarrant was in it. She had not asked for any of it to happen and she had not been asked for any of it and she was sixty-three and she had a bundle of kindling to fetch and she fetched it while they were arranging the room and she did it without being told to, and nobody stopped her and nobody said it was a courtesy.

The second person was a girl of about nineteen who came to the door for a bundle of kindling and was in the room a few minutes because there was nowhere to put the kindling.

She was not asked her name. She did not give it. She was not thanked and she did not expect to be, and there is no form anywhere that says she was ever there, and she has a bundle of kindling and a story about a room in a house about four miles off a road, and she is not going to be a character in anything.

**A second person in a room and not a party to the thing.** She was nineteen and she is not a party to a dead woman's name and neither is the woman of sixty-three and neither is the man of thirty-eight standing by the shutter, because he is a party to the same instrument and there are two boxes on one form with their names in them and nobody has signed either.

Which leaves the clerk.

---

"I am going to say the price out loud before I do it," said Marn Ottery, "and I am going to say all of it, and I am going to say it to the room and not to anybody in particular, and if I do not say all of it then somebody in this room will say all of it for me in about four years and it will be wrong."

Nobody said anything.

"**I am a party to an instrument about a dead woman's name. From this hour I will be served anything said in this room.** I cannot be a witness and I was never going to be one, and I am saying it in the room so that nobody can say afterwards that I did not know."

She put the pen down.

"And I am not going to be thanked. I have said that at a counter in a city about four hundred and thirty miles up this river and I meant it then and I mean it now, and if anybody thanks me I will get up and go out into the lane, and I have not got a hat and it is raining and I will still do it."

Nobody thanked her.

---

"Then I am going to ask her," said Marn Ottery, "and the second person is going to write it down, and I am going to be the one who asks, and the whole of the reason I am the one who asks is the reason the fifth line has never been done, and I am going to say that out loud as well because it is the finding and it is mine and I am not going to be the person who found a thing out and then did it quietly."

She turned round to the girl of nineteen.

"Are you a party to any of this."

The girl said, "To any of what."

"To the thing that is being said in this room. In this lane. About a book on a bench in a house with mortar in the door. **Have you ever been named in a paper, or signed one, or had one served on you**, or had a name of yours written down anywhere as a person somebody could be served about?"

"No," said the girl. "I have a bundle of kindling."

"That is a no," said Marn Ottery. "Write that down, and write the day, in your own hand, and put it on that line. Write what I asked and write what you said and do not write what either of us thinks about it."

The girl of nineteen wrote it down on the top sheet, in the fifth line, in pencil, in a hand that was not the office's hand and was not the hand of the woman of sixty-three and had never in its life been near a form.

---

"And now you," said Marn Ottery, to Bess Tarrant. "The same question, in the same words, and you answer it in the ordinary way and if the answer takes a moment then it takes a moment."

"I have never been in anything," said Bess Tarrant. "Not a paper, not a form, not a court. I kept a house and I wrote things down for people who came to the kitchen and I have never been asked to be anything in my life, and if somebody has told you otherwise then somebody has been wrong about me, and I should like to know who."

"Nobody has told me anything about you," said Marn Ottery. "Write it down. Both of you. The day and the words and nothing else."

---

Then the woman of sixty-three said the thing.

She said it in her own order and not in the order it was written in, because it was years old in her own head and she had been carrying it about without writing it, which is the only way anybody ever carries a thing.

She said: a woman stood up in a room, and there was a boy in the room, and the woman said the boy was to be put down as holding nothing, and the woman said nobody was to be asked the family anything, and the woman was not asked where it came from.

The girl of nineteen wrote it down. All of it. In pencil. About eleven lines.

And the girl of nineteen did not read it back to her, because the fourth line says the second person does not read it back to the person and does not show it to them, and the girl of nineteen had not been told that, and Marn Ottery told her, at that point, in the room, out loud, and the girl said, "Why not."

"Because that is the fourth line and the fourth line is the cruellest thing anybody ever printed in about nine hundred buildings, and I am not going to argue with it in a kitchen, and I am not going to quietly break it either, because I have spent eleven years finding out that breaking a printed rule quietly is how you end up with a register that says something nobody checked."

---

And then Marn Ottery read it back, twice, the second reading slower, to a woman of sixty-three in her own kitchen, and the girl of nineteen watched her do it and did not understand where it came from and has not since.

"It is not what I remember," said Bess Tarrant, the second time.

"That is the correct answer and it is the answer I gave you in the house and I am giving it again," said Marn Ottery. "**A person remembering a thing and a person reading a thing back are two different acts and only one of them is evidence.** And you have now got both of them in one room on the same afternoon and they do not agree, and that is not a fault in either of them and it is not going to be settled by anybody and there is no form for it."

---

"It is done," she said afterwards, at about the fourth hour, with the lamp still on. "I want to be clear with everybody in this room about what has happened, because I have been thinking about it for a day and a half on a road and I have reached the point where I am frightened of it and I would rather say so than be impressive about it.

"**It has been done and it is exactly as difficult as it always was and it took about nine minutes. That is the entire finding.** There is no door in that door. There was never a door in it. A person asks a question and a person answers it and a quarter of an hour goes by, and it has been available to about nine hundred offices in this empire for a hundred and forty years, and nobody has done it because the person who was entitled to do it was the one person who could not be told what to ask."

"And tomorrow," said Bess Tarrant, "what happens tomorrow."

"**Nothing.**" She said it flatly. "Nothing at all. That is the other half and I am not going to let the room have the first half without the second half. It is done once, in a kitchen, by three women and a girl with kindling, and it is written in pencil on a sheet in a house about four miles off a road, and the register on the bench in the other room still says what it has said for a hundred and forty years, in nine hands, in the office's name, in every line."

"So it did not do anything."

"It did one thing and it is not a small one and I am not going to pretend otherwise." Marn Ottery put the pen down. "**A person can be put into a column of a roll by hand and taken out by hand, and there is nothing in this empire that stops anybody doing it, and that is not a flaw in a record. That is what a record is.** I had that given to me for nothing in a back room on a flood terrace in the third week of the ninth month of next year by a provincial archivist who is not in this lane and does not know I am in it and is not a friend of anybody in this matter. It is true and it will be exactly as true in nine years as it is this morning.

"And this is what I have proved by doing the fifth line, and I have thought about it for a day and a half and this is the only thing I have got. **Nobody took anybody out of that register. Nobody has ever taken anybody out of that register.** Somebody simply never put the right thing in, in about nine hundred buildings, for a hundred and forty years, out of habit. It does not take a villain and it does not take a conspiracy and it does not take an office and it does not take anybody at all. It takes a second person at about the ninth hour who is copying a sheet and who does not know that there is a difference."

"And when does it stop," said Marek Kest.

"It does not stop," said Marn Ottery. "What has happened this morning is that in about four years there will be a person in a room who can stand up and say that the fifth line exists, that it is printed, that it is a question, and that it was done once, in a kitchen, with a date on it, and a hand anybody can compare against for the next forty years. **That is not a machine stopping. That is a person being findable**, and a person being findable is worth more than a machine stopping and it is a completely different thing. I am a clerk and I know the difference between the two, because I make about nine hundred of the first kind of thing a year and none of the second."

---

Bess Tarrant went for her kindling at about the fifth hour and came back with it and put it on the hearth and did not ask whether she was wanted and was wanted.

The girl of nineteen took her bundle and went.

The lamp was still on the table and the sheet was on the table with the fifth line filled in twice, in two hands, in pencil, and Marn Ottery looked at it for a while and then did not take it and did not copy it, and the reason she gave, in the room, to nobody, in the ordinary way, is the one she has given at a counter four times in about four weeks.

"A provincial archivist who answers a stranger's question about a roll has put a stranger's question into a roll, and there is no form anywhere for taking a question back out of a record. **I have not answered a question. I have asked one, in a room, with a witness in it**, and the witness is nineteen and has a bundle of kindling and her name is not in anything."

And the man of thirty-eight, at the door, in the rain, in the lane, said the four words that were the whole of what he had written in his own book about this house, and he said them out loud to a woman of thirty-four, and she wrote them down and dated them and did not read them out.
