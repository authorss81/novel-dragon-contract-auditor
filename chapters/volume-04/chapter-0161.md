# Chapter 161: The Name Against the Date

The day came off the wall of the north bay the way a nail comes off a wall when nobody has touched it for ninety days, which is to say it did not come off at all. It was on a sheet with the list of works on it and it was at the foot of the sheet, and it was against Halla Wray's name, and she had written the name herself on the seventh of the first month of next year in a hand she still recognised and did not like.

She is fifty-one. She is the foreman. She is second on the list of about forty-one people, which is a thing she has never been able to explain to anybody and which is not a demotion. She has undone four months of her own work with her own second idea and about forty people can now find where she is on any given day, and she had believed, up to the first week of the eighth month, that this was the same as being known.

It is not the same. **A name at the foot of a list of works is a signature.** A signature is a promise. A promise has a day.

---

She had known the day was coming since the second of the fifth month of next year, because the second of the fifth month of next year is the day the season began and a season is about ninety days and ninety days from the second of the fifth month is a day in the first week of the eighth month, and no person had put that in a sentence. It had arrived by arithmetic. Arithmetic is not a notice.

So she went and asked a court to fix it, which is the first thing she did and the last thing she will ever do, and the court sent back a form with a reason written in it and no box anywhere on the form for a why.

Rennick Adley had it before she had put it in the drawer in the tool shed. He writes things down. He had it in his own hand inside four minutes, and when she told him what it said he wrote nine lines, and the ninth of them is this, and she made him read it back to her, which she had not asked him to do: *a form came back with a reason in it and no box for a why.*

"**That is a resolution of a kind and it is findable,** and being right is what makes it a document, and there is no way in this empire to un-ask, and I would rather it had come back with nothing on it."

"It came back with a reason in it and no why," said Halla Wray. "That is the whole of what I am going to say about it in a bay, and if anybody asks me in a room that is the sentence they get, because the rest of it is a sentence about a sheet of paper that is not an instrument, and saying any more of it out loud in here is how a person starts being asked about it by people who were not asking."

---

The seat would not pay it either, and she had known that too and had gone and looked anyway, because a woman of fifty-one who has been a foreman for a long time will go and look at a thing twice before she says it is not there.

Sefa Ild was at the west door with a lamp she was not using.

"The district's flood authority for that reach was given to two persons in the first month of next year," she said. "The first of them put a name at the foot of the list of works in her own hand. **The second of them was entered by the keeper without asking him, on purpose, and wrote on the face of her own sheet that she had done it without asking, and he refused it in front of about forty people in nine words and was right.**"

"So the seat is a piece of paper that says two people may do a duty and may not order anybody to do it. It has no money. It has no fund. It has no line."

"I have looked for the line." Sefa Ild did not come off the door. "The Company's whole money is four shillings a year and all of it is the rent of the building we are standing in, and I can show you the book, and the book says the same thing in a different hand."

That is the whole of the money there is for a review. Halla Wray stood in the bay and looked at that for about nine seconds and then went and found the reader.

---

Tavi Venn is seventeen. She is on a written engagement beginning on the first of the fourth month of next year with a copy in a satchel and the same notice as everybody else, and she is paid forty-five pence a day, four days a week, and she is not thanked and she is not going to start.

"She has to read it," said Halla Wray.

"To whom."

"To about nine of them. You know which nine. "The foreman put her hands flat on the bench against the north wall, which is a thing Doreth Ferrow does and which she has watched four rooms do and has never once been in one where it was done well. "And you have to read it twice."

"I read everything twice. "Tavi Venn set the satchel down on the bench in the middle, which is the bench that came with the building. "Second reading slower. I know. The second reading slower is not a courtesy, it is the reading. The first one is for the words and the second one is for the people who did not have them the first time."

"I am not paying you for the walking."

"I know you are not paying me for the reading either, and that is a different thing, and I would like it written down that I said the first one out loud before the second one so that nobody can do the arithmetic in the wrong order later. "She got the leaf out. "You are going to owe me two days and I am going to give you them, and the owing is yours and the giving is mine, and nobody is to thank anybody for either of them, and that is not a rule, it is a thing I have decided and you can put it in your book."

Halla Wray wrote it in the bay's own ledger, in the second column, with the day on it, and did not read it out, and did not know why she had not read it out.

---

They read it on the platform at the Reed Cut gate at about the fifth hour on the second day, and on the bank, and at the fourth house at the north of the ford, and at the tool shed, and at the two doors between, and it took the better part of two days and it was the reading and not the walking that cost anybody anything.

There is a girl of eleven on that platform every evening with a pail. She cannot read a paragraph. Nobody has ever given her a reason and nobody is going to.

She was read to. That is the whole of what happened to her, and it is enough for one evening, and she was given no reason and she is owed nothing by anybody on that bank.

A woman of about seventy-one lives at the fourth house at the north of the ford and cannot read a paragraph and asked in writing to be asked at her own door four times a year exactly as she asked it, and she was asked, and she said the same thing she has said twice before, which is that she is not going to say it a third time. Nobody asked her a third time.

---

About thirty-one people came.

That is the number and it is not a good one and nobody on that bank improved it. **About forty-one said yes with a day against them and a day against the day they were asked.** About nine of those forty-one did not come, and nobody in that bay knows which nine, and there is no column for it on any sheet in ninety-one households, and there is nobody who is paid to go and find out.

Halla Wray stood in the middle of a bay of about thirty people and read the day out one more time herself, badly, at about the sixth hour, and stopped in the middle of it and said, "That is a review and there is no word on any of this that says review, and I am going to say the rest of it and it is going to be short."

Nobody stopped her.

"It is not a hearing. "She looked round the bay. "**There is nobody to hear anything.** No assessor. No chair. No minute. No form. The only question on this sheet is whether a person is in the room this morning, and the only evidence that anybody came is a mark in a column in a book, and the mark is made by somebody who was not the person who came."

"By me," said Tavi Venn.

"By you. At forty-five pence a day, four days a week, on a written engagement with a copy in that satchel and the same notice as everybody else. "The foreman took her hands off the bench. "**A reader who is paid can be told to stop.** I want that said in this room and I want it said by the only person in it who is paid to do this."

"You are not telling me to stop."

"No. I am saying it can be done and I am saying it in front of about thirty people, and I would rather it was said by you than found out about by a stranger in about nine years. "The foreman stopped there, because she had said the thing and she is not a woman who says a thing three times. "**And a person whose attendance is written down by somebody else is a person whose attendance can be written down wrongly by somebody else, and there is no column anywhere on this bank for a person who turns up on the second day and says it was not the first.**"

Nobody wrote it down. About four people in that bay could have.

---

The book-keeper came in at about the half hour with the bay's own ledger under his arm and a slate and a stub of chalk, and put the ledger on the bench in the middle, and it is a hard-backed book the company has kept since before the war and it has three columns and the third one has not had a heading over it since the sixth year.

"You are going to want a column," he said.

"I am."

"There is not one. "He turned the book round so the foreman could see it. "**There are three and the third has no heading, and I have been keeping this book since the second month of next year and I have never once wanted a fourth column, and I have wanted one every day since the second of the fifth.**"

"Then we make one."

"We cannot make one. "He put the chalk down on the bench and did not pick it up again. "**A list of works is a list of jobs with days against them, and a name at the foot of it, and it is the fifth term of a ruling that is posted in two places, and it is the only kind of sentence in this empire that a person can act on the same day.** A list of people who came to a room is not that. There is no term of any ruling anywhere that says anybody may keep one, and there is no column on this bank for it, and if I rule one in this book in my own hand then in about four years there is a page in a book with a heading over it that a man in an office put there, and that man did not stand in this bay this morning."

"So write it on the slate."

The book-keeper looked at the slate for a while.

"That is a thing and not a record," he said. "**A slate is not a book and it is not a page and nobody can look it up in it in nine years, and it is the first thing anybody has ever handed me that was worse on purpose.**"

"Worse on purpose is right," said Sefa Ild, from the west door, where she had been standing since before anybody came in. "**A slate can be washed.** A book cannot. A slate is the only record on this bank that a person can destroy in about nine seconds and it is the only one that anybody here is allowed to destroy, and I would like that written down in whatever it is going to be written down in, because in about four years somebody is going to look at a wet slate and be told it was never written."

"Then it is a slate," said Halla Wray.

"It is a slate, and it is in my hand and not in a column, and it is mine, and I am the only person in ninety-one households who is paid for standing in this room and I am not paid for a slate."

She wrote on it in chalk, at the top of the first line, the day, and then the numbers as she counted them, and the counting took about nine minutes because about four of the people in that bay needed a person to stand next to them while somebody else counted, and nobody helped and nobody was asked.

A woman of about thirty-one at the first house came in at the end of it and had let herself be written down as a description twice, at her own insistence, and the slate says what the sheet says, and she looked at it and said that was right and went home.

The slate went on the nail over the bench in the middle. It is not in any book and it is not a record and it will be washed.

---

A chair of sixty-four was helped onto the bench against the north wall and she cannot read a paragraph and she had the day read to her twice, the second reading slower, and then she asked for it a third time because of one thing in it.

"Which thing."

"The part where you said *the only question on this sheet is whether a person is in the room.*" Doreth Ferrow had the satchel book brought to her and had the leaf turned to. "I want the finding in the book. All of it. In your hand, with the day on it. And I want it in plain words, and I want the two words *cheapest* and *party* out of it, because a book is a place a thing goes to be looked up and I am not putting a judgement in a book."

"The second word is not in the finding," said Rennick Adley.

"Then leave it out anyway," said Doreth Ferrow, "so that whoever comes in here in nine years with a pen does not find it waiting for him."

And it went in the book with no heading with the day on it, in a book-keeper's hand, in plain words, with two words left out on purpose by a woman who cannot read them.

---

And then, at about the seventh hour, with about nine people still in the bay and the light going off the water outside the west door that a woman of twenty-eight packed with a wedge off a sleeper in about nine minutes on her own account and did not read out,

the girl of seventeen asked the question.

She asked it once. She did not explain it. She did not apologise for it and she did not say that anybody could go home.

*And who is going to ask us again.*

She asked it again at about the eighth hour because a foreman of fifty-one at the back had not heard it, and she asked it the same words, and she did not explain it twice.

Nobody answered it in the room. That is what the room did with it.

She wrote it on the back of a leaf in her own satchel book, where she keeps what she cannot read to whom, in her own hand, with the day against each line, and the leaf went into the book and the book went into the satchel, and it is findable, and about four years from now a person with fourpence can have a copy of it out of a book with no office on it.

**And nobody thanked the reader, and she was not thanked, and she is not going to start.**

**A guarantee under the fire-and-water device stands offered and unanswered at no charge on a bank about four hundred and forty foot long, answerable at any time afterwards by a person answering in its terms, and there is no such person and there is not going to be one, and about sixty children under sixteen are inside it, and inside a guild's blank list, and inside a rotation they have not been asked about, and have been told about none of it.**
