# Chapter 153: A Form With Three Boxes

The written application took an afternoon to write and it is three ruled boxes long, and the paper it is on is about a third of a sheet, and Marn Ottery ruled the boxes herself on the back of a blank because the Table does not stock a form for asking a table a question and never has.

One. Who asks. Two. The thing asked about. Three. The person the instrument touches.

"That is the whole of it," she said. "That is three boxes and they have been on about ten thousand pieces of paper in eleven years and I have never seen one with a reason in it, and I would like somebody to tell me whether that is because nobody has ever had a reason or because a reason is not a thing you can put in a box."

He wrote the first box in his own hand. His name, and where he may be found, and it was a room in a building in a city four hundred and thirty miles down a river and it was the room he sat in.

He wrote the second box. The run number, four figures and no letters, and the row, and what a table may hold and for how long and who is allowed to take a thing out of it, and the fourpence, and the fact that there is no provision for anybody else and there never has been.

The third box he put a pen against and did not fill in.

"The instrument touches nobody alive," said Marn Ottery.

"Yes."

"Then that is a true sentence and it is the correct answer, and the box wants a person, and a person is what does not go in it." She put one finger on the ruled line. "**And there is no version of this application that goes up the road with that box empty, because an empty box is a form that has not been filled in, and a form that has not been filled in is a question that was not asked, and I will not enter it and nobody upstairs will enter it and you would be a person who bought a shilling's worth of good paper and walked out of a building.**"

---

She went and got the box out from under the counter at about the fourth hour, and she had to get down on one knee for it, and it came up with about four hundred forms in it and about eleven pounds of paper in it, and she put it on the slab and took the lid off and stood looking at it.

"I did this in the sixth month of last year," she said. "On my own time. Told nobody. Went through the lot and read the name or the description or whatever was in the box at the top of each one." She turned the box about a quarter turn. "**Every one of the four hundred was bought at this counter. And every one of them came back to this counter instead of going out of the building in the hand of the person who filled it in, and that is not a rule and it is not anybody's job and there is no line in this building that says it, and I have been at this counter eleven years and I have never once been able to find out who does it.**"

"Two hundred and sixty-one of the fourth lines are empty. The strip is still down on those, or it was never got to, or nobody has ever been told that there is one to get to. A hundred and forty of the four hundred have the fourth line filled in, and on those the strip has been lifted and brought back and matched to the sheet by the number on the corner, and the office has the answer. In a hundred and twenty-six of those there is a person's name in the box at the top. In fourteen of them the box at the top does not say a person's name — a guild, a combination, a trade, a chapel, a poor-box, a company of watermen, an unincorporated company twice, a body described as the body, a body described as all persons having the use of a thing, a body that had put the word *all* in.

"And in eleven of the fourteen, at the foot of the form, underneath the fourth line, in a different hand, there is a person's name and a date. Not in the box. Underneath it, where a person signs. I said then and I am saying now that nobody at this counter ever asked whether that person had been asked, and I have had eleven months to find out whether they were and I have not found out and I am going to find out this afternoon."

"How."

"**By looking at the form.**"

She got a card out of the pigeonhole behind her and held it up so that both of them could see it, and it was about two inches by one and printed, and it had four lines on it and a line at the top for a day.

"That is the fourth line," she said. "It comes with the form. It is gummed to the foot of it, at the corner, and there is a printed number on the corner of the strip and the same printed number on the corner of the sheet, so that the two can be matched when the strip comes back. **The fourth line asks in what way the instrument touches you. And it is gummed down.**"

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"About a hundred and forty forms in that box have the fourth line filled in," said Marn Ottery. "About a hundred and forty, and about nine hundred forms a year go out of that rack, and I will tell you what I have watched at this counter for eleven years. About forty a year write it badly. About four a year write it well. About four hundred a year do not get to it at all, and the ones who do not get to it are mostly people who have been told what the instrument is for and have gone off to find a lawyer. **So it is about forty-four people a year who get as far as that strip and write on it, and this building can show you a hundred and forty of them in a hundred and forty years, and the other six thousand are in six thousand houses, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a person must bring a strip of paper back.** And I have stood here for eleven years and I have never once seen anybody ask a person for a knife, and there is no knife on this counter, and there has never been a knife on this counter, and there is a small pair of scissors in the second drawer for cutting ribbon on estates papers and nobody has ever thought of them in their life."

"Get at it with a thumb," said Marek Kest.

"Get at it with a thumb. And that is the second half of it and the second half is the reason I could not sleep for about four days in the second week of the fifth month of next year, and I have not slept properly since and I am not going to." She put the card down on the slab. "**To lift that strip you damage the sheet.** The gum takes the corner with it. You cannot take the fourth line off that form and leave the form behind you. And there are two things a person can do with it after that. Hand the sheet in with the strip still gummed to it and a corner torn off it, which is what a hundred and forty of the forms in that box have had done, and it costs a person nothing whatever except a corner. Or take the strip off and keep it, and the only way to take it off is to tear the office's copy of the form in half."

"And then the office has nothing."

"The office has nothing. The office has got a sheet with a person's name and an address and a day and a document, and a hole where the answer was, and the answer is in a building four hundred and thirty miles away in a person's own house, and there is no copy of it anywhere, and no form anywhere that says a person must keep it, and no way in this empire to make anybody produce it." She stopped and started again. "**A person who answers the only question on that form cannot be answered about the answer. That is not a consequence. I have looked for it. I have looked for four days and I have not found a corner of that form that was built to be read twice.**"

---

The nine minutes happened at about the fifth hour with the box on the slab and the passage empty and about four people in the room who were not listening.

She did them standing up and she did them out loud, and she was not talking to a lawyer and she was not talking to a table.

"Right. **A form with a reason in it and no box for a no is a form in which nobody is offered a no.** I have said that sentence before in this room to a woman of twenty-four and I did not know I was saying it about a box, I was saying it about a column, and it is the same fact and it is the same shape and I have now got the shape in my hand.

"**A strip of paper is a record of a question for exactly as long as somebody is carrying it, and nobody is paid to carry it back, and so this building can show you a hundred and forty answers in a hundred and forty years and cannot tell you how many there ought to be. And in the hundred and forty there is one number in it that I can put a name to, and the number is eleven, and there is no box on the paper for a no, and the no is not on the paper at all — the no is under the gum, and a person cannot get to a no they do not know is there.**"

"Eleven is not a small number," said Tamsin Rook.

"Eleven is not a small number and it is not a coincidence and it is not a conspiracy, and those are the three things I have said in my own head for eleven days and I am saying them out loud now so that they are in the air in a building instead of in my head at about the fifth hour. It is not a conspiracy because there is nobody to conspire. A conspiracy wants about nine people who know. There is one clerk at this counter and there is a press in the back room and there is a board with four prices on it and there is not one line anywhere in this building that says a person may leave the fourth line alone.

"It is not a coincidence because it is the same in every one of the eleven, and the same in every one of the eleven is a machine, and a machine does not want anything, and a machine that does not want anything can run for a hundred and forty years.

"And it is not a small number because of the gum. About four hundred a year do not get to it, and the reason they do not get to it is not that they are frightened. **Most of them never find out it is there.** They come up, they pay the shilling, they write their name and where they may be found and what the instrument is, and they get to the bottom of the sheet and there is a strip of paper gummed over the rest of it, and there is no mark on the strip saying there is writing under it, and they think the form is finished. And then they walk out of a building believing they have asked a question.

"And the eleven."

"**A body is the only thing on that form that cannot answer for itself.** A person in the box at the top answers for the paper. A trade answers for the paper, a chapel answers for the paper, and a body is a word with a seal on it and it cannot be served and it cannot be asked and it has nobody in it who can be fined. And so the form does the only thing this building knows how to do, which is to ask for a person, and it did not put a question to that person and it did not print a line for one. **And it took a hundred and forty years to produce eleven names, and it has never once asked one of the eleven whether they were there.**" She put her hand flat on the box. "A person who is going to be sent for tears their own copy, and a body that is going to be sent for gets a person put in front of it, and that is where the eleven come from, and it took me eleven months to work out that the only names this machine has ever produced were produced by the one line on it that has nothing to do with the instrument."

---

She wrote it out afterwards on a sheet of foolscap, in her own hand, in about nine lines, and dated it, and signed it, and said out loud that it was a clerk's own note and not a certificate and that nobody might rely on it including her.

And then she did not put it in the file.

"Because a file is a place a thing goes to be looked up," she said, "and I said that exact sentence in the sixth month of last year about a different piece of paper, and I have caught myself saying it twice, and I have been at this counter eleven years and I have never once said a sentence twice in this room. And I am not going to say it a third time either, because there is a limit and I have found it."

"Where does it go, then."

"Nowhere it can be lost. Not a file. The press in the back room, with the date on the outside of it, and my name on the outside of it, and no address on the outside of it, because a thing with an address on it is a thing somebody can be served with, and this is about eleven people who signed for a body and were never asked, and I am not going to hand any of them a road to themselves." She put the two sheets face down and squared them. "It is in a press in a building in a city. In about four years, if anybody ever wants it, they will have to come to this counter and ask, and they will have to ask in a form, and I will be here, because there is no relief at this counter and there has not been since I came to it, and I have said that out loud to two people this month and I am not going to say it again for another year."

---

And here is what the eleven are and are not, at the end of the third week of the fifth month of next year.

There are eleven names on a sheet of foolscap in a press in the back room of a building in Auremar. They are not read out and they are not written down. They are eleven people, and every one of them signed underneath a body, and not one of them was ever asked what the body was made of, and not one of them could have found out without destroying the only piece of paper the office had of them.

The man who is a clerk of the Inheritance Desk and holds no certification looked at the third box of his own application for about nine seconds and then left it empty, and put the pen down beside it.

He put the pen down and left the box empty, and said, out loud, in a room with a plate-glass front, to a clerk who is not a lawyer:

"**If the instrument touches nobody alive, then the person it touched is dead, and the only living thing this can be made to touch is whoever is standing here when the paper is filled in. And that is not a fault in the form.**"

"No," said Marn Ottery. "**That is the form being honest. And I have watched about four hundred people decide in about nine seconds that it was a fault in the form.**"

"Then I am the fault in the form."

"You are the cheapest thing in this building," said Marn Ottery, "and I did not say that to be unkind and I am going to say it again in about nine minutes and I am going to say it to a woman of twenty-four as well, because she is about to say it to herself and it is better coming from a stranger."
