# Chapter 170: The Notice on a Nail

There is no notice in this empire that will go to a person in charge of a press.

Tamsin Rook worked that out at about the fifth hour on the second day of the last week of the tenth month of next year, standing in an embankment corridor behind a building on a flood terrace, with a refusal in a form in her hand and about four feet of stone between her and the person it was about.

A notice is a list of works. A list of works is a list of jobs with days against them and a name at the foot of it, and that is the only kind of sentence in this empire that a person can act on the same day, and it gets to be that because a foreman nails it to a rail at a gate and anybody may come and read it.

And you cannot send one to a clerk. You cannot send one to a woman with a key on a string. You cannot send one to a person at a window about four feet off the ground who has never in nine years been appointed to anything, and there is no form that appoints her, and a form that appoints her would take away the only thing about her that makes the four lines work.

So she went and stood in the corridor and said it out loud, in person, which is the fourth time in about four months that she has done something to a room that a form exists for and would rather not have done.

---

Ivet Sarn took the form and read it once and put it face down on the sill, which is what a person does with a document they intend to think about later.

"That is right," she said.

"That is what a court said."

"I know what a court said. I am saying the court is right and I am saying it in about four seconds and I am not going to argue with you about whether I am allowed to. "She looked at the form face down. "Bound. The whole is held to be the whole. That is a true sentence and it is about nine words on either side of a word, and the word is the one in the middle, and I have been in charge of a press for nine years and I have never once been asked what the word means."

"What do you think it means."

"I am a clerk at a window and I do not think. I hold a key and I enter what I am given and I do not go looking for what a word in a reason is doing in the middle of a sentence on a day when a room is about to be cleared."

---

The notice was on a nail at the end of the corridor and it had been on the nail for about nine days and nobody had taken it down and nobody had said anything about it.

It was a list of works.

It had jobs against days. It had a name at the foot of it.

And the name at the foot of it was not a person's name. It was the name of a district.

Tamsin Rook stood and looked at it for about four seconds and then said the sentence out loud, and she said it in the corridor and not in a room, and there were two other people in the corridor and one of them was a witness.

"The last line of a schedule of a term of an office in a press in this building has a holder and no day at the end of it, and the holder is the office. "She did not point at the notice. "And this has jobs against days and a name at the foot of it and no day at the end of it, and the name is a district. Same shape. And a list of works with no day at the end of it is a duty that has been made to go on by somebody who is not going to be in the room."

"A district is not a person," said Marek Kest.

"**A district is not a person and a press is not a person and a company is not a person, and the whole of this matter is a book that is full of things that are not people doing the work of people.**" She put the refusal into her satchel. "A list of works gets to be the only sentence anybody can act on the same day because a person's name is at the foot of it. Take the name off and it is a list of jobs with nobody's promise at the end of it, and a list of jobs with nobody's promise at the end of it does not stop. It goes on. And nobody is going to be able to say afterwards when anybody was supposed to have been in this corridor, because there is no day."

---

She used the rule once, in the last week of the tenth month of next year, in writing, with a date on it.

The rule is theirs. It is from the twelfth month of last year and either of them may put a choice in writing with a day on it and the other must answer in a room, in words, with a date.

She put it on a sheet of her own paper and gave it to him and it said, in her own hand:

> *Two things, and one of them is a day and one of them is a choice. Choose one of them and I will do it in a room, in words, with a date. — T. Rook, the last week of the tenth month of next year.*

He read it twice and answered it in a corridor, in words, with a date.

"The rule does not apply."

"Because it is a day and not a choice."

"Because a rule that only ever works in one direction is not a rule, it is a preference. **It is sixteen months old and it has been used six times in fourteen months and every one of the six was a piece of work or a wage or a sheet of paper, and it has not once in sixteen months been about a person,** and there is a woman in this building with a key on a string."

"That is not the reason."

"No. It is that the two of us have never once had a person to put it to, and there is one in this building now, and I am not going to spend a rule on making a woman open a door she has not been asked to open."

"**A fact is not advice,** and I have been saying that in rooms for about four months and I have never once had it tested like this." She did not take the sheet back.

"Then write to Nevin Trost. "He gave the sheet back. "He has a file of his own and a book he keeps at home with the day on it, and I have never written to him, and the reason I have not is that a man who files a thing about his own office has to have it in a book somewhere, and if it is in that book then it is with somebody who cannot be instructed. That is a fact and it is not advice, and your third line is not broken by it, and you are not to thank me for the instruction because I have not given you one."

"You have just tested it."

"Then say it in a room and not in a corridor, and write it down there if you want it written down, and I will not thank you for it. And I am not going to tell you not to write it down, because telling you not to would be advice, and I have read your third line."

---

**Then she wrote the finding down in a book with no office on it, with the day on it, and she read it back twice, the second reading slower, and when the woman of thirty-eight asked her where she had got that from she said it out loud, which is the first time she has said it to anybody.**

"A girl of seventeen."

"The reader."

"The reader. She reads a sheet out twice on a platform in the cold, the second reading slower, and she has been doing it for about a season, and she is paid forty-five pence a day four days a week on a written engagement with a copy in a satchel, and nobody has ever asked her where she got it, and she has been asked twice and has not told anybody twice, and both of those are on the record in two places, because a clerk of thirty-four in a city said them out loud in a room in the last week of the seventh month of next year, and she has not explained where she got hers either, and the two of them have never met."

They stood in the corridor.

"The first reading is for the words and the second reading is for the people who did not have them the first time, " said Tamsin Rook. "That is not a rule. There is no form for it. It is a habit, and it is eleven years old in one building and about a season old in a bay, and nobody decided it and nobody has to."

---

The clearing begins on the fourth of the month after this one.

Ivet Sarn said it in the corridor, flatly, the way she says everything, and she said it while holding a rag, because the board over the window is wiped every morning and she does that herself.

"They are taking the fourth press out on the fourth. Not the press. The thing in it, into a cart, to a building about nine days' cart travel from here, and the press stays in the wall because a wall is not a thing anybody moves. "She wiped a part of the board that had nothing on it. "**I have been in charge of it for nine years and I am not going to be in charge of it after the fourth, and there is no form for that either, and nobody has told me I am going to be asked, and I have not asked whether I will be.**"

"Will you be asked."

"I do not know. And that is the whole of what I have. "She put the rag down. "**A person cannot be compelled to be in a room and there is no form in this empire for it, and if somebody in an office four hundred and thirty miles up a river writes my name on a list of works and nails it to a rail, I will not see it, because I am not on a bank and there is no rail, and about nine people know that and none of them can do anything about it and I am one of the nine.**"

"You can be asked."

"I can be asked. That is the whole of what is left and I have had nine years to work out that it is a sentence and not a wall, and a sentence is a thing a person can refuse, and I have not refused, and I am not going to say here in a corridor in front of two people that I have not refused, because that is a thing I will say to whoever asks me and not to a corridor."

Nobody thanked her. She said in advance that nobody was to, and nobody did, and the finding is that a woman who says in advance that she is not going to be thanked is almost always believed and almost never taken at her word, and that both of those are the same finding.

---

The two of them went down the corridor and out and down to the water and up the bank, and it took about two hours, and the second season of a rotation nobody had agreed to was in its third week and about nine people were on the water because there was a scour at the third-of-the-way place and about a hundred and forty feet of ditch on the south side is on the list.

A woman of twenty-eight was in a bay holding a lamp in about the second hour and the west door shut on them when they came in, because she packed its lintel with a wedge off a sleeper in about nine minutes in the fourth week of the sixth month of next year on her own account.

The bay's own ledger was on the bench against the north wall with three columns and the third one had no heading over it since the sixth year, and there were two entries at the back of it about a week apart, in two different hands, and neither of them had anybody's name on them except one at the foot of the first.

The slate on the nail over the bench in the middle had been washed and there was a day on it and a number and no names.

Nobody thanked anybody for any of it.

---

And here is what the last week of the tenth month of next year leaves standing, in the plainest words there are, and none of it is a question and none of it has been answered by anybody.

A review held on the first week of the eighth month of next year, and it was not a hearing, and about thirty-one people were in a room and about nine of the about forty-one who had said yes did not come, and there is no column for a person who did not come. A day walked up the length of a bank to about nine doors that cannot read a paragraph, by a girl of seventeen who is paid forty-five pence a day and is not paid for her feet, and a question asked out loud once on a platform and once in a bay, and the question was *and who is going to ask us again.*

That question has not been answered. The second season is in its third week and running, agreed to by nobody, asked of nobody, and about four people who cannot read a paragraph have now not been asked twice.

An answer read out at a counter in a city four hundred and thirty miles up this river, twice, the second reading slower, and it is a row and not a leaf, and what was struck out was a leaf of a bound schedule, and a bound schedule does not travel. A road walked five times, thirty days, three pounds and thirteen shillings and threepence out of about forty-four pounds a year, and there is no fund and there is no form for a fund.

A form with four lines on it, given away at a lock by a man who walked, in a hand of about sixty, with his own day on it and not the day of the thing, and the second of those four lines wants a person, and a press is not a person. A first name refused at a bench, entered, findable, and a refusal is not a denial and a document is not a confirmation. A correction made in a back room on a flood terrace, in a margin, with a day on it and a name at the bottom of it, and the whole of the method is a pen and a quiet afternoon, and nothing in this empire stops anybody doing it, and that is what a record is.

A person in charge of a press, who is not a party, and is served, and is asked, and holds no box and no fee and no term and no day at the end of it, and is the only position in this matter that anybody can stop, and a person cannot be compelled to be in a room and there is no form for it. A volume in a wall with nine terms in it and a tenth that has a holder and no day, and the holder is the office, and the line where the day would go is empty, and the book has no column for the person who emptied it. A copy of ten entries in four sheets in the inside of a coat, and no leaf out of a binding, and a gap in a copy is a fact about the copying and a gap in an original is a different sentence.

A refusal in a form with a day on it and a name at the foot of it and one word in the middle of the sentence that means more than it says, and no box for a why, and being right is what makes a document, and there is no way in this empire to un-ask.

And a notice on a nail at the end of a corridor with jobs against days and a district at the foot of it and no day at the end of it, and a room to be cleared on the fourth of the month after this one.

**A guarantee under the fire-and-water device stands offered and unanswered at no charge on a bank about four hundred and forty foot long, answerable at any time afterwards by a person answering in its terms, and there is no such person and there is not going to be one, and about sixty children under sixteen are inside it, and inside a guild's blank list, and inside a rotation they have not been asked about, and have been told about none of it.**

**A bill at Lowcross of nineteen pounds three and fourpence, unpaid, nobody liable, and about a penny a day the real cost of it, and no line for a bridge in that fund and there has not been one in nineteen years, and four hundred and eleven people live on the far side and the district's return for four weeks says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings, and those are two figures and they are never the same.**

**A reader of seventeen on a written engagement, at forty-five pence a day, four days a week, not thanked, not going to start, and this is the last season in which anybody on that bank can ask her something and be answered for nothing.**

**And about four hundred and thirty miles of road between a person and a door, in each direction, at about six days, with no fund, and no form for a fund, and a river that is running and does not know any of this.**
