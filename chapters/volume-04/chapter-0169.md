# Chapter 169: A Door That Has to Be Opened by a Person

The press is in the wall at the end of the corridor and it is about four feet high and it has a door and a hasp and a key, and the key was on a string on a woman's hip and she took it off and put it in the lock herself.

She is a clerk of thirty-eight and she has been in charge of it for nine years by a key on a string, and she turned it, and she did not open it on her own account, and there is a difference and both of the other people in the corridor heard her say which one it was.

"You open it," she said. "I have not been asked to and I have not offered and if I open it on my own account then in about four years this is a thing I did in a corridor at about the ninth hour, and there is a woman of twenty-eight four hundred and thirty miles down this river who did a thing to a lintel on her own account and entered it in a book and did not read it out, and I am not going to be her, and I am not going to be a person who is going to be asked about a door."

"It is a press," said Marek Kest.

"Then it can be opened by a person who has been asked to open it, and that is a different sentence, and it is the only sentence in this matter that has ever worked."

---

The woman of twenty-four was in the corridor and not in the corridor.

That is the whole of that and it took about nine minutes to arrange and none of it was a quarrel.

"A party is served," said Ivet Sarn. "A witness is asked. **You are a party, and you have been a party since the day went at the top of a form in the second week of the fifth month of next year, and you are not a witness in this room, and if you stand in this corridor being a party then the whole of what happens in that corridor is a thing a party did.**"

"And if I am in the room."

"Then there is no witness in this matter at all, and I have been at a window for nine years and I have never once let a room have no witness in it, because a room with no witness is a room where whatever anybody says afterwards is true. "She held the door. "Stand at the end of the corridor. You can see him. He can see you. And in about four years if anybody comes along with a paper and a question, there is one person in this building who can say that a woman stood at the end of this corridor and did not come in, and that is worth more than anything you could do in the room."

Tamsin Rook went to the end of the corridor and stood against the wall with her satchel and did not argue, and she said the words *that is a fact and not advice* twice on her way down the corridor, out loud, to nobody, and her third line says she is not to advise anybody, and she did not advise anybody.

---

The corridor is about thirty feet long and it has a window at the west end with a pane out of it and a bench along the east side that is not the bench anybody sits on, and a woman of twenty-four stood at the west end of it for about forty minutes and could hear a lamp being set down and could not hear a word.

At about the second quarter of it, a man came up the corridor with a paper in his hand and stopped at about the middle of it and waited, because there was a person at the west end and he was the sort of person who waits when there is a person.

"Can you read that for me," he said.

"I can."

"Then will you."

"No," said Tamsin Rook. "And I want to tell you why, because you have walked up a corridor to ask and you have about four minutes. I am a copyist. A copyist's trade is writing a thing out again in a hand, and a reader's trade is a different trade and it is somebody else's, and I do not know whether you can read, and if you cannot read then me doing it is a favour, and a favour has no notice, and in about nine days you will be back and I will have done it again and neither of us will have any idea where the line is."

"Then who does."

"**There is a girl of seventeen on a bank four hundred and thirty miles down this river who is paid forty-five pence a day to read things out loud, four days a week, on a written engagement, and she is the best reader in this district and she is not here and I do not know her schedule and I am not going to invent one.**"

The man looked at her for a moment and then he said, "That is a proper answer," and went and stood at the sill, and waited there, and was seen to at about the ninth hour.

She had a drawer in a room four hundred and thirty miles up this river with four envelopes in it, and one of them had been there since the first month of the year before last, and she did not think about it in a corridor on a flood terrace. That is the fourth time in two years she has not thought about it, and she is not going to start now, and it is not going to be a decision.

And she was thanked by nobody, because there was nothing in the corridor that anybody had done for her, and that is the correct amount of thanks for standing at the end of a corridor for forty minutes, and she had asked for it in advance in a room in a city in the last week of the seventh month of next year and has not been thanked since.

---

The lamp was on the sill. The schedule is bound in a vellum that has gone the colour of a thumb, and the stitching is visible at the top of the spine, and it is one volume and it is about the size of a hymn book and it is heavier than a hymn book.

He did not open it on the bench. He opened it where it was, on the shelf, with a person standing in the corridor.

A schedule of a term of an office. Each entry is a term, and each term has a holder, and each term has a day at the end of it. That is the whole shape of the book and it is the shape of about nine hundred such books in about nine hundred buildings in this empire, and the shape has not changed.

Nine completed terms. Nine holders. Nine days at the end of nine terms.

The tenth entry has a holder and no day.

---

He read it three times and then he said it out loud, and he said it to the woman with the key and not to the woman at the end of the corridor.

"The holder of the tenth term is the office. Not a person. The office, in its own name, as its own holder, and the day at the end of that term is not there, and there is a line for it and the line is empty, and the line is empty because somebody took the day out of it, and somebody had to have hands."

He put his finger on the line without touching the page, the way a person does in a press.

"And there is no column in this book for the person who did that. There is a column for the holder and a column for the day and there is nothing else, and there has never been anything else, and a schedule of a term is not a register of duties, and about nine hundred people in nine hundred buildings have believed those two books were the same one, and they are not, and the day was taken out of it in a book that had no column in it for the person who took it out."

---

He copied it.

He copied the whole of the ten entries onto four sheets of a copyist's paper, in his own hand, at about the ninth hour, standing up, with a lamp on a sill and a woman in a corridor holding a door open with her hip because both her hands were needed for the page.

He did not take the leaf out of the binding. He did not take the volume off the shelf. He did not unbind anything, and he said out loud why, twice, because the second time was for the woman at the end of the corridor.

"A gap in a copy is a fact about the copying. That is a thing I have had in a book with no heading since the second month of next year and it is the first thing anybody ever taught me that I have kept. "He squared the four sheets. "A gap in an original is a different sentence. A gap in an original is a fact about a removal, and I am not going to be the man who writes that sentence about this."

"You could have it," said Ivet Sarn. "It is four feet away and there is nobody in this building who would know for nine years."

"**I could have it and I would have a leaf in a bag and a bag in a coat and about four hundred and thirty miles of road, and I would be a man carrying a removed thing, and there is no form anywhere that says a person may remove a leaf out of a bound schedule, and there is not going to be one, and I have walked five times to get here.**" He put the four sheets in his inside coat, next to two forms. "So I have a copy. And a copy of a thing is a fact about the copying. **I have about four hundred and thirty miles of road and three pounds and thirteen shillings and threepence and I have got a copy, and the copy is worth exactly what a copy is worth, which is that it can be read and it cannot be leaned on.**"

Nobody in that corridor argued with him about it and the woman with the key shut the press and turned it and put the key back on the string, and the string had been shortened twice and both shortenings were hers.

---

The district court sits above a shop and it has a clerk's window of its own about four feet off the ground, and the clerk there is about fifty-five and has been there nineteen years, and he took the application at about the second hour of the third day of the third week of the tenth month of next year and read it and read it again.

"You are asking for a direction."

"I am asking for a direction that a leaf of a bound schedule in a county repository be produced to a person for the purpose of a proceeding, in a room, in this district."

"That is a direction. Put your name at the bottom."

He put his name at the bottom and it went on the file and it came back in four days, and a form came back in four days, and a form is a document, and a document with a reason in it and no box for a why is a resolution of a kind and is findable.

> *Refused. The thing asked for is bound, and the whole is held to be the whole, and a direction is not made for a part of a thing that is not severable. The applicant may apply again with the whole.*

There is no box on that form for a why.

And the reason uses the word *bound* and does not say what it means, and it says it once, and it is in the middle of the sentence, and there is about nine words on either side of it.

---

Being right is what makes a document. A document is findable. **There is no way in this empire to un-ask, and he asked, and in about four years anybody with fourpence can read that a man of thirty-eight four hundred and thirty miles down this river asked a district court for a direction in the third week of the tenth month of next year and was told no in a sentence with a word in it that means more than it says.**

He read it twice. The second reading slower.

"It is right," he said.

"It is right," said the clerk of fifty-five, who had not said so. "And I have been here nineteen years and I have never once had a form come back with a word like that in it, and I am not going to pretend I know what it means, and I would be obliged if you did not tell me."

"I am not going to."

He put the two forms in the inside pocket of a coat where a third one already was, and the third one is nineteen years old and is a refusal and it has a day on it and he has read it about four hundred times and he is not going to say what it says.

---

And that was the end of the fifth journey and the beginning of the last thing in it, which was not a journey at all and cost nothing and is the only part of this matter he would be able to do again.

He had a person in a corridor with a key on a string, and a schedule in a wall with a holder and no day on the last line of it, and a copy of ten entries in his coat, and a refusal in a form with a word in it, and a written application with two names in it and an empty space at the foot of it.

**And he could not post any of it to her, because there is no notice in this empire that will go to a person in charge of a press, and a notice is a list of works, and a list of works is nailed to a rail at a gate four hundred and thirty miles from here, and the only other way is to walk back up that corridor and stand in it and say it out loud, and he had two days and a lamp and a woman who could be asked to stop and would not be able to.**

He had not told her. That was the thing he had to go back and do, and it was the only thing in the whole of the six days that he did not already know how to say.

---

A volume with a holder and no day on the last term of it, and the holder is the office, and the line where the day would go is empty, and the book has no column for the person who emptied it.

**A copy of ten entries in four sheets in the inside of a coat, and no leaf out of a binding, and about three pounds and thirteen shillings and threepence out of about forty-four pounds a year, and no fund, and no form for a fund.**

And a refusal in a form with a day on it and a name at the foot of it and one word in the middle of the sentence that means more than it says, and no box for a why.
