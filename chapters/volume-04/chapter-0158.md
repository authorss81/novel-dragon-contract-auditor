# Chapter 158: A Door That Is a Door

The door is in the west end of the north bay of a hired stone store and it has a lintel over it and the lintel has dropped about half an inch in about forty years and has never been packed and does not shut.

That is the whole of it. It is a door. It is not a fault and it is not a mystery and nobody in ninety-one households has ever mentioned it, and the reason nobody has ever mentioned it took Sefa Ild three weeks to work out and it is that **a bay is a room and not a work, and there is no list of works that has a door on it, and there is no column anywhere in this matter for a door.**

She is twenty-eight. She has kept that bay since the second month of next year, at a wage the board set out of four shillings a year, and it is the only money the Slade Cut Company has and all of it goes on the rent of the building, and the sum that reaches her is smaller than the four shillings and she has never once asked what it is and there is no line in any book in that building with her name against it except the one she has written herself in the bay's own book.

She has been paid for standing in a room for about four months and she is not a foreman and does not want to be one and has said so twice in that bay to people who were not asking.

---

"You have a bench against the north wall that came with the building," said the book-keeper of thirty-eight, on the second day of the fourth week of the sixth month of next year, at about the seventh hour, "and a bench in the middle, and a door in the west end."

"You have been in here four times since the fourth month of last year," said Sefa Ild.

"I have. I have counted about four hundred people through that door and I have never once looked up."

"Then it is a door and you have been looking at it every time and neither of you noticed." She got up off the bench she had been sitting on, which is the bench in the middle, and went and stood in the frame. "**It is four feet nine from the jamb to the jamb and about six foot eight high and the lintel is packed out on the right-hand side and not on the left, and it has dropped about half an inch, and it has been dropping for about forty years, and there is no packer in this building and there has never been one, and a packer costs about fourpence.**"

"Nobody ever packed it."

"Nobody ever packed it because packing it would make it a thing somebody had done." She said it the way she says things in that bay, which is once and then not again. "And I will tell you the whole of the reason in one sentence and then I am not going to say it again this month. In a room where about nine people cannot read a paragraph and about nineteen have said no in a doorway, a thing somebody had done is a thing that can be asked about, and I have been on the other side of that, and so have you."

---

She got a wedge off the top of a stack of sleepers against the far wall at about the eighth hour and it took her about nine minutes and she did it on her own account and nobody instructed her and nobody asked her to.

Nobody helped. That is a fact about that bay and it is a fact about the four people standing in it, and half of them can read a paragraph and could have held the other end of a lintel in a few seconds.

Nobody helped because nobody had been asked, and there is a difference between that and nobody being willing, and the whole of this matter is the difference, and it has taken four months of a bay and it is a door in the west end of a bay.

"There," she said, and stepped back.

The door shut. It shut with a sound that about four people in that room had never heard in their lives, because it is a hired stone store off the Slade and the sound of a door shutting in it is not a thing anybody has been in a room for.

---

"Right," said Sefa Ild. "Now I am going to say three things and the first two are about this room and the third is about the whole of the last four months and I have not said it out loud to anybody and I am saying it in here because in here is the only room in ninety-one households where nobody is waiting to write something down."

She leaned against the bench in the middle.

"**A room with one door in it is a room a person can be put into.** Not can be got at. **Put into.** And there is no form in this empire for compelling a person to be brought to a room, and I have looked for one in about four minutes and not found it and I am not going to say I have looked properly, and a house does not need one because a house is a person's own and they open their own door.

"And a door that does not shut is a door anybody can be put through. **That is the whole of the two of them together and it took me about nine minutes with a wedge and it is the cheapest thing anybody in this matter has ever done and it cost fourpence of a sleeper.**"

"And the second thing," said Rennick Adley.

"**A door needs a person to shut it and a duty needs a holder that can stop, and those are the same size of thing.**" She looked at the closed door. "That is not mine. That is a keeper of a district water office, in a room, in three parts, in the first month of last year, and about nine hundred rooms have heard the three parts and not understood them, and I have understood them since about the fourth month and I have never once said it out loud about a door, and it has been in this room since the fourth month and nobody asked me.

"A person can be asked about a door. A person cannot be asked about a duty. Those are not the same question and I have been in a bay for four months where about forty-one people were asked about a duty sixty times and nobody was asked about a door once, and the door is the only thing in that bay that had to be shut by a person."

---

"And the third thing," said the chair of sixty-four, from the bench against the north wall, who had had the first two read to her. "You said three."

"I did."

"Say the third one."

Sefa Ild stood in front of a bay that had not asked her for anything and said it.

"I have been paid a wage for standing in this room for about four months and nobody has ever asked me what the room is for, and I have worked out what it is for, and I am not going to tell anybody, and I am going to tell you why I am not going to tell anybody and then I am going to stop.

"The room is for the asking. It is the only place in ninety-one households where somebody is asked a question they can say no to, and they say no about nineteen times, and the nineteen are still behind that bank, and the nineteen are not on anything and have not been asked again and are not going to be. And a person who has said no in this room is a person who has a door.

"And a person who cannot be found cannot be asked to stop, and a person who cannot be asked to stop is a person who holds a thing for ever, and there is one name on the list of works in this room that is not a name. **It is a description, and she let herself be written down as a description twice, at her own insistence, and a described person is a person and is harder to serve and easier to lose, and she is the only one on that list who is not a name, and she is on it.**"

Nobody wrote it down. About four people in that bay could have.

"**A room with a door that shuts is a room a person can leave. That is all a door is for. I have been paid four months for standing in a room and the room had no way out of it and I did not know that until this morning and I am not going to be a foreman about it and I would like it noticed that I have not said the word *supervision* once in four months and that is on purpose.**"

---

At about the sixth hour she entered it in the bay's own book with the day on it.

The bay's own book is a hard-backed ledger the company has kept since before the war, and it has three columns and the third one has not had a heading over it since the sixth year, and there is no line in the whole of it with a wedge against it, and there never will be.

She wrote: *West door. Lintel packed, own account, wedge off the top sleeper. S. Ild.*

And she did not read it out, and that is the point, and she has said so once.

"You are not going to read that out," said the book-keeper.

"If I read it out then every person in this room knows that I did a thing to the building on my own account, and in about four years somebody is going to come along with a paper about the building, and there is a name at the foot of it, and the name will be mine, and it will be the only name in nine hundred and forty that anybody could ever have asked me about." She shut the book. "A thing somebody did in a room is a thing that can be asked about. That is the sentence. It is the same sentence as the lintel and it took me three weeks and about nine minutes."

---

And nobody thanked her.

Somebody started to, about the fourth hour, in the way people do, and she put her hand up the way the chair of sixty-four does it, and the chair of sixty-four has been putting it up in that bay for nineteen years, and it stopped, and nothing about it is on the page, and she had a hold of that too before anybody said anything, because she has watched four rooms do it and has never once been in one where it was done well.

"Do not," she said. "**I am not doing it for the room. I am doing it because the door was making a noise and it was going to keep on making a noise, and half the people in here cannot sleep with a noise and the rest of them have stopped hearing it, and those are two different problems and only one of them is mine.**"

---

And what was left standing at the end of the fourth week of the sixth month of next year, in the plainest words there are:

A door in the west end of a hired bay that shuts, and a lintel packed with a wedge off a sleeper, and one day in a hard-backed ledger with three columns and no heading over the third of them, and a name at the foot of one line.

**Three doors four hundred and thirty miles apart, and no form anywhere in this empire that joins them:** a clerk's window at a court on a flood terrace, which is a door with a counter in front of it; a counter at the end of a passage in a building with a plate-glass front, which is a door with a counter in front of it; and a door in the west end of a bay, which is a door.

And a woman of twenty-eight who has been paid for standing in a room for about four months and has never been asked what the room is for, and has now told a room, and has not told anybody else, and does not want to be thanked, and is not going to be a foreman, and is owed nothing.

And about nineteen people behind that bank who said no in a doorway and are not on anything and have not been asked again and are not going to be, and about four people who cannot read a paragraph who were never asked and never will be, and a guarantee standing offered and unanswered at no charge on a bank about four hundred and forty foot long with about sixty children under sixteen inside it, and none of it is a symbol and all of it is still there.
