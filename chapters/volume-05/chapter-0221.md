# Chapter 221: A Name In A Book Is Not An Answer

The tray went up the corridor at about the ninth hour and came down again at about the quarter past, and the room is on the ground floor of a building off the old river road and it has a window about two feet wide with no pane in it, and a shelf against the far wall with eleven books on it, and a table, and a chair.

Orla Denning is about fifty-five. She has been at the table nineteen years and she has entered about four thousand things into those eleven books, and there is no name in any one of them, and that is not carelessness, it is the only shape a minute book has got.

She had got the sixth one down off the shelf before the tray came back.

---

What she was going to write in it had been written down once, in a room with a rail in it, in the first week of the fifth month of the year after, by her, and it had a clerk's name at the foot of it then and it has one now, and in between those two things a court of this empire sat and recorded that the question exists.

A question had been put to that court nine years ago in a room with a number on the door. The court is a court of this empire. It still exists. It sits on the same ground. The question was about a clause. The clause has a name, and the name is written in about nine hundred documents in this empire and is not on any of the boards in this city, and a court asked about it nine years ago, and it has not been answered by the court that asked it, and nobody has answered it since.

**She wrote the day against it first, because that is what she has done four thousand times, and then the thing, and then her own name at the foot of it.** The whole of it took about nine seconds, and she counted them, and she has never once told anybody she counts them.

---

Then she put the pen down and said it out loud, to the room, because a room is the only kind of place in this empire where a thing like that can be said and she has never once said it.

"**A name written down is not an answer, and a name on a question is the only protection a question has ever had in this empire.**"

She waited, in case the tray heard it, and the tray was on the stairs.

"Naming a thing is not the same as answering it and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to answer one. I have written that in a book in about nine seconds and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires me to do anything else about it, and I am not going to do anything else about it, and I would like it read back to me by somebody in a room and there is nobody who is going to."

---

Then she did the other part, which is the part she had actually come down off the shelf for, and it took about four minutes, and she made it as a list because that is what she is.

"A clause is not a question. It is a piece of a thing that was agreed, and it does not ask anybody anything, and no person can be asked to keep one, and if nobody says a word about it for nine years it goes on being exactly as true as it was on the first day, which is the whole of what a clause is for and the whole of what it is not for."

"A clause is not a person. **It cannot be asked a question** and it cannot be asked to explain itself and it cannot be refused anything, and there is no form in this empire for serving a thing that was agreed before anybody in this room was born."

"A clause is not an answer either, and I want that said differently from the rest of it, because for nineteen years people have come into this room and gone out again believing that a thing which has been named has been dealt with, and it has not, and I have never been able to say so to any of them. **A name in a book is a name in a book and it is not a person and it cannot be asked anything, and I have said that out loud in this room twice and both times to a room that did not ask.** The whole of what I do in this room is put a thing in a book, and the whole of what they want is for it to be not true any more, and I cannot do that, and I am not going to pretend I can."

---

The door was open and the man of about thirty came in with a tray on his shoulder, and the tray is a thing with a flat board on it, and the same line is printed on the front of every one of them in this empire, and he has carried them up and down that corridor for nine years and has never once asked anybody anything.

He put the tray down on the table, which he is not supposed to do, and looked at the book on it, which he is not supposed to do, and said:

"Is that the office."

"Which office," said Orla Denning.

"The one this building is for."

She did not answer it for about four seconds, and the four seconds were not caution, and she has thought about them since.

"No. There is no office in this building and there never has been one, and the eleven things on that shelf are about one and none of the eleven is it."

---

" Then what is in the building."

"That is a plain question," said Orla Denning, "and I am going to answer it, and I am going to say the other thing first because I have said it in this room twice and both times it was too late."

The man of about thirty waited. He had a tray on the table and nine years of not asking anybody anything behind him, and he waited the way a man waits who has decided to wait.

"What I have just written in that book is the name of a thing, and the name of a thing is not a person. A document that names an office is a document and not an office. It is one side of a sheet of good paper and it is not printed, and a seat's own device is cut into the head of it, and it is set out in six numbered lines and a foot, and the second of those six asks that no inference of any kind be drawn from the absence of any name, and the sixth declines to name whoever holds the office at all. Every one of the six is about a room. Not one of them is about a man."

"You have read one."

"I entered the day of one. That is not the same as reading one and I am not going to improve the difference for you. There are people in this city who would improve it, and one of them has a name at the foot of a paper of the same office and the same six lines, and **I am not going to say that name in this room, and you did not ask me for it, and I would like it noticed in this room that you did not ask me for it.**"

---

"Then you have written a question down twice, and nobody has answered it either time."

"No. I have written down that a question exists. That is what a minute book does, and it is the only thing a minute book has ever done, and it has never once pretended to be anything else. And I am going to tell you the reason it is worth nine seconds, and then I am going to serve your tray, and then you are going to take it up that corridor and I am not going to say one word more about any of it."

She turned the book round so that he could see the entry without reading it, which he could not, and which was not the point.

"**Because a question that is written down, with a day on it and a clerk's name at the foot of it, is the only thing in this empire anybody can be held to.** Not to answering it. To having asked it. In about four years a man with a copy of the right page can put that line beside anything he likes and ask a clerk why she wrote it down, and she will have to stand there and say what she meant, and that is the whole of what a minute book buys anybody. It buys nobody an answer. It buys the question a name on it, and a name on a question is the whole of the protection a question has ever had in this empire, and it cost about nine seconds this morning."

The man of about thirty looked at the entry for a while and then said, "That is a poor thing to have gone to the trouble over."

"It is the only thing there is. And I have got about four thousand of them and not one of them has ever been answered, and the one I wrote this morning is the first one I have written twice, and I wrote it twice because the first one was in a room with a rail in it and about nine people in it, and a question said in a room with nine people in it is a question with nine people standing in the way of it."

---

She put her hand flat on the table, which she does about twice a year, and then she did not pick up the tray.

"Ask me what a line in one of these is," she said. "Go on. You have not asked me anything this morning and you have carried every tray that has gone up that corridor for nine years and asked nothing, and I am not going to waste a good morning by not being asked anything, so ask me."

The man of about thirty looked at the shelf.

"What is a line in one of them."

"That is a better question than the one you asked me first," said Orla Denning, "and it is a plain question as well, and I am going to answer it and then you are going to take your tray up that corridor."

She got up and took the second book down off the shelf and put it on the table beside the sixth one and opened it at about a third of the way in, and turned it round, and then she read one line out loud, because he cannot read and there was nobody else in the room.

"A day, a room, a thing a room decided, and a clerk's name." She shut it. "That is a line. That is four thousand lines, one after another, for nineteen years, and I could not tell you one of the people any of them is about, and neither could the man who wrote the first one, and that is not because we are careful. **It is because a minute book is a record of what a room decided and it is not a register of people, and a record of a decision is not a document about a person, and a line in a book is not a person and it cannot be asked anything.**"

"That is a strange thing to be told by a clerk."

"It is a strange thing to be told by anybody," said Orla Denning, "and about nine hundred and forty names are in a book on that shelf that I could do this whole morning out of instead, and I have never opened it for a person, and one woman asked me to in the second week of the twelfth month of the year after and I said no and I have not said yes since."

She picked the tray up and put it on his shoulder.

"And the two you have asked me are plain questions and they are not questions about a word on a form, and the count of plain questions is a different count and is not printed anywhere. The count of the other kind is four. It has been four since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next, and it was four before that, and nobody has asked anybody anything since, and there is no post in this empire whose job is to ask, and I am not going to be one and neither are you."

---

The man of about thirty got to the door with the tray and then stopped and came about four steps back, which he has not done in nine years, and said:

"You are going to write it down again."

"No," said Orla Denning.

"You said you wrote it twice."

"I wrote the day against it twice and the thing itself once, in the fifth month of the year after, in a room with a rail in it, and I have not written it a second time this morning and I am not going to." She took the tray off his shoulder and set it back on the table, which she had no right to do. "**You have not noticed the difference and I am not going to point it out to you, and that is the last thing I am going to say this morning, and I have said the price out loud twice in nineteen years and both times it was to a room that did not ask.**"

Then she said the thing that had been sitting under all of it since she had taken the book down, standing up, and he stayed because he had a tray to take up the corridor and could not have gone without it.

"There is a question on the record in this building that nobody is ever going to be able to answer, and it is not the one I have just written down, and I have known that since the first week of the seventh month of the year after and I have told nobody, and I am telling you because there is nobody else in this building and you are about to carry a tray up a corridor."

"Then somebody will send for it."

"Somebody sent for it once. The only paper that would bear on it is a paper that does not exist in a form a person can hold, and the office that would have it is not a person, and you cannot serve an office and you cannot make one come and stand in a room, and so there is nobody in this empire who could be asked whether that office gave an order. **It is not unanswered because it is hard. It is unanswered because the thing that would answer it cannot be produced at all.** That is a stronger wall than a document nobody has read, and it is the finding of this morning."

He did not look as though he had understood that and she did not explain it.

"A document nobody has read is a thing that can be read next week by anybody who is patient. A document that cannot be produced is a thing that can be produced next month, or next year, or never, and there is nobody in between to hurry it. And the same machinery that made the two of you is the machinery that made the absence, and it makes both in about nine seconds, and it has never once asked anybody."

The tray went up the corridor and came down again at about the half hour, and Orla Denning wrote nothing else in the sixth book that day. The day is against the entry and her name is at the foot of the entry and the answer is not in the book, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to answer a question once it has been written down with a day on it and a clerk's name underneath it. About nine hundred buildings in this empire have a form for asking. Not one of them has a form for waiting, and nobody has ever been appointed to the waiting, and that is the whole of what happened on the second day of the first week of the third month of the year after next in a room on the ground floor of a building off the old river road, and it was nine seconds and a tray.
