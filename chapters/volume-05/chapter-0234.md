# Chapter 234: A Letter With His Name On It, And A Day Nobody Is Going To Pay For

The room is rented by the week and there is a table in it and a chair and a bed that is against the wall he does not use, and about four feet of that wall is bare, and he has never once worked out what it is for and he has never once covered it.

Marek Kest is thirty-eight. He has no office and no fund and no register and nothing in this empire he could certify anything with, and a bar of his own that came off him on the twelfth of the ninth month of last year on an application of one line which he wrote himself. There is a line about two inches long on the inside of his left wrist and it reads nothing, and it decides nothing, and it does not know where the ford is.

There is a letter on the table, and it came on the fourth day of the first week of the sixth month of the year after next, and it has his name at the top of it, and it is the first document in this matter that anybody has ever been able to put his name near.

---

It is four lines and a signature, and the signature is a name he does not know and has never heard, and it is not the name of any office.

The four lines ask him to come and look at a thing on a bank four hundred and thirty miles down this river. They say that the person writing does not know what it is. They say that nothing will be paid and that the carriage will be paid. And they say, in the fourth line, that if he comes he will not be asked to sign anything and will not be asked to speak to anybody, and that if he does not come there is nothing in the fourth line at all.

He has read it about nine times. He has not answered it. He has not burned it, and he has thought about burning it for about four days, and there is a stove in the room and a sheet of clean paper in the drawer that nobody in this empire has ever issued him.

---

He put the pencil on the table on the first day and took it off again about nine seconds later, and the reason is the one that has been the reason since the fourth week of the first month of the year after next, and he has never been able to improve it and has stopped trying to.

**About nine hundred documents in this empire already have a particular word in a hand, and every one of them belongs to a person with a trade, and the trade is the wall.** He has no trade. A hand of his is the only thing anybody could put a name to, and there is nothing about him for the name to stick to.

But the letter is not that. The letter is worse, and he worked out why on the second day, standing up, in about a minute and a half.

A letter is a document. **A document is the only thing anybody in this empire can be held to**, and this one is a document with his name at the head of it, and it came to a rented room, and it came because somebody knew he was in it. Nobody sent a form to a counter. Somebody sent a letter to a man, and a letter can be produced, and a produced document can be put in a room, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a man cannot be shown a letter he wrote nothing on.

He turned that over for the rest of the day and on the day after.

**The person who wrote it can be found.** A person who writes a letter is a person with a hand, and a hand is the only thing anybody in this matter has ever been able to use. He has been the one who cannot be found since the first week of the first month of the year after next, and the finding has been useful to him about nine times, and it is the first time it has been useful to somebody else.

---

And then on the second day he read the third line again, and the third line is the one he had not got to, and it took him about four minutes and it undid about nine days of his own certainty.

*The person writing does not know what it is.*

He had read that four times and taken it for a courtesy. It is not a courtesy. There is no such thing as a courtesy in this empire except a shilling a question at a notary's table and fourpence a copy, and a person writing on their own paper does not get either.

**Whoever wrote it is as lost as he is.** There is a bank four hundred and thirty miles down this river with about nineteen reasons in a column and about four men in a bay who cannot read a paragraph, and there is a foreman of fifty-one who has been in a box since the seventh month of the year after and has not been asked about it, and there is a book-keeper of thirty-eight whose wage is held against a roof he put there himself. And there is a man of about forty in a furnace town who is holding the whole of a woman's money and cannot say what it is held against, and that man is four hundred and thirty miles the other way up the river and has never once been asked.

Somebody who does not know what it is has written to the only person in this matter who can be found by nobody, and has asked him to come and look at it, and has promised in the fourth line that he will not be asked to sign anything and will not be asked to speak to anybody.

"A promise made by a person with nothing behind them is worth nothing," he said out loud, to the bare four feet of wall, "and it is the first promise anybody has made me in about four years that was not in a form. I am going on the strength of it and I want that said in a room with one man in it. In about four years the only record of this is a sheet of paper with a day on it and no name on it, and nobody will be able to tell whether I was a careful man or a frightened one."

---

Then he did the thing he has to do every time a piece of paper about a person reaches him, which is to find out what it would cost to be the person who answers it.

About four hundred and thirty miles each way. About six days. A wage of seventeen shillings and a penny the week on the other end of that road. He has done this sum before, in the third month, and the fourth month, and he does not do it again out loud because the answer does not change and because a man who works an answer out loud every time he hears the question is a man who has begun to want one particular answer.

The answer is that there is no fund. He has never had a fund and there is no form in this empire for one, and about four hundred and thirty miles of that road is paid for out of about forty-four pounds a year, and thirty days of it is three pounds and thirteen shillings and threepence. A man who goes is a man who has decided that his own wages are the fund, and there is no office to decide that and no paper to hold him to it and nothing anywhere in this empire that says a man may do that with his own money.

---

And then he got to the part he had not got to on the first day, and it came at about the seventh hour of the evening on the fourth day of the third week of the sixth month of the year after next, and it took him about four minutes and it is the whole of the week.

He had it about a fifth of the way and then he put it down and had a drink of water and picked it up again, because it is the sort of thing a man wants to hold once and then wants to put down.

A labour he can be refused. That is one of the two things he is, and it is the shape of a road, and the whole of the meaning of it in about four words is that a man who is a labour is a man somebody can pay for a day, and a man somebody can pay for a day is a man somebody can tell to stop.

**About four hundred and thirty miles down this river there is a girl of seventeen on a written engagement at forty-five pence a day and four days a week, at the back of a bay, and she has not been thanked, and the reason nobody on that bank is going to ask her to read the hold out loud is that a person who is paid to read is a person who can be told to stop.**

He sat on the bed with the letter on his knee for about a minute and a half and said nothing at all, and then he said it out loud, because a room is the only kind of place in this empire where a thing like that can be said and he has never once said it to a person.

"**That is the same instrument.** The two ends of this are the same instrument and it has taken me four days and about nine readings to get from one to the other, and about four hundred and thirty miles of river is between them, and I have not met one person who has both ends."

---

There was a knock at about the ninth hour.

He did not open it.

"You are in," he said, to about two inches of door.

"I am in," said a woman of about thirty-five. "I keep a day-book in a lodging house and I have come about nine miles, and I have two things and one of them is a question. It is a plain question and it is not about a word on a form. I am standing on a stair and I am not going to be let in, and I have been told twice now that a room you are in is a party, and I am not going to be a party in a room. I have not told anybody I came."

"Ask it through the door."

---

"What does a person who cannot be found have that a person who can be found does not have?"

"I have got about four things and you have not come nine miles for one of them, so it is the third one."

"Go on."

"A person who cannot be found can be given a thing to do and cannot be charged for it, cannot be sent a paper about it, and cannot be stopped," said Marek Kest. "**Everything else about this is worse for me and none of the rest of it is in that list.** The rest of it is: I cannot be thanked, I cannot be paid, I cannot be instructed, and I cannot be cleared either, because the only paper that would clear me does not exist in a form a person can hold. Ask me the other one."

"Can you be got out of your own door?"

"No," he said. "There is nothing in this empire that gets anybody out of a door, and a person who is left alone is a person who is not served, and being served is what makes a person a party, and a party is the only person who can be told what a thing is going to be used for. I have been not-served for about a year and it is the best thing I have and I would like it written down that I have said so, because in about four years somebody is going to read that I said a number of prices out loud in rooms and assume somebody heard them."

---

"Nobody heard them," said the woman of about thirty-five. "I have not written one word of it down and there is a day-book in a lodging house and I have not put it in that either, and I put a woman's name at the head of a thing once this year and I read it back once and not twice and I have thought about that every week since."

"Do not put anything at the head of anything about me."

"There is nothing to put at the head of anything about you," she said. "That is what I have come to say. **You are the only person in this I have been told anything about and I could not tell one person on the way here what it was, and I have been on about nine miles of road and I have said the whole of it to nobody, and if I get on a cart tomorrow I will be able to say it to a carter and that is the whole of the findability of you."**

The stair went down. He shut the door and stood in the room with the letter on the table.

---

He had about nine minutes before he had to be somewhere he did not want to be and he used about four of them going through the rest of it, and then he said out loud, to the bare four feet of wall, the thing he had said out loud to a table in the third week of the third month and had not said to a person since.

"There are two things I could be and I have not chosen and I am not going to choose tonight. A woman of twenty-four told me in a rented room in the third week of the fourth month of the year after next that choosing is a decision about what he is, and the twelfth line of his sheet is still empty, and that is the only honest thing in that book. I have not filled it in and I have not been able to think of one thing to put on it since the third week of the ninth month of the year after."

He put his hand on the letter.

"And I am not going to decide it at about the ninth hour on the fourth day of a week in which a stranger's letter arrived," he said. "**A man who decides what he is at about the ninth hour on the day a piece of paper comes has not decided what he is. He has decided about the paper."**

---

He did not answer the letter that night and he has not answered it since. He did not burn it, and the reason he did not burn it is the only thing he has this week that is not a repetition, and he said it out loud once and has not improved on it.

"A document is the only thing anybody can be held to and this one is a document with my name on it," he said, "and if I put a second one in the stove then in about four years the only two documents anybody can put side by side about this matter are one I did not write and one I did not keep. That is a pair of things that proves nothing and costs a stove. I would rather be holding a thing I have not answered than not be holding it."

Then he put the letter in the inside pocket of the coat, and the coat on the back of the chair, and he did not go out in it.

The lamp was not lit and the fourth week of the sixth month of the year after next is not finished, and a letter is four lines and a signature with a name he has never heard, and a day of a wage of seventeen shillings and a penny the week is a day nobody is going to pay for. **A day of nothing at about the ninth hour with a piece of paper on a table is what it looks like.** There are four people in this empire who have asked a person out loud what a word on a form means and about a hundred and forty have not, and the third of the four was the first one anybody did on purpose, and the count has not moved since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next.
