# Chapter 209: The Third Asking, At A Desk

She had been in this city since the third week of the fifth month of the year after and it was now the second week of the eleventh month, and she had been to four offices and two counters and one hearing room with a rail in it, and after the fourth office she stopped going to offices.

Nell Kest is twenty. She is a dye-house worker, which is what she is paid for, and she keeps the books of the eleven houses on a lane in a furnace town about four hundred and thirty miles up this river and of four other houses in three other districts, which is what she is not paid for. Her wage stopped in the second week of the third month and it is stopped now and the word for it is *held*. She has a sheet of her own with a heading she wrote herself and one day on it and she has told nobody about either.

The office of a seat is in a building off the old river road, about nine miles from a counter, in a room on the ground floor with a window about two feet wide and a pane out of it. There are eleven minute books on a shelf. A woman of about fifty-five is at a table, and she is not a lawyer and she is not a party and she is not a judge, and she has never been asked what any of it meant.

---

She stood in the doorway for about a minute and a half before she said anything, because she had come about four miles across this city to that door and she had rehearsed it the whole way and about a third of it had gone out of her head in the last stretch.

"You filled a box," said Nell Kest.

"I did."

"On the fourth day of the third week of the fifth month of this year. **And the box was for the person, institution or thing engaged in the matter. And you wrote a name in it, and the name was mine, and I did not know there was a box and I did not know there was a day, and about four hundred and thirty miles up this river a woman of about sixty walked down a lane with a sheet on a board and read it off a roll and told me, and I said no out loud in a room and it did not work.**"

"Yes," said Orla Denning. "All of that is correct. Sit down if you want to sit down."

"I will not sit down. I have come about four miles to ask a question and I am going to ask it standing up and I am going to be standing up when you answer it, and if I sit down I will be a person being received and I have been received by a great many people this year and I have not been asked one question by any of them."

---

Then she asked it, and it was the third time in about a hundred and forty years that one person in this empire had asked another person what a word on a form meant, and the first time anybody had asked on purpose.

"How did the box get filled. Not what it says. Not what a representative is, a clerk of thirty-four told me that at a counter in the second week of the ninth month and gave it to me for nothing and would not write it down, and I have it. I want the other half. I want to know how a person of twenty about four hundred and thirty miles away got into a box in a building she has never been in, by a hand she has never seen, out of a book she has never had, and I want it in a room and out loud, and I have been about six weeks working out that there is exactly one person in this empire who can tell me and she is about nine miles from the counter I stood at in the ninth month."

Orla Denning put her pen down.

Then she answered it, and it took about four minutes, and she answered it standing on the other side of a table with her hands flat on it, and she did not sit either.

"A return. A return is one printed sheet with a column on it and the column is headed for the person, institution or thing engaged in the matter, and it goes out to a district, and the district keeps a roll of households, and a keeper of a roll of households copies the column into her own book where a person can see it.

"The column is not filled in by the district. The column is filled in here, in this room, at this end, in advance, out of a roll, because a keeper cannot invent a person and cannot be asked what a word on a form means. When the office asks a district a question the district has no answer to, it gets back the only answer there is, and writes the answer down in a box with a day on it, and sends it, and the keeper copies it into a roll.

"The question the office asked your district was: the person of that family who is of age and resident. A roll of households does not say a person is of age and resident. It says a household is at a door. So what came back was a household, and the household that came back was yours, and the entry for it says a place of custom or of work and it does not say which of the women in it, and there is no box in this empire for the difference between a person and a trade."

---

"And you took the household," said Nell Kest, "and you put a person in it."

"I took what came back and I put it in a box that takes a thing as well as a person, and I did it in about nine seconds, and I was not asked, and nobody was asked, and I have written on the head of a file in this room that I did it and that nobody asked me and that I did it correctly, and the file is not in the press, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a clerk to put a file in a press."

"Why is the file not in the press."

"Because a file in the press is a file that a person can be told about, and being told about it is a service and being served is a party, and there is no way back out of a party," said Orla Denning. "And I have been a clerk in this room for nineteen years and I was not in a party before the fifth month of this year and I would like to stay out of one for as long as this building lets me, and that is a reason and it is not a good one and I have not improved it."

---

Then the woman of twenty asked the question she had come four miles for, which she had not said yet.

"How many of them are there."

"I cannot tell you."

"You are the office. You are the only office in this empire that does this. You must be able to tell me."

"I have kept eleven minute books for nineteen years and I have entered about four thousand things into them and every one of them is a thing a room decided, and a thing a room decided does not record the thing that was done to a person in about nine seconds in a box," said Orla Denning. "**The office keeps no register of them. That is not carelessness.** A register is a book of persons and this office has never kept a book of persons, and it never needed one, because the persons were in other districts' books and this office never had to look at them.

"So the number is not lost. The number has never been anywhere. It is in a roll of households in every district in this empire, and every one of them is about four hundred and thirty miles from this desk, and not one of them has a keeper in it who has been asked what the fourth column is for. And I would say to you, as one clerk to another, and I have never said anything of the kind to anybody in nineteen years, that there are two of you that I know of and that is not a number, it is a memory, and a memory of two is worse than a figure of none, because a figure of none is a thing a man can look for and never find, and a memory of two is a clerk of fifty-five in a room with a pane out of the window."

---

Then she said the last of it, standing up, at about the seventh hour, in a room with a pane out of the window, and nobody thanked her and nobody is going to.

"I am going to tell you the price of the next four minutes and then I am going to say them, and the price is the truth and it is not about me.

"**You have just asked a question about a form in a room, out loud, and you are twenty, and you are not a party to anything, and nobody is going to serve you with this, and there is no instrument anywhere in this empire that will let anybody tell you what I am about to say you cannot have.**

"A person who asks becomes answerable for the answer. Not you. Me. In about four years a man with a copy of the right page can put this room side by side with a sheet of my hand and ask a clerk of this office why the column was filled in at this end in advance, and she will have to answer it, and her name will be at the foot of it, and the answer will be that she was not asked in about nine seconds in the fifth month of this year, and there is no form anywhere that asks her whether she was asked. You have moved a thing from a book with no name on it to a room with two people in it, and that is the whole of what you have done, and the two people are a woman of fifty-five who has written nineteen years of minutes and a woman of twenty with a wage that is held and eleven houses in a book of her own that nobody pays her to keep, and she has been in this city since the third week of the fifth month, and neither of us can be protected and you have not gained anything at all except the four minutes."

"I know that," said Nell Kest. "I priced it in the fourth week of the fifth month to a woman of about sixty holding a board and she wrote it in a column and there was no box for a why and I have looked. I came four miles to be answerable for a question. That is the first thing anybody has ever let me be the price of and I am not going to be coy about it in a room."

---

Then she did not hand over the sheet.

"You want to see it," said Orla Denning, "because a day is the only part of it anybody can find, and you have a day, and you have been holding it since the fifth month, and you have not told me about it and I am not asking."

"You are not asking."

"A person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and I have known that since about the seventh month of next year and I have never been on the wrong side of it until this year."

"Keep it," said Nell Kest. "I am not giving it to anybody. It is a sheet of my own with a heading I wrote myself and there is one day on it and the day is the only part of the whole business that can be found, and the minute I give it to an office it becomes a record of a person in an office, and I have spent three years refusing to make records of people and I am not starting with myself."

---

Then she said what she had come to say, and it was a decision, and it was hers.

"I cannot get out of the box. You have not told me I can and I did not think anybody could, and I have asked four offices and two counters and a woman at a hearing and not one of them said the word *out* once.

"And I am twenty, and I am about four hundred and thirty miles from a lane with eleven doors on it, and my wage is held, and the only person who can tell me what the fourth column of that roll is for is a woman of about sixty who walks it once a year with a board and a basket, and nobody is going four hundred and thirty miles to ask her and there is no fund and no form for a fund, and I have said I was not going to add it up and I am not going to now.

"**So here is what I have decided and it is the only decision I have and I have had it since the ninth month. I cannot get out of the box, and I am going to be the person who knows how it was filled, and I am not going to be a person who knows it alone.** In this city there is a counter where a clerk of thirty-four told me what a representative is and would not write it down. Four hundred and thirty miles up this river there is a lane with a roll of households on it. About nine miles from here there is a man with a book and no office on it and eleven questions in it and a twelfth line he has left empty because there is no post in this empire whose job is to ask.

"That is three, and there is a fourth, and she is a clerk of thirty-eight at a window on a flood terrace about four hundred and thirty miles down this river, and she has never been asked anything either, and I am going to be asking four people instead of one, and there is no form for that and nobody is going to help me and I am not going to be thanked for it, because there is no form anywhere that requires a person to be thanked and I have said that out loud too many times this year."

Orla Denning did not write any of it down, and she said so.

"I am not going to write that down either, and I want to be exact about why, because you will think it is kindness and it is not. It is that a woman of fifty-five who writes a woman's plan in her own book in about nine seconds has done to you the identical thing that was done to you in the fifth month of this year, and I have had the two of them side by side since about the fourth week of the ninth month and I have not been able to put them down again.

"**The thing that happened to you was a document. If I write your plan down then you have been got at twice by two people who were not villains, and one of them is standing in front of you on the other side of a table.**"

"That is the answer to the question I have not asked you yet," said Nell Kest.

"What question."

"Whether you are frightened of having written the two lines on that file head," said Nell Kest. "You are. And it has cost you about four hundred and thirty miles of nothing and a tray, and you did it anyway, on a day, in a room, with nobody in it, and I have not got a word for what that is and I am not going to make one up at the end of a four-mile walk."

"I have a word for it," said Orla Denning, "and the word is *expensive*, and you are twenty and you have not got the four years I have got, and I would like you to go and be asked four questions and not one of them here."

"Sit down if you want to sit down," she said. "The room is not going anywhere and neither am I, and I have a few minutes before the tray comes down this corridor, and there is a chair on that side with nothing on it."
