# Chapter 222: The Day He Did Not Ask Her Anything

He came on the fourth day of the second week of the third month of the year after next, at about the ninth hour, and he had nothing in his hands, and she noticed the nothing in his hands before he had got to the rail.

Tamsin Rook is twenty-four. She is a party to an instrument about a dead woman's name. Two instruments require her in any room he is in and she has never once merged them and he has never once merged them, and a form gets merged by being inconvenient: the first is a paper made in a form in four days with a day on it, and the second is a watch that does not name him and does not name her and requires only that a person who is not from the office that cannot be named be present. About six hundred people in this city could satisfy the watch. She has satisfied it every time it has been called for and nobody has ever paid her for it.

She has a bay with a press in it and about six hundred printed slips in the press that say the fourth line is not to be completed by this office, and a counter with a board over it that has four things on it and none of them is time, and a rail, and a bay door that is open because it is about the ninth hour in the third month.

---

"You have come four times since the first month," said Tamsin Rook.

"Five."

"Five," she said. "And you have not asked me anything in any of the five, and I have got a reason for every one of the five, and I have not asked you for any of them, and I am not going to, and I have thought about that for about four days and I want to say it out loud before you say anything else, because if you say anything else first then this is not a thing I said."

He put his hands in his pockets, which is what a man does when he has come to a counter and been told he has come to a counter.

"I have been working out why you are not going to ask me," said Tamsin Rook, "and I have got it, and it is not the reason you think, and I am going to say it and then I am going to ask you a plain question and you are going to answer it or not."

---

"You asked me in the second week of the ninth month of the year after whether a thing in a room without a person in it can be asked to have done a thing," he said. "And I have not asked you, and you knew I would not, and you have been waiting for me to come and explain."

"I have not been waiting for anything. I have been serving people at a counter."

"You have been waiting for about four days."

"I have been counting the days," said Tamsin Rook, "and that is a different thing and I will not be moved off it by a man who counts."

---

"Here it is. The only question I have got in this empire that is worth anything is the one about an office and whether an office can be made to account for a thing it did in the fifth month of the year after. I have had it since the first week of the seventh month and I have not asked it of you, and you are going to hear the shape of it and not the whole of it, because I am not giving you the whole of it."

"**If I asked it in this bay you would have to answer it, and answering it would make you a person somebody has answered, and in about four years a man with a copy of the right page could put a line under your name beside a sheet with a clerk's name at the foot of it.** And that would be the second time somebody in this matter was found by a thing they said in a room. The first time was a woman of twenty, who is answerable and cannot be un-answered, and she has four lines at the back of a book of eleven houses that nobody pays her to keep, and she does not know that I know about them."

"And I am not going to be the reason there are two. You are the only person in this matter who is answerable and cannot be found, and the reason you cannot be found is that nobody was ever going to have to look you up, and **that is the same sentence as the reason the office of a seat cannot count its own entries, and I would like it noticed that the reason a man is safe and the reason a room is helpless are the same sentence and neither of them was designed.**"

---

He did not say anything for about a minute, and she let him, and she has thought about the length of that minute since.

"I have written a choice down twice now," he said, "in order to be refused, and been refused twice, and the second time you told me the answer before I had finished the question."

"Yes."

"**This is the third time and it is the first one I have not written down at all, and I am going to tell you that now, in a room, out loud, with the day on it, and you are going to enter it, because a correct not-doing is a thing and there is no form for it and I am not going to be the only person who knows it happened.**"

"The rule of the twelfth month of last year," said Tamsin Rook. "Either of us puts a choice in writing with a date on it and the other must answer it in a room in words with a date."

"I am not putting it in writing. I am putting the not-putting in writing, and that is a different thing, and I have checked with nobody because there is nobody to check with, and I am telling you that is what I have done."

---

She took her own book out from under the counter. It is not the court's book and has no office on it and has her hand in it in about nine places, and she opened it to a page with nothing on it and wrote a line on it and turned it round.

> *The fourth day of the second week of the third month of the year after next. A choice was not put in writing and no question was asked, at about the ninth hour, in a bay, by the person it would have been put to, and the reason given out loud, and no form anywhere in this empire requires a not-doing to be entered. — M.K.*

"Read it," she said.

He read it twice.

"The second reading is slower," said Tamsin Rook, "and it is always slower, and you have never once asked me why and I have never once told you, and it is because the second reading is the one that finds a thing I have got wrong, and it has found one about four times in two years and I have never once told you about those either."

"You are telling me now."

"I am telling you that I am telling you now, and that is different, and I am not going to improve it in a bay at about the ninth hour."

---

Then a man came in for a copy of a form at fourpence and she had to serve him, and serving him took about two minutes, and he did not ask her anything, and she has never once in this matter had a person stand at a counter she was serving and ask her a question.

That is not the point of any of this and it is the reason for half of it. **A room gets a day.** A person can come up to a counter, be served in it, ask in it, and walk out of it, and there is nothing on the board under the rail and no fee and no form that says what may be asked at it and none that says what may not.

When he had gone she put the book down on the counter face up, which she does about twice a year, and said:

"That is the count, and you have not heard it this month, and I am going to say it because you are the only person in this city who would know if I had got it wrong."

"Four," he said.

"**Four, and it has been four since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next, and it was four before that, and the first three are a court room in the fifth month of the year after that wanted a record, a clerk's window in the first week of the tenth, and a desk in the second week of the eleventh that a woman of twenty walked about four miles to.** The fourth is a rented room about nine miles from that same office of a seat, and the same woman walked it in one day, and nobody sent her, and she did it on her own account. Two of the four were accidents and nobody chose either of them. The third was the first one anybody did on purpose. And in the ten weeks since the fourth, nobody has asked anybody anything."

"That is a long time."

"It is ten weeks and it is not a finding about people," said Tamsin Rook. "It is a finding about what happens when a thing happens once. **There is no way to want to know a thing you have not heard about and there is no way to hear about it, and the only way anybody found out about the first three was to be in a room where somebody else was asking.** And I have been at this counter every working day since the second day of the first week of the first month and about nine people a year want to ask me something and not one of them has opened their mouth, and I have said the reason out loud four times now, once in the ninth month of the year after and once in the eleventh and once in the first month of the year after next and once in the second, and I have not improved it once."

---

Then the door at the back of the bay opened and Marn Ottery came in with her hat still on, which she does when she is coming in for a form and not for anything else, and she is a clerk of thirty-four at the fourth counter of the Notaries' Table, which is the one for scale instruments, and she came to this bay four bays along because this bay has a form in the press that her press does not have.

"Two hundred of them," said Marn Ottery.

"Sixty," said Tamsin Rook.

"That is not the number I came for and you know it is not."

"It is not," said Tamsin Rook, "and I have not got the authority to let a form go out of this building without the line under it filled in, and the line under it is not to be completed by this office, and about six hundred of them are in that press and nobody has ever had the nerve to say that sentence out loud to a person in a room."

Marn Ottery put her hat on the counter.

"Then I have come for nothing, which is the first time in eleven years I have come to this bay for nothing, and I would like it noted that I am not going to be paid for this and I am not going to be thanked for it either, and I have read the same four lines you read in the fourth week of the first month and I have got the same answer, and there is no fund in this city for either of us."

"There is no form anywhere in this empire for a fund," said Tamsin Rook, "and I have looked for one in about nine hundred buildings and there is not one, and you have looked, and we are two people in this city who have both looked."

---

The two of them stood at the counter for about four minutes and did not say anything, and then Marn Ottery said:

"I heard a clerk of about fifty-five say the price out loud to a man with a tray in the second day of the first week of this month."

"You heard it from a carrier."

"I heard it from a carrier," said Marn Ottery, "who is one of about nine people a day who carry things up and down that corridor and who has never asked anybody anything in his life, and he told me about it in a street for nothing, and he did not know what he was carrying and neither do I."

Tamsin Rook looked at the counter.

"Then it is in a street," she said, "and there is no form anywhere in this empire that puts a street in a book, and that is the first time in this matter that a thing has got out of a room for nothing and landed in a person who is not paid to carry it, and I have not got a rule about that and I am not going to make one at about the tenth hour."

---

Marn Ottery went, and he had not said one word through the whole of it, and then the bay was quiet again for about nine minutes.

"You did not ask her either," he said.

"I did not ask her anything and she did not ask me anything and we both said the price out loud to each other for nothing," said Tamsin Rook, "and that is the second instrument anybody in this matter has built in about nine weeks and neither of us appointed the other and neither of us wrote it down, and I am not going to say what it is called, because whatever it is called it will get a form inside four years and then it will be a machine and it will need a person to be told they may come."

She shut the book.

"And there are two boxes in this one that I ruled by hand and filled in with the same name, and they are not the paper and they are not the watch, and the paper and the watch are two instruments I have never once merged. **I have not ruled a third and I am not going to**, and a person who rules a box for everything that happens to her in a year has turned her own book into a form, and I would not be the first clerk in this city to do that and I would like to be the last."

"You have never once asked me whether I have decided," said Marek Kest.

"**No. And you have never once asked me, and both of those are the same silence, and it has been going on a long time, and you were not going to ask me this morning and I was not going to be asked and there is nobody going to be disappointed about that, including me.** Two pieces of evidence is not a decision and three is not either, and I am not going to be the one who says it is."

The door of the bay was open and the board over the rail had four things on it and none of them was a question, and a choice had not been put in writing in a book with no office on it, and a clerk of fifty-five had put a name on a question in about nine seconds in a room four bays and a corridor away, and there is no post in this empire whose job is to ask, and the count is four.
