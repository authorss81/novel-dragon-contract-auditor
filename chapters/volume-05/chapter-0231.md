# Chapter 231: The Column With No Names In It

The hired stone store off the Slade is about four hundred yards from a person who can be served, and the north end of it is about nine foot by eleven with a bench along the north wall, and the rent of the building comes out of the book four times a year at a shilling a quarter.

Rennick Adley is thirty-eight and he is not a clerk. He has been keeping the books of the Slade Cut Company since the second month of next year, and the Company's whole money is four shillings a year and all of it is the rent of the building, and he has a fair hand and he can count and nobody has ever once asked him for either.

He put the roof there on purpose. He entered the rent himself, in his own hand, in one line, in the second month of next year, and read it back twice, and he has not entered it since.

---

The works money is counted once a week, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, in that store, by him, alone, out loud. About forty-one people are on the cut and about nineteen of them have said no to this season, out loud, one after another, on a day, and they are still behind the bank. The hold against the company has not moved since the fourth week of the fourth month of the year after. The water was a foot and a bit off the mark where it had been in the third month.

He took the rent off first, because the rent is the only thing the company owes anybody. Then he counted what was left.

What was left was about a shilling and a bit.

He counted it twice and then he did the thing he has done every week since the second month of next year, which is to count the days.

---

A man of about thirty from the second gang came in at about the fifth hour with his hat in his hand and asked whether he would be on the list after the season shut.

It was a plain question and not a question about a word on a form, and Rennick Adley said so before he answered it, because he has been asked to mark that kind of thing since the first week of the first month of the year after next and he does it out loud now.

"You are asking me a plain question and I am going to answer it as one," he said, "and if it turns into a question about a word on a paper I am going to stop in the middle of it and tell you."

"Go on."

"I cannot tell you. The list of works is made in a bay in about four minutes and the foreman of this cut reads it out and I write it down. It is a list of works and not a list of persons. I have never had it in my hands before it is read and I have never got it afterwards, and I could not tell you tomorrow whether you were on it or not without looking."

---

"You have written my name at the foot of a line four or five times since the second month of next year."

"I have."

"So you know."

"I know what I wrote down. I do not know what the foreman said, and I write down what she says and not what she means, and that is not a trick and it is not modesty. It is the whole of the job."

The man put his hat on and then took it off again.

"Four seasons," he said. "I have been on this cut four seasons and nobody has ever asked me one question about whether I am on the list, and I have never asked anybody either, and I have worked out that the reason neither of us asks is that the answer is the same either way and it is the list working."

"That is a good sentence and I am not going to improve it," said Rennick Adley, "and I am not going to write it down either, **and the reason is that it is about a person and I have not got a column that will take a person.**"

---

The man put his hand flat on the ledger where it was shut on the bench, the way a man does when he has decided he has a right to a thing, and Rennick Adley put two fingers on the cover and did not move it and did not say anything for about two seconds.

"That is forty-one names," he said. "Not now. Not in about four years when a person with a copy of the right page is stood in a room and asks whose writing this is."

"I was not going to open it."

"You were going to put your thumb on the first page and I could see that from here," said Rennick Adley, "and there is nothing at all wrong with a man wanting to see a page. There is only nobody in this empire more findable than a hand, and that is a thing I have had to say out loud to about four people this year and I have said it to a clerk of thirty-four at a counter in a different county and she said it first."

---

Then the man asked his second question, and it was the one Rennick Adley had been waiting for without knowing he was waiting for it, and it was not about a word on a form either.

"Then whose name is at the foot of the line and why is it not written the way a man would write it."

Rennick Adley put the pencil down.

"Say the rest of that."

"The man of fifty-five with the chain. His name is on the list and it is not his hand. I have watched about four hundred names go onto about four hundred lines and every one of them is written by somebody, and I have never once seen him write his own, and I have never asked him how a man gets a name onto a line in a hand that is not his own."

"Then what is your name doing on that list?"

"It is doing what it is doing," said the man. "That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer there is and I am going to give you the rest of it in about four seconds and I am not going to write it down. I write the list because a foreman reads forty-one names out loud in a bay in about four minutes and somebody has to hold them. That is the whole of what I am for on this cut. **I am a man who is paid to hold forty-one names in a hand for about four minutes, and I have never once been paid for a day of anything else, and there is nothing anywhere in this empire that a man can be paid for a day of unless it is a day of works on a list."**

---

The man went out and the lamp was not lit, and it was later than it ought to be, and Rennick Adley sat on the bench with the ledger shut on his knee and did the thing he had been not doing for about four weeks.

**He is not on the list, and he has never been on the list.** A list of works is a list of works, **and a book-keeper does not do works**, and there is a name at the foot of every line of the list of works in a fair hand, and one of those names is not his and never has been. He put the list together himself, in the second month of next year, in a fair hand, one name under another, on a day, and he has kept it that way ever since, and he did it on purpose, and the reason he did it on purpose is a good one.

**A name in a column is a thing a person can be served at.** A list of works with forty-one names on it goes into a book in a building four hundred and thirty miles down this river, in a company book with about nine hundred and forty names in it in nine hands over about sixty years. About four hundred of the nine hundred and forty are dead, **and the newest hand in that book is his.** A person on a list of works is findable in about four years by anybody with a copy of the right page. A person who is not on a list of works is not findable that way at all.

So he did not put himself on it. And that is correct, and he has never once wished it were otherwise, and it is the reason his wage is held against a roof of four shillings a year that he put there himself with a pencil in about four seconds.

---

What he could not get round, and what he had been going round since the second day of the first week of the fifth month of the year after next, **was the arithmetic of it.**

His wage is not a wage on the list. It is what is left of the works money after the rent of the building has come off the top, and the works money is what forty-one people take, and about nineteen of them are not taking anything this season. And what is left is a shilling and a bit at the end of a week, and a shilling and a bit is not a wage and never has been and there is no form in this empire by which a book-keeper can be paid out of a shilling and a bit.

**And the days he spends on the numbers of other people are not paid at all.** Not the day he read a man's own paper to him in a yard in the first week of the twelfth month of the year after. **Not the day a foreman came out of the store and stood at the end of the bench and said a question out loud and he did not write it down.** Not the nine days he spent working out how to ask about four people who cannot read a paragraph, and finding out the way, and deciding not to, and writing that down with a day on it and reading it back twice. Not today.

"There is nothing in this empire that will pay me for a day I do not do works," he said out loud, to the store, "and a foreman of fifty-one on this bank has known that since a day in the sixth year of a guild's blank list, and nobody has ever told me, and I have not found out until this afternoon."

---

Then he opened the ledger to the entry, which was the last one he made in the second day of the first week of the fifth month of the year after next, and read it, and it is this.

*A girl of seventeen is on this list of works at the rate the list is set at, on a written engagement, on her fourth day. She has not been thanked. Nobody has thanked her.*

There is no reason under it. It is the first entry in that ledger since the second month of next year with no reason under it and there are about four hundred more entries in it than that, and every one of the other four hundred and some has got a reason, and the reasons are his, and he read them going back, and they are good.

He had been four weeks trying to find one for hers.

Then he saw why there is not one, and it took him about four seconds, and it is the simplest thing anybody has worked out in that store in about a year.

**A reason under an entry about a person needs a day, and a day about a person needs a name, and there is not one name in that column.**
The third column of that bay's own ledger has about nineteen reasons in it with a day against every one of them and no names in that column at all. The names are in the company book in a different building four hundred and thirty miles down this river in nine hands over about sixty years. A name cannot be turned back into a reason and a reason cannot be turned back into a day. There is nothing anywhere in this empire that joins those two books.

**So there is one entry in four hundred with a name at the foot of it, and it is the one he cannot say anything about.** **He can say anything he likes about a day, because a day has nobody in it.** The moment there is a person in a line, he has nothing to put underneath it, and that is not a fault in the reason and it is not him being careful. It is the shape of a book he made on purpose in the second month of next year.

---

And then he said out loud, to a store with a stove in it, the thing that is the whole of the four weeks he has been in.

"A thing that leaves no mark can go four hundred yards," he said. "I did it in a yard in the first week of the twelfth month of the year after and I have done it twice since and a man of fifty-five has had two pieces of his own paper read to him and nothing written down at all, and in about four years there will be a book with about nine hundred and forty names in it in nine hands and a day against one line of it and no man attached to the line."

"**A thing that leaves no mark can go four hundred yards.** It cannot go into a book, and a wage is in a book, and that is the whole of the week."

He shut the ledger.

---

The rest of it was where it has been since the seventh month of the year after and none of it moved this week.

A factor's letter of four lines was on that bench where it has been since the second week of the third month of the year after, wanting the name of a person who may be served and that person's consent, and it says without both that it cannot proceed and will not proceed, and it is not going to be answered, and every day it is not answered is a day nobody has been entered.

A bill at Lowcross is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and is unpaid and nobody is liable, and there has not been a line for a bridge in that fund in nineteen years, and he is not going to pay it and nobody in that store is going to pay it.

A guarantee is standing offered and unanswered on about four hundred and forty foot of that bank with about sixty children under sixteen inside it, and he has not got the terms of it in his head and he is not going to read them out in a store at about the sixth hour.

About four people in that bay cannot read a paragraph and have never been asked anything, and the finding he entered in his own hand in the second week of the tenth month of the year after stands, and he has not re-entered it, and he has not improved it, and it is not his to improve.

And about four hundred and thirty miles down this river there is a man of thirty-eight with no office and no fund and a bar of his own that came off him on the twelfth of the ninth month of last year on an application of one line which he wrote himself. There is a line about two inches long on the inside of his left wrist and it reads nothing, and he is the only person in this matter that anybody can go looking for and come back from with nothing. About four hundred and thirty miles up that same river there is a woman's whole wage sitting in a weigh-house ledger with her wage in it and not her name under it, held by a man he has never met, against a thing he is not in a position to pay.

Four askings in about a hundred and forty years and no post anywhere in this empire whose job is to ask, and the count has not moved since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next, and the third of the four was the first one anybody did on purpose, and it is the end of the first week of the sixth month of the year after next and the count is four and the lamp is not lit.
