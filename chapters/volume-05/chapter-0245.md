# Chapter 245: A Sheet Of Paper With A Day On It And Nothing Else, And Three Papers On A Bench

The district works gets its post on a counting day and about four of the men in that bay have been getting it there for nine years, and it comes in a bundle, and the bundle goes on the sill outside the bay door from about the sixth hour until somebody goes in and gets it.

On the second day of the first week of the tenth month of the year after next, in the wet, a man of about thirty came out of that bay and picked up a bundle of about nine things off that sill and brought it in, and he put it on the bench along the north wall because there was nowhere else in a bay about nine foot by eleven to put a wet bundle.

Then he stood there and waited, because a man who has brought nine things into a room and does not know whether to hand them over or put them down is in the ordinary position of about nine men in this matter every week. Halla Wray told him to put them on the bench and go home, which he did, and she has thought since that she did that to nine men in nine years and to one man in particular on a day about four years ago and has not worked out which of them she did it to for the sake of it.

**Halla Wray is fifty-one and she is the foreman of the Slade Cut** and she has been in a box since the first week of the seventh month of the year after and she told a bay about that on the second day of the second week of the ninth month of the year after next, and about nine men heard her and not one of them has said a word about it since, which is what she expected and which is the correct thing.

---

She went through the bundle at about the sixth hour with the lamp not lit, and eight of the nine were ordinary, and the ninth was not.

A sheet of foolscap, folded once, and it was wet through at the fold, and there was no name at the top of it and there was no name at the foot of it.

She held it up to the window. There was nothing on the back.

**There was a day on it, and nothing else, and the day was the fourth day of the fourth week of the eighth month of the year after next**, and a day with nobody's name on it is not a summons to anybody.

---

She read it four times, and the fourth reading was slower, and that is a rule that belongs to a man about four hundred and thirty miles down this river and not to her, and she has never told anybody where she got it and is not going to.

Then she did the ordinary thing, which is that she looked at the fold.

A piece of paper folded by a man is folded with the crease at the bottom and a piece of paper folded by a woman is folded with the crease at the top. She has folded about nine hundred pieces of paper in her life and she has opened about nine hundred that other people had folded, and she did not have to be told that and nobody told her.

And she put it face down on the bench and did not say a word for about two minutes, and the men in that bay did not notice a thing, because a foreman looking at a piece of paper is a foreman doing accounts and there is a whole set of about four such things in a week.

---

Then she went up the road, about four hundred yards, to the hired stone store, and there is a bench along the north wall in there too, and a book-keeper of thirty-eight was not on it because the lamp was not lit and it was earlier than he came.

There were two things on that bench and there had been for a long time.

A notice of six numbered lines with a device cut into the head of it and a seal at the foot of it and nothing under the seal, on the bench since the seventh month of the year after. And a factor's letter of four lines, wanting the name of a person who may be served and that person's consent, and it says without both that it cannot proceed and will not proceed, and it is not going to be answered, and every day it is not answered is a day nobody has been entered.

She put the third one down beside the other two.

Three pieces of paper on a bench about four hundred yards from nine men, and not one of the three can be answered, and two of the three are asking for a name and the third one has not got one to ask for.

---

She stood there and worked out why the third one had been on a sill in the wet for about four weeks, and the answer took her about four seconds and it is the whole of that day.

**A thing with no name on it cannot be served on anybody** and a thing that cannot be served on anybody cannot be opened, because opening it is a thing a person does to a thing that has been given to them, and nobody has been given this one. The district works gets about nine pieces a counting day and hands them out to men whose names are on about nine sheets, and there is no sheet with her name on it, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says what a person does with a thing that has no name on it. So it sat on a sill in the wet for about four weeks and got softer and softer and it came inside today because a man of about thirty was in a bay and the sill was wet.

That is not anybody's fault. She wants that said, because she is a foreman and a foreman's first job when something has gone wrong on her bank is to find out who did it wrong, and there is nobody, and that is not a scandal, it is a machine.

---

Then she read it again, standing up, and worked out what it was, and it took her longer, and it took her most of the walk back down the road.

**A day with a person's name on it is a summons.** A day with nothing on it at all is not a summons and is not an offer and is not a request. It is a fact about a day, put into a building about four hundred and thirty miles from the day, by a person nobody in that building can name, and there is nothing in this empire that can make anybody answer a fact.

The fourth day of the fourth week of the eighth month of the year after next. A day about six weeks gone. Nothing about what a person is going to do and nothing about what a person would like done, and no address, and no request, and no name.

"**A person who is paid to read is a person who can be told to stop,**" she said out loud, to that bay, on a counting day, and then she stopped dead, because that sentence is not hers, and she has never once said it in that bay, and there were men in it.

---

"It was not for you," she said. "That was out loud and it was in the wrong room and I am not going to say it again this year."

"You have said it in a shed," said a man of about thirty from the second gang. "In the sixth month. In front of nine of us."

"I know exactly where I said it and I know exactly who was in it and I am not going to be asked about it by a bay, because a bay is not a room and a room is not a book and there is no form anywhere in this empire that carries a thing from one of those to another." She had her hand on the bench and she did not pick the paper up. "**And I have been carrying that sentence since a foreman of about forty said it to me in a shed in the second week of the sixth month and I did not know then that it was a load-bearing sentence**, and I have worked that out in about a second and a half, and I am not going to say what it has got under it, because if I say what is under it then about nine men in this bay are standing on it."

---

And then the part that was the real work of the day, and it was not about the paper at all.

A man of about twenty-six came in at about the seventh hour and asked her, standing where he was, whether a person could be put on a list of works by somebody who was not the foreman of it, and she said no. He said he had heard of a man who was, and she said that man was a book-keeper and a book-keeper does not do works, and the reason his name is in a hand that is not his is that somebody wrote it, and there is no form in this empire that says who wrote a name in a column or why.

"Then how would anybody know?"

"Nobody would know, and that is the correct arrangement, and about four people on this bank have worked that out this year and four of them are not on a list at all and there is no way in this empire to find out which four, and I am not going to help."

---

And she thought, on the road, at about four hundred yards, about the crease, and about the four hundred yards, and about the fact that a thing which leaves no mark can cross four hundred yards and cannot get into a book, and about four weeks of thinking had not produced anything else.

**And this is a thing that has gone about four hundred and thirty miles and it has left one mark**, and the mark is a crease at the bottom of a piece of paper, and a crease is not a name and a crease cannot be served on anybody and there is no instrument in this empire that could put a crease and a person together.

A thing that leaves no mark can go four hundred yards and cannot go into a book, and a book-keeper said that to a store with a stove in it in the first week of the sixth month of the year after next, and a foreman heard it in a bay about four seconds after she said it, and about four people in this city have heard it since.

She stood in the middle of that bay and worked out, standing up, in about four seconds, what she was the only person alive who could be given evidence about.

Somebody in this city has sent a piece of paper to a bank about four hundred and thirty miles down this river, and has not put a name at the top of it or a name at the foot of it, and has folded it with the crease at the bottom, and has paid for it out of their own money out of about forty-four pounds a year with no fund and no form anywhere in this empire for a fund.

And the only thing about that which could be put in a column is the fold, and a column is the thing that turns a day into a man, and she is not going to be the one who does it, and she has told nobody, and she is not going to.

---

**And she is not going to answer it, and here is why, and it is the reason she gave for the factor's letter** and the reason has got about four weeks older and no better.

The factor's letter can be refused by doing nothing, and it is being refused by doing nothing, and that is a decision, and a decision is a thing a person can be told about. This one cannot be refused, because there is nothing in it to refuse, and a thing that can only be ignored is a thing that has been put into a room where it cannot do anything.

And that is what it was for. She knows that without being told, in the way a foreman knows what a bay is going to do before the bay has done it. Somebody about four hundred and thirty miles down this river has put a day on a piece of paper and posted it to a place where it could not be answered, and has not asked for anything, and that is the whole of it, and the whole of it is the right size to be a thing a person can be told to stop.

**And she has not been asked to do anything, and a person who is not asked has not been given a duty**, and that is the oldest sentence in this matter and it is the only one she has never once had to think about.

---

A man of about twenty-six came back in at about the seventh hour and asked whether she was all right, and she said she was, and he said she had not said anything for the better part of a quarter of an hour, and she said there had been nothing to say, and he said there was a difference between nothing to say and not saying it, and went back to his vat.

"That is the whole of the work," said Halla Wray. "Everything in this bay is that. There is a difference between a thing that has not been said and a thing that has not been said out loud, and I have been in this bay nine years and I have never once been able to put a value on the difference, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that asks a person to put a value on it."

Then she did the ordinary things, because a foreman who stops doing the ordinary things is a foreman who has begun to make a speech of her own job.

A man of about fifty asked whether the hold would be on the list before the season shut and she told him it would not and why in about four seconds, and he said that was not what he meant, and she said it was the only meaning it has. A man of about fifty came in and asked about the third link of a chain, and she told him about the third link and the week before it wants oil, in about two minutes, out loud, without turning round, from behind the bench, and the men in that bay watched a foreman do a perfectly ordinary thing to a man who cannot read a paragraph and not one of them noticed anything at all.

**And the lamp was not lit, and the three pieces of paper were on a bench about four hundred yards up the road**, and the guarantee is standing offered and unanswered on about four hundred and forty foot of that bank with about sixty children under sixteen inside it. About nineteen people are still behind it, and about four people in that bay have still never been asked one question, and a reader of seventeen is at the back of it and has not been thanked and is not going to start, and nobody was sent to anybody, and nobody came.
