# Chapter 225: The Third Time He Came Back And Asked Her Nothing

The window is on the ground floor and it has a sill you can put a book on, and there is a basket under the sill that is about nine inches deep, and the corridor behind it goes away from the window for about four hundred yards with four presses along it, and about nine people go past this window in an hour and not one of them has ever asked her anything.

Ivet Sarn is thirty-eight. She has been at this window for nine years. The court keeps a roll of households of the district for the water, and the roll says a household is at a door, and that is all it has ever said, and it is on the face of the form.

He came on the second day of the first week of the fourth month of the year after next, at about the tenth hour, and she knew him before he had got to the sill, and she has thought about the fact that she knew him since the first week of the tenth month of the year after and he has been coming ever since.

He put a slip down with a reference on the top of it and gave four words for it, and she looked at the reference and said fourpence and made a copy on a sheet of press in about a minute and a half, and she read the copy out, and he said it was right, and he paid fourpence.

That is the whole of the transaction. It is the ordinary trade at that window and about four hundred of those are bought and filled in in a year and not one of them is a question.

Then he did not go.

---

"You have moved again," said Ivet Sarn.

"Third time."

"Out of the district?"

"Over the boundary. A street and a river and a different water office. The woman at the door told me inside a week that the water had changed and that I would want to know what the water wanted."

"And you did not go and ask her."

"I did not go and ask her," said the man. "I have a wife and a boy and I have been to a window about four times since the first week of the tenth month and I have never once got an answer out of a room, and I have got a great deal out of a window, and that is a different thing and I have stopped being able to tell them apart."

---

"Then ask me the question you have come for," said Ivet Sarn, "and I am going to say the price of it first, and I am going to say it slowly, and it is the third time I have said it to you and I have not improved it once and I am not going to start."

"I am not going to ask you about a word on a form."

"Then what are you here for."

"**A man in two districts is a man in two districts, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a person may only be in one.** Your household is at a door, and it was at a door on a street with a shop on it, and it is at a door over the boundary, and both of those doors are real and both of them have people sleeping at them, and this court's roll has one of them in it and the other court's roll has the other one, and the two rolls are in two buildings with a street and a river between them, and there is nothing anywhere in this empire that joins them."

"About nine hundred districts," said Ivet Sarn, "and this court sends one return to every one of them and a return goes to one district and not to two, and a man can be at a door in four districts in four rolls in this empire and be on none of them in the sense he means, and I told you that in the first week of the tenth month of the year after and it has taken you until the first week of the fourth month of the year after next to be able to do arithmetic about it."

---

"That is a plain question," said the man, "is it not. You have not asked me anything about a word on anything."

"It is a plain question and I have said so out loud in this room with you in it, and the count of plain questions is a different count and is not printed anywhere, and I have said that to you before and I am saying it again because you have not been at this window since the third week of the twelfth month of the year after. **The count of the other kind is four, and it has been four since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next, and nobody has asked anybody anything since, and there is no post in this empire whose job is to ask, and I am not going to be one and I have told you that you may ask and you have not asked and I am not going to be thanked for saying it.**"

"You told me in the second month of the year after next that I could ask you anything."

"I told a man of about sixty at a counter four bays and a corridor and a river away that he could, and I do not know a thing about that man, and I am not going to pretend I gave that to you as well, because you did not ask and I have not offered and I would like both of those noticed."

---

Then he asked the thing she had known he would ask, and he had not asked it in the first week of the tenth month, and he had not asked it in the third week of the twelfth, and he asked it now, and it was not about a word on a form either.

"What is in the basket."

Ivet Sarn did not answer him for about a minute, and the minute was not caution, and the reason was the reason she gave herself in the first week of the tenth month, and it was not caution and she has never once said it was caution.

"I told you in the third week of the twelfth month of the year after what is in it," she said, "and I have not told anybody else and I am not going to tell anybody else, and I am not going to take one out, and the reason is the same reason as last time and I am going to say it again in the same words because I have improved on nothing since the twelfth month."

"**There is one in that basket about a street with a shop on it, and if it goes into a hand then in about four years there is a person who can put it beside a sheet of mine and ask me what the difference is between the two, and that person would not be a stranger. That person would be you, and you would be doing it about yourself and about your wife and your boy, and I have got no way of stopping you and I would not want to.**"

"I am not going to ask you for one."

"I know you are not," said Ivet Sarn. "**You are the only person in this empire who has come back to this window three times and asked me nothing, and a man who comes back and asks nothing is the only kind of person it is safe to tell anything to at all, and I would like that written down somewhere and I am not going to write it anywhere.**"

---

"There is a fourth column in your own book," he said, "on the top shelf of the second press, and it has had nothing over it for nine years."

"You have been in the corridor."

"I have been in the corridor four hundred times in nine years and I can read, which is the one thing about me that has not changed since the ninth month of the year after, and I have never once asked you about it because I did not want to be the man who came to a window to say a thing about a column, and a clerk's own column is not a thing a customer has got."

"**Then you have been the right kind of man for nine years and you are the first one to have noticed it, and nobody is going to thank you for noticing it and I am not going to.**"

"And you have never filled it in."

"I have not filled it in and I have never told anybody why and I have had nine years to work out an answer and I have not got one, and the reason I have never told anybody is that I do not know, and a person who cannot say why has no business putting a heading over a thing."

---

He looked at the window for a while and then said the obvious thing, which is the thing nobody has ever said to her about it, and she had been waiting nine years to hear it from somebody who was not paid to say it.

"Then nobody is ever going to fill it in."

"Yes," said Ivet Sarn.

"That is a waste of a column."

"That is a waste of a column and it is the correct thing to be done with it and I would like you to go away now, and I am not being rude, and I have got about four people a day at this window and I have got a tray going up that corridor inside a minute."

---

He went about nine steps up the corridor and then stopped, the way a man stops, and did not turn round, and said:

"That door is open."

It was, a couple of inches, on the left of the corridor about halfway along, and it has been every working day for nine years, and she has been past it about two thousand times, and there is nothing in it that anybody has ever told her about.

**A person who opens a room nobody has asked her about is a person who has given herself a duty, and a person who has given herself a duty cannot stop.** She has got enough of those in nine years and one of them is the reason there is a paper about a winter in a drawer in her own room that she has never told the court about.

"I am not going to go in there," said Ivet Sarn.

"You were not going to go in there when I said it."

"I was not going to go in there before you said it and I am not going to go in there now, and you have not asked me to and I want that noticed, because a man who stands in a corridor and does not ask a question is doing me a favour and there is no form in this empire for that either and I have got about nine of them to my account and not one of them is a debt I can pay back."
---

Then she went up the corridor, which she does about four times a day, to the second press, because a man of about fifty had asked for a second copy of a thing he had already had and had not given a reason for and she was not entitled to one.

The corridor is about four hundred yards long and there are four presses along it and the presses hold about four hundred returns a year between them, bought and filled in, and the majority of them are a household at a door and the day and the water, and about four hundred a year at this window and about four hundred a year in the presses, and none of the two four hundreds is a question and nobody has ever asked either of them for anything.

And on the way back she stopped at the door a couple of inches open, halfway along, and she stood there for about four seconds, and she did not go in, and there is nothing in that room that anybody has ever told her about, and she has got a drawer in her own room with a paper in it about a winter that she has never told the court about, and she knows what it is to have opened a room nobody asked her about, and she knows what it costs in about four years.

She came back down the corridor and put the second copy on the sill and read it out and the man of about fifty said it was right and paid fourpence again and went.

"He asked for it twice," said the man of about thirty-four, from the window.

"He does that about nine times a year and I have never asked him why and I am not going to start."

"Would it tell you anything."

"It would tell me that a man of about fifty wanted a second piece of paper for about fourpence, and that is a different thing, and it is the only thing it would tell me, and I have nine years of nine of those a year and not one of them has ever been worth the asking." She put the copy face down on the sill. "And I have just walked past four presses with about four hundred returns in them and I have not told you what is in the presses, and that is not an oversight and I am not going to let it be one, because I told you about nine inches under this sill and I have not told you about the four hundred yards behind you, and both of those are true, and the reason I told you the first one is that you came back and asked me nothing, and the reason I am not telling you the second one is that you came back and asked me nothing, and I have not got two different reasons and I have got one and I am using it twice."

---

Then she shut the window at about the fourth minute past the hour, and she did not mean to, and it was the ordinary time, and a man of about fifty came for a copy at about a minute past and had to wait, and she served him in about two minutes and read it out and he paid fourpence and went the way they all go.

**That is the whole of the trade at that window, and about four hundred of those are bought and filled in in a year, and not one of them is a question, and the reason is not that the people who come to a counter do not want to know things.** It is that a person who wants to know something at a counter does not come to a counter, they come to a counter to buy fourpence worth of paper, and they would be ashamed to stand there and use up a minute of a clerk's time for nothing.

She wrote in her own book with no office on it, in her own hand, with the day on it, and read it back twice, and the second reading was slower, and at the bottom she put the reason, which is the reason she has written eleven times in nine years and which has not got any better:

**A hand of mine is the only hand anybody is going to be able to put side by side in about four years, and I have now done this for the thirteenth time and I have not stopped and I am not going to.**

And after that, in the same book, on the same day, a second line, and it is the only second line she has ever written in it, and it is not a rule:

**Two doors, one man.**

---

She put the book away and looked at the corridor, which was empty, and at the door a couple of inches open halfway along it, and she did not go and close it either, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to shut a door, and the count is four, and it has not moved since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next, and a woman of about thirty-five in another city has said in a room that a name in a book does not go looking, and a woman of sixty-three in the same city has said her own name out loud in a street three times to nobody, and neither of those is an asking either, and none of the three of them is a form, and none of them is a post, and there is no heading over any of them and that is the reason they have all three of them got as far as they have got, which is not far, and which is the whole of the first week of the fourth month of the year after next in a room on a flood terrace about four hundred and thirty miles down this river from where any of it was said.
