# Chapter 244: Sixteen Books, And The Decision To Go And Tell Four Women That She Is Not Paid

The room is rented by the week and it is two streets off a street that goes down to the river road, and there are sixteen books on a table in it and a board, and fifteen of the sixteen are somebody else's and not one of the fifteen has an office on it.

Nell Kest is twenty. She is a dye-house worker, which is what she is paid for, and she keeps the books of eleven houses on a lane in this town and of four other houses in three other districts, and she has kept every one of them from this city since she was seventeen and has not been up the river since she came down it, and that is what she is not paid for.

Her wage stopped in the second week of the third month of the year after and it is stopped now, and the word for it is held, and the money for the nineteen days she has worked since the year turned is in a weigh-house ledger in this town with her wage in it and not her name under it.

---

The sixteenth book was asked for on the second day of the second week of the fourth month of the year after next by a woman of about thirty whose door the water came to in the first week of the second month and went off again, and nobody came.

She decided to keep it then and she has decided twice since not to give it back, and the reasons have got worse each time and the last one is that a woman whose numbers are in a book in this city is a woman who cannot be told to produce them.

**There is no fee and no day rate, and nobody is to be told.** Her own name is at the head of it, in her own hand, on purpose, in about four seconds, with a day against it and no name on it, and there is nothing in this empire that has ever been drawn up for a book-keeper to put her own name at the head of another person's numbers.

---

On the fourth day of the fourth week of the ninth month of the year after next she worked out what she was, which is not a thing she had not known.

She had known for about three years. What she had not worked out until that day was that the knowing had a shape, and the shape was that she had been doing about four days a week of work that nobody knew about, in a room two streets off a street that goes down to a river road, for nobody, on a wage that is not hers, and **that she had been carrying it the way a person carries a bag of somebody else's shopping up a stair and has never once been asked to.**

She said it out loud to that table, which she does about twice a week, and then she said the other half of it, which is the part that is actually hers.

"A person who is paid to keep a number is a person who can be told what the number is," she said. "That is the whole of it and I have known it since I was seventeen and I have never said it out loud to a living soul, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for a book-keeper, and the four of us who do it get it done in kitchens and on steps."

---

Then she said the other things, because she had the afternoon and there was nobody in the room, and because a person who has just named a thing to an empty room has to go on and name the rest or it sounds like a speech.

"A book-keeper about four hundred and thirty miles down this river worked out before the year turned that the days he spends on other people's numbers are not paid and cannot be, and nobody had ever told him, and he has been on that bank two years and he is not on the list of works and has never been on it."

"How do you know that?"

"Because a woman of about thirty told me in a lane in this city in the second week of the third month of the year after next, and she had it from somebody who had been up that road, and she was not lying. I have not met him and I am not going to." Nell Kest turned the first page of the eleventh house round and squared it with the edge of the table, which is what she does at about the seventh hour every day of the week and has done for about three years. "**And a foreman off a dye end in this city has said one true thing about himself out loud five times**, and the fifth of them was to a boy of about nine with a slate, and it is on a slate, and neither of them has a slate, and he is not thanked and he is not going to be, and I have not thanked him either and I have had three chances."

---

The foreman came up the two stairs at about the sixth hour, for the fourth time, and he is about forty and his name is on nothing and he did not shut the door and did not sit down, and he has a coat on.

"Before you say it," he said. "The wage is held. I have been told to tell you and I have been told it since the second week of the third month of the year after and I have said it to you three times and I have never once been told what it is held against and there is no form and there is not going to be one, and I have stopped waiting to be told."

"You have not sent the line."

"I have not sent the line and I have had it in the inside of this coat since the fourth day of the third week of the seventh month of the year after next, with a day on it and no name on it. I have decided in about four seconds on a stair that a document would let me say a thing out loud without a word and I am not going to be able to do that, and you of all people will know why." He put his hand flat on the table, not on a book. "And I am not going to tell you what is on it, and you are not going to ask me, and that is a thing we are both going to be right about for about four years."

---

"You are not going to tell me what is on it."

"No."

"And I am not going to ask you, and I have thought about asking you about eleven times in about four months and I have not, and the reason I have not is that you would have said no and you would have been right to, and then I would have had to find a reason to stop wanting to know." She turned round. "And I have worked out, in a rented room in a city about four hundred and thirty miles down this river, in about nine minutes, that a person who knows what a document says is a person who can be served with it, and I have sixteen books and one of them has my own name at the head of it and I would like to keep the three things I have that nobody can use."

"Which three."

"My name on one book, the door you have not come up again, and about four years in which nobody has heard me say out loud that I am not paid." She put her pen down. "And I have just said the third one out loud, and I have not said it out loud in about four years, and you are a foreman and you are not going to be thanked for it, and I am not going to be thanked for it either, and there is nobody in this empire who can be."

---

"Then I am going to say a thing and it is the thing I have been not saying since the second week of the third month of the year after, and you did not come up that stair to hear it and I would like it said that you did not."

"I came up that stair because I have nothing else to do at the sixth hour."

"**I have never been paid for any of the sixteen.** Not one of them, not one day, not one entry, and the fourth week of the ninth month of the year after next is the week I have decided to stop carrying that quietly and to go and tell them, and I have four people to tell and I have told one of them and she has offered to pay me and I have said no."

---

The foreman took his cap off, which he had not done in three visits.

"You have said no to a woman who has money."

"I have said no to a woman who has money and whose door the water came to and who stood in a street and cried about a sum, and I said no because if I take her money then I am a trade and a trade can be told what to do, and she cannot be told what to do, and that is the only thing I have got." She put both hands flat on the sixteen books. "**Everything I have for money is a thing somebody else can stop and everything I have for nothing is a thing nobody can produce**, and one of them has my name at the head of it, and that is the whole of what I have got and it is not a good position to be in."

"You have said that to me before."

"I have said it in this room to four people and to about forty books, and this is the first time I have said it to somebody who can stop me, and I would like it noticed that I said it anyway."

---

"Why are you telling them at all? That is the half of it I do not have."

"Because I have worked out what happens if I do not." She turned round and faced the table and not him. "**In about four years one of those women is going to have a question about a sum** and I am going to be the only person who can answer it, and I am going to have to be able to say where I got it. And I have worked out that a person who has never once been told that anything in her house is held by her cannot be told that either, and the first thing she is going to ask is who holds it and on what, and I am not going to be able to answer that, and I would rather be standing in the room when it happens than not."

"That is the best reason anybody has given me in about four years and I have heard four," said the foreman. "And I am not going to be the one who says it is good, because I am a man who is holding a woman's whole wage and cannot say what it is held against, and if your reason is good then mine is worse, and I have known that since the fourth day of the third week of the seventh month of the year after next."

---

She told the first one on the second day of that same week, and it took about nine minutes, and it went the way she had worked out it would go and not any other way.

The woman of about thirty listened to the whole of it and then said that she would pay her a shilling a week for the books, which is more than anybody has ever offered her, and Nell Kest said no, and the woman said that a person who will not take money for something is not doing it for the money, and then said the other thing, which was the one that cost.

"Then you will not have it when I am gone."

---

Nell Kest did not answer that for about a minute.

"About four years," she said. "And you are right, and I have known it since the second week of the third month of the year after, and I have kept the book anyway, and I want it written down somewhere that a person who keeps another person's numbers for nothing is keeping them for the four years and not for the person, and that the two of those are not the same thing and that I chose the four years on purpose out of sixteen books and not out of one."

"You are not paid and you have no post and there is no form for either."

"**And if somebody came up those stairs tomorrow with a sheet** and a seal and asked me to account for the sixteen, I could not show a soul the fourth of them, and I would have to say so out loud in a room, and the woman I could not account for is the one whose street the water office spells two ways."

---

The foreman was not there for that. Nobody was there for that.

She sat with the sixteenth book open in front of her for about a quarter of an hour afterwards and did not write anything, and what she was not doing was writing the reason, and there was a reason available and she has never had it written down anywhere why she did not take it.

"There is a place a person can put a fact and nowhere at all a person can put a thing they would rather somebody else knew," she said. "The sixteenth book has a space under the day and a space under the space, and I have looked at both of them about four hundred times since the year turned, and both of them are for a person, and I am not going to be one in my own hand about a thing I am not paid to be one about."

There is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be in a room when somebody else works out what they are, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be thanked for a table of books and a refusal. Nobody in this matter has ever been thanked and there is not going to be a form for it now.

**A book-keeper about four hundred and thirty miles down this river keeps the numbers of eleven houses on a lane and of four houses in three other districts, and is not on the list of works and has never been on it.** A foreman off a dye end in this city is holding a piece of paper in a coat and is not going to tell anybody, and a woman of twenty in this room is in a box and he is in one as well, and neither of them has been asked about it by anybody at all. A bill at Lowcross is nineteen pounds three and fourpence unpaid with no line against it in nineteen years, and nobody in this room is going to be the reason it is unpaid.

**And a woman of twenty has sixteen books, one of which has her own name at the head of it**, and she has decided to go and tell four women that she is not paid, and the first one has been told, and nobody is going to thank her for any of it.
