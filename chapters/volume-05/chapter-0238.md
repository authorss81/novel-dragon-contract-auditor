# Chapter 238: Nineteen Reasons Read Out Loud In A Bay, And A Man Who Knows His Own

The list of works for the season that begins after this one was read out in the bay on the fourth day of the fourth week of the seventh month of the year after next and it took about four minutes, and nobody agreed it and nobody refused anything, and the four were on it the way the four are on every list.

Halla Wray is fifty-one. She is the foreman of the Slade Cut, which is an office with no office in it, and she has been on that bank since she was nine, and she has been in a box since the first week of the seventh month of the year after, and she has not been asked about it once and is not going to be asked again.

The water was down a foot and a bit from where it had been in the sixth month. The hold against a company whose whole money is four shillings a year and all of it the rent of a building had not moved since the fourth week of the fourth month of the year after.

---

About nineteen people said no again in the last week of the seventh month of the year after and they said it one after another on a day in about four minutes, and the reasons they gave were the same nineteen reasons, and every one of the reasons went in the third column of that bay's own ledger with the day against it and no names in that column at all.

Then, **on the fourth day of the fourth week of the seventh month of the year after next, she read them out.**

All nineteen. In the bay. In the ordinary way, in a foreman's voice, the second reading slower, and nothing skipped, and the names not read, because a name in a room is a thing that can be carried out of a room.

---

A man of about thirty from the second gang said, "That is mine."

Halla Wray stopped.

"That one is yours," she said. "I read it as I found it and I did not know it was yours and I am not going to say that I did not, and I am going to say that there is no name on any of the nineteen and I did not put one there and I am not going to put one there now."

"Then it is findable now."

"It is findable now," said Halla Wray. "**It has been in a book in a building four hundred and thirty miles down this river since the seventh month of the year after, and in about four years a person with a copy of the right page could put that day and that reason side by side and ask about a season a person had not been in.** For about three years nobody could have done that, because the column has no name in it and a reason does not carry a name. A man who gave one did not know that nine people in a bay were going to hear it read out."

---

"You read it out."

"I read it out," said Halla Wray, "and I am not going to pretend I read it out by accident, and I am going to say why in about four sentences and then I am going to do the ordinary things, because it is the last week of a season and there are about nine men in that bay."

"Go on."

"A reason that has been read out in a bay to nine men is findable in a way a name is not," she said. "A name is one document. It is in one hand in one building and a person with a copy of the right page has to have that page. A reason is in nine mouths and every one of those mouths is a person who can be asked, and there is not one of them who could be stopped, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a man may not say out loud in a bay that a reason is his."

---

The man of about thirty sat down on the bench and did not say anything for about a minute.

"I have been on this bank nine years and nobody has ever known anything about me that I did not tell them," he said, "and I have just told nine men something I told one, and it was in a bay and it was read out and I could not stop it and I am not going to try to stop it now."

"That is the right way to have it."

"Is it."

"It is the way it is going to be for about nineteen people and I have not got a better one and I am not going to look for one this week," said Halla Wray. "And I want the other half of it said out loud in the same breath. A man who has been findable by his own reason for about four minutes is also a man who has been on a list of works and answered by a foreman out loud for nine years. About four of the men in this bay cannot read a paragraph and have never been asked anything, and being left alone is a thing that has been good to them. I have given one reason about that and three about myself and every one of them was bad, and I am not going to hand you a fifth in a bay."

---

And then a man of about forty from the third gang stood up where he had been sitting, and he had a reason too, and about four people in that bay knew it before he had said a word of it, and Halla Wray put her hand out flat before he got there.

"No," she said.

"I have not said it."

"You have your face on and it is the same face the man of thirty has had since the reading," said Halla Wray. "And I am not going to stop a man from saying a thing he has a right to say, and there is no form in this empire that gives me that, and I am telling you what I am about to do and not stopping you."

"Then do it."

---

"**Nineteen reasons in a column and nine men in a bay is already more than anybody on this bank has ever had put about themselves.** If a second one of you stands up then it stops being a thing a foreman did and it starts being a habit, and I have watched about nine people in this empire make a habit into a thing and every single one of them ended up with a heading over it. I am not going to be the one that puts it over about forty-one people."

"So I should not say it."

"You should say it," she said, "and I am telling you that you are the second one and the second one is worse than the first one, and that is true and I am not going to soften it, and I am not going to ask you not to and I am not going to say anything else about it. Say it to him after I have gone out of the bay and not before."

He said it to him after she had gone out of the bay, and nine men were in that bay and four of them heard it, and it is not in a book and it is not in a column and it is not a day, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a man may not say a true thing about himself to another man in a shed, and nobody has written it down and nobody is going to.

---

The man of about thirty caught her at the end of the bay on his way out and asked whether she was going to read them again.

"I do not know."

"You read them twice in your own head and everybody heard the second one."

"I have watched him read that ledger going back and he has got a reason under every entry in it that I have ever seen, and I am not going to be the one who makes him put one under nineteen more this side of the water going out," said Halla Wray. "And I am not going to tell you the answer to that, and the reason I am not going to tell you the answer is that a person who reads a column out loud twice in a season is a person who has made it a practice, and a practice is the one thing in this empire that grows a heading over it on its own without anybody deciding anything."

"I have thought of that."

"Then you have thought of it faster than I did and I am not going to say so in a bay."

---

Then she did the ordinary things, and there were about nine of them before the lamp.

A man of about thirty-four asked whether the hold would be on the list before the season shut, and she told him it would not and why in about four seconds, and he said that was not what he meant, and she said it was the only meaning it has.

A man of about twenty-eight asked her whether a person could be on two lists at once and she said she did not know and would not find out for him.

A man of about fifty came in and asked about the third link of a chain, and she told him about the third link and the week before it wants oil, in about two minutes, out loud, without turning round, from behind the bench, and nine men watched a foreman do a perfectly ordinary thing to a man who cannot read a paragraph and not one of them noticed anything at all.

And a man of about fifty from the fourth gang put his head in at about the seventh hour and said he wanted to get over to Lowcross in the morning and could he use the bridge.

She said the bridge is shut and has been shut since before the water came in, and he said he had a reason, and she said that is not a reason the bridge knows about.

"**I know**," said the man. "And that is the reason I am still here, and I have said it to you every time I have come to that door this year and I have counted them and nobody has ever asked me to count them out loud."

"Nobody is going to ask you to count them out loud. If they do there is not one form for it and you do not have to help them, and I have said that in this shed three times now and the third time is shorter and if you keep coming to that door I am going to run out of ways of saying it."

---

Then she went up the road, about four hundred yards, and there is a hired stone store at the end of it with a bench along the north wall, and a book-keeper of thirty-eight was on the bench with the ledger shut on his knee and the lamp not lit. **She did not knock, and she had never once knocked on that door in nine years, and it is a thing she thought about for most of a week.**

She did not go in. She stood in the doorway, which is a thing a foreman does about once in about nine years and had never done before.

"I read the nineteen out loud in that bay," she said. "All of them. The second reading slower. And a man of about thirty from the second gang said one of them was his, and he was right, and I did not know it was his and I am not going to pretend I did not."

Rennick Adley put his hand flat on the cover of the ledger and did not open it.

"Then there is a second kind of findable," he said, "and it is worse than a name, and it has been in that column since the seventh month of the year after and about nineteen people put it there and not one of them knew that a reason could be recognised."

"That is what I wanted to say to a person and not to a bay."

"Then it is said." He did not ask her where she had got the idea from. He wanted to, and he did not, and both of them knew it and neither of them said so, and that is the arrangement from the seventh month of the year after and it is not broken. "I am not going to ask you why, and I am not going to write it down, and the reason is a book in a building four hundred and thirty miles down this river that has got no heading over it and no list of persons in it and about nine hundred and forty names in nine hands over about sixty years, and about four hundred of them are dead, and the newest hand in that book is mine."

"And the half."

"I have not asked," said Rennick Adley. "And I am not going to ask, and I would like it noticed in a doorway that I have wanted it since the third month of the year after and I have not asked, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to ask a foreman for the rest of a sentence."

**And he did not ask, and that is the arrangement, and it is a habit, and I have watched about nine people in this empire make a habit out of a habit in my time in these bays and every single one of them ended up with a heading over it.**

Then she went back down the road about four hundred yards, and the lamp was not lit in either building and it was later than it ought to be. **There is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a foreman to say out loud in a doorway that she has read nineteen reasons to nine men, and she said it anyway, and that is the second true thing a foreman of fifty-one has said about herself in a room, and the first was said to a bay and this one was said to one person, and neither of them is going to be asked whether it was a good idea.**


She had been gone about two minutes when the man of about thirty came back into that bay and asked the man of about thirty-four where the foreman was, and the man of about thirty-four said she had gone up the road, and the man of about thirty said he knew, and stood there.

"Then somebody has heard it," he said.

"Then somebody has heard it," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have not, and I am not going to ask, and you are not going to tell me, and in about four years neither of us is going to be able to say whether it happened in that bay or in a shed or in a yard, and that is the correct thing and I want it noticed that I think so without being asked."

The man of about thirty did not argue with that, and he did not thank him for it, and then he said the thing he had come back to say, and he said it to about nine men in a bay and not to the man beside him. "I gave a reason out loud earlier in the week and the foreman read nineteen of them out loud and mine was one of them and about four other men heard it, and I have not slept properly since and I am not going to."

Nobody said anything, and about nine men in a bay heard a man of about thirty say that, and not one of them wrote it down. **And a foreman of fifty-one was four hundred yards up a road at about the sixth hour telling a book-keeper what she had done in a bay, and a foreman of fifty-one does not know that she has been told about twice, because a man of about thirty said it to a bay and a bay is not a room and a room is not a book and there is no form anywhere in this empire that carries a thing from one to the other.**
