# Chapter 229: A Question In A Shed And Four Lines On A Bench

The shed is about nine foot by eleven, up the road and not in the store, and there is a bench along the north wall that four men can sit on, and a lamp in it that is not lit until about the seventh hour, and it was about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the second day of the first week of the fifth month of the year after next.

Rennick Adley is thirty-eight and he is not a clerk. He has been keeping the books of the Slade Cut Company in a hired stone store off the Slade since the second month of next year, and the Company's whole money is four shillings a year and all of it is the rent of the building, and there are about nineteen reasons in the third column of the ledger with a day against every one of them and no names in that column at all. The names are in a different book, in a different building, in nine hands, over about sixty years.

He did the ordinary things first, because the ordinary things are the ordinary things and they are on a day.

---

The count of the works took about four minutes with the men in it, and the water was a foot and a bit off the mark where it had been in the third month, and the hold against a company of nine families and about forty-one people has not moved since the fourth week of the fourth month of the year after.

A factor's letter of four lines was on the bench where it has been since the second week of the third month of the year after, wanting the name of a person who may be served and that person's consent, and it says without both that it cannot proceed and will not proceed. **It is not going to be answered, and every day it is not answered is a day nobody has been entered.** A carter asked in the second week of the tenth month of the year after to have his own line written in the place where the no is, and went up the road for the winter, and is not coming back for the asking.

A bill at Lowcross is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and is unpaid and nobody is liable, and there has not been a line for a bridge in that fund in nineteen years, and nobody in that shed is going to be asked for a penny and nobody is going to be asked for a better reason than the one he has got.

And about four people in that bay cannot read a paragraph. They are on the list of works. Their work is on the list. **They have never been asked anything and they are not going to be asked this season, and the finding he entered in his own hand in the second week of the tenth month of the year after stands, and he has not re-entered it, and he has not improved it, and it is not his to improve.**

---
---

Then a man of about fifty-five came to the end of the bench with a printed slip in his hand, and it was his own, and he wanted to know what the day under it meant, because there is a day against every line of a list of works and he has never once asked anybody what a day is for, and nobody has ever told him.

Rennick Adley told him in about four seconds, in a book-keeper's voice, and did not read the slip out of his hand and did not write anything down, and then he stopped, because he had done it, and he had done it in a bay with about nine men in it and he had not meant to.

"I meant whether it is the day it started or the day somebody decided," said the man of about fifty-five.

"Then somebody decided."

"That is what I thought."

"That is what it is, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a man to work that out, and there never has been one, and I have not written it down and I am not going to."

"You read it to me just now."

"I answered you," said Rennick Adley. "**A man reading a man his own paper is not a question and it is not one of the four and it is not counted, and the count of the four is a count of people who asked a person out loud what a word on a form means, and there is a clerk of fifty-five in this city who says that in a room and I am not going to improve on it.** And you have asked me nothing about the hold and I am not going to ask you, and that is a not-doing and I put not-doings in that book and there is a day against this one."

---

Then she came out of the store with the list in her hand and she put it on the bench and read it, in about four minutes, in a foreman's voice, the way she reads everything, and about nine men heard her and did not hear anything in particular, because that is what she is for.

Halla Wray is fifty-one. She is the foreman of the Slade Cut, which is an office with no office in it, and she has been on that bank since she was nine. She can read and nobody at the district works has ever once in nine years asked her to read anything, and she has been answering questions for four men in that bay since she was forty-two, without being asked, and a man with a ledger has seen her do it twice now and has not said so to her and is not going to.

Then she said the thing, and she said it to him, and it was not about the works.

---

"**If the office was meant to be temporary, who changed it into an inheritable one.**"

Rennick Adley did not answer it, and he did not ask her where she had got it, and neither of those was careful. Both of them were the arrangement, and the arrangement was made in the seventh month of the year after in a shed with about nine men in it and it is that the day he asks her is the day she stops saying the half of a sentence she has got, and the half came out of her mouth in the last week of the eleventh month of the year after to a man from a district works who asked her a question about the works, and it cannot be got back, and he heard it and did not ask and did not write it down.

"That is a plain question," said Rennick Adley, after about half a minute, "and it is not about a word on a form, and I am not going to count it and neither are you."

"I did not count it."

"I am not going to ask you where you got it," he said, "and I want that noticed, and the reason is that a man who asks a foreman a question that is not about the works is a man who has begun asking a foreman questions, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for that and there is no post and no wage for it and I have a wage."

---

"Write it down," said Halla Wray.

"No."

"That is the first thing you have ever refused me that was not about money."

"It is not about money. It is about a book in a building," said Rennick Adley, "and it is the only thing in this shed that is not about money."

He put his hand flat on the ledger and did not open it.

"**That letter on that bench wants a name that may be served. Four lines, and a factor's hand, and it says it will not proceed without a name and that name's consent.** A man who wants that name writes to the company, and the company has no office and no fund and a book-keeper, and he does not have to come up the road at all. He asks for the book."

"And that is the whole of the reason," said Rennick Adley, "and it is a reason about a bench and not about a question, and I have thought about it for about four minutes and I have got a better one and I am going to keep the better one for myself and write this one down instead."

---

"And the name of the thing is in about nine hundred documents in this empire," said Halla Wray. "I have read it nine times on a sheet of good paper on this bench and there is a seal at the foot of it and nothing under the seal, and the second line asks that no inference be drawn from the absence of any name, and there is a copy of the six lines in a book of my own with the day against it and no name on it, and neither of those two sheets is in my hand except one of them and that one is a copy and I wrote it out myself, and neither of them has my name on it and I did not put it there."

"You have read the notice nine times."

"I have read the notice nine times and the second line nine times and the sixth line about four times, and the sixth line will not name whoever holds the office, and I have never said that out loud in a shed before and I have said it to you now and I am not going to say it again."

---

"Then say it once more and I will not write it down."

"No," said Halla Wray, "because you have just told me the reason you are not going to write it down, and it is a good reason, and it is the reason I have not put my own name on anything since the first week of the seventh month of the year after, and it is also the reason nobody has come up this road asking me anything, and both of those are the same reason and it is the fourth time I have said it and it is a bad one."

"It is a good reason."

"**It is a good reason that keeps a foreman in a box that nobody is looking in, and a bad reason that keeps four men who cannot read a paragraph from ever being told what the hold is, and I have got two bad reasons and I am not going to hand you a third one in a shed at about the fifth hour and have you read it back to me in a book.**"

---

Then she went in and he did not follow her, and he sat on the bench with the ledger shut and the four lines on it and about nine men going up the road, and he did the second thing he had come to do, which is an entry, and it is an entry in his own hand with the day against it in a book with no heading at the top of it and a name at the foot of every line of a list of works.

He wrote: *A girl of seventeen is on this list of works at the rate the list is set at, on a written engagement, on her fourth day. She has not been thanked. Nobody has thanked her.*

And then he stopped, and he looked at the entry for about four minutes, and he did not put a reason under it, and it is the first entry he has made in that book since the second month of next year without a reason under it, and every one of the other four hundred and some has got one, and he read them going back, and they are good reasons, and they are his.

---

He did not put a reason under it because he could not find one, and he has gone back to it since and he still cannot, and he is not going to spend the season on it.

"A person who is paid to read is a person who can be told to stop," he said out loud to the bench. "That is the reason for the four. That is the reason a foreman has not told her she is the one person in this bay who could read the hold. That is the reason I did not tell the foreman I read a man's paper to him in a yard in the first week of the twelfth month of the year after. **It is the same reason every time and I have got it exactly where I want it, and I could have put it under this entry in about four seconds and it would have been true, and I did not, and I do not know why.**"

He shut the book.

---

He did not write the question down either. That was the second thing, and it was harder than the first, and it is the first time in about two years he has wanted to put a thing in that book and not done it, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for a thing a man wanted to write and did not.

"A foreman of fifty-one said a question out loud in a shed on the second day of the first week of the fifth month of the year after next," he said, to the bench, "and about nine men heard it and not one of them will be able to repeat it, and neither can I in about four years, and the man who can repeat it is the one person in this empire that the question is about, and he is not in this room, and he cannot be in this room, and there is no form in this empire that says a man has to be in a room."

---

A man of about fifty from the fourth gang came to the door at about the sixth hour and said he wanted to get over to Lowcross in the morning and could he use the bridge, and Halla Wray came out and said the bridge is shut and has been shut since before the water came in, and he said he had a reason, and she said that is not a reason the bridge knows about.

"I know," said the man.

"Then it is not a reason and I am not going to ask you for another one, and there is no form in this empire that requires a man to give you a better reason than *I want to get over there*, and I have read every form in that store and there is not one page in any of them that says a person has to hand you a reason for a thing they want."

"That is the reason I am still here," said the man. "You have said that in this shed as many times this year as there are weeks in the season and I have counted them and I have never been asked to count them out loud and I am not going to."

"Nobody is going to ask you to count them out loud. If they do, there is no form for it and you do not have to help them."

---

The lamp was not lit and it was later than it ought to be, and there were four lines on a bench that are not going to be answered, and a bill at Lowcross at nineteen pounds three and fourpence with no line against it in nineteen years, and a guarantee standing offered and unanswered on about four hundred and forty foot of bank with about sixty children under sixteen inside it, and a reader of seventeen on a list of works at the rate the list is set at who was not thanked and was not spoken to about the season and is not going to start, and about four men who cannot read a paragraph who have still never been asked what the hold is, and a book-keeper of thirty-eight with a book shut on his knee and an entry in it with no reason under it, and a foreman's question in a shed at about the fifth hour that nobody answered and that he is not going to write down, and the count is four, and it has not moved, and the lamp is not lit yet.
