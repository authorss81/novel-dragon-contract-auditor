# Chapter 235: Sixteen Books, And A Line In A Coat

The room is rented by the week and it is two streets off a street that goes down to the river road, and there are sixteen books in it on a table and a board, and fifteen of the sixteen are somebody else's and not one of the fifteen has an office on it.

Nell Kest is twenty. She is a dye-house worker, which is what she is paid for, and she keeps the books of eleven houses on a lane in a furnace town about four hundred and thirty miles up this river and of four other houses in three other districts, and she has kept every one of them from this city since she was seventeen and has not been up that river since she came down it, and that is what she is not paid for.

Her wage stopped in the second week of the third month of the year after and it is stopped now, and the word for it is *held*, and the money for the nineteen days she has worked this year is in a weigh-house ledger in a furnace town with her wage in it and not her name under it.

---

The sixteenth book was asked for on the second day of the second week of the fourth month of the year after next by a woman of about thirty whose door the water came to in the first week of the second month and went off again, and nobody came. Four of them and a door and nobody came.

She has decided to keep it. It is unpaid, **there is no fee and no day rate, and nobody is to be told.** Her own name is at the head of it, in her own hand, on purpose, in about four seconds, with a day against it and no name on it, and there is nothing in this empire that has ever been drawn up for a book-keeper to put her own name at the head of another person's numbers.

The woman of about thirty came back on the second day of the first week of the seventh month of the year after next with two numbers wrong and she cried about the second one, and Nell Kest let her cry for about two minutes and then gave her the cloth and told her to sit down.

---

"You have written the street the way the water office spells it and I have told you twice that is the right one."

"The woman at the second door spells it the other way and she is going to be asked about the water."

"Then the woman at the second door is going to be asked about the water in a street with two names in it," said Nell Kest, "and if I put your spelling in that book then in about four years a man with a copy of the right page can put a door next to a name that is not at that door, and I have told you why and I am not going to say it again this year."

"You told me four times and I counted them and I am not one of your about nine."

"You are not," said Nell Kest, "and I am glad of it, and I have never once been thanked for any of this and I am not going to be, and there is nobody in this empire you could thank for keeping a book for nothing because nobody has ever drawn up a form for a person to be thanked for keeping a book."

---

**Then a man of about twenty-eight came in at about the sixth hour with a piece of paper in his hand**, and he is the fourth person to have been in that room in about a year, and there were only three of them, and two of them have paid her.

"That is his mother's and not mine, and there is a sum at the bottom of it that is wrong, and I have known it was wrong for about six weeks and I have been carrying it up two streets twice a week saying nothing."

"Say it to me and not to him."

"Two shillings and sixpence out and four shillings and threepence in, and the difference is not a mistake in the sum. Somebody paid him four shillings and wrote down two and six, and it is in a fair hand, and it is not your hand, and I have been about four hours deciding whether to come up two streets to say that to a person who is not paid for it."

"Put the sum in the book and put the day against it and put nothing else under it."

"That is what I have been told to do by about four people and I do not know one of them."

"Then take it from me, and I am not one of the four," said Nell Kest, "and I am going to say the reason out loud and then I am going to do it. **A book with a mistake in it and no day against it is a book that is lying. A book with a mistake in it and a day against it is a book that was wrong on one day and has been right about being wrong ever since, and there is nothing anywhere in this empire that says the second one is not to be used."**

---

"There is a name at the head of it and the name is not his and the name is not his mother's either."

"I know whose name it is and I am not going to tell you. I am not going to tell you because about nine hundred and forty names in a book in a building four hundred and thirty miles from this room came off buildings like that one, and I said out loud in a rented room in the second week of the fourth month of the year after next that a door with a name on it is a thing that can be carried out of a room. I am not going to be the second one."

"Then it stays where it is."

"It stays in it with the day against it and the wrong sum under the day, and the wrong sum is more use to him than a right one with nothing against it," said Nell Kest, "and he will be able to show it to whoever asks in about four years and it will be a thing that happened on a day and not a thing somebody thought up in a room about four hundred and thirty miles away."

He went down the stairs and she wrote the day and the two figures and nothing else under them and read it back once, and she did not put her own name at the foot of it, and that is a thing she decided in about two seconds and would not have said out loud to anybody.

---

Then the foreman off the dye end came up two stairs for the third time, and he is about forty, and his name is on nothing, and he did not shut the door and did not sit down, and he has a coat on.

"Before you say it," he said, "the wage is held. I have been told to tell you and I have been told it since the second week of the third month of the year after and I have said it to you twice and I have never once been told what it is held against and there is no form and there is not going to be one, and I have stopped waiting to be told."

"You have not sent the line."

"I have not sent the line."

"You are not going to send it."

"No," said the foreman, "and I have worked out why in about a minute and a half on a stair and I would like to be told if I have got it right."

---

"Tell me."

"A line with the day on it is a document," he said. "**A document is the only thing anybody can be held to and I have never in forty years been held to one thing, and if I put a day against your wage being held then in about four years a person can put the day next to a wage and ask who held it and on what, and there is nobody to answer, and it will be you and me."**

Nell Kest put her pen down.

"That is right and it took you a minute and a half and it has taken me two years and I have never once said it out loud to anybody," she said, "and I have been doing other people's numbers since I was seventeen and I have known it the whole time and nobody has ever told me and I did not know I was waiting to be told."

---

"Then what do I do with it?"

"Write it. Keep it. Do not send it." She pushed the sixteenth book aside and made a space. "Put the day on it and the word held and nothing else and no name, and keep it in the inside of that coat, and when a day comes that you want it to exist, send it, and on that day it stops being a thing you have thought and starts being a thing that has happened, and you will not be able to take it back, and that is the only reason anybody has ever had for writing one down."

"That is not a reason anybody has ever had for writing one down."

"No," said Nell Kest. "It is a reason I have just made up in a room in about four seconds and I am not going to be thanked for it and I would like it said that I know it is not a good one. **Everything I have for money is a thing somebody else can stop and everything I have for nothing is a thing nobody can produce, and I have sixteen books and one of them has my name at the head of it, and that is the whole of what I have got and it is not a good position to be in."**

---

The foreman went down the two stairs and she stood in the room with a book open and did not write anything about the money, which is what she has done every time since the second week of the third month of the year after.

---

Her brother's letter came on the sixteenth of the eighth of last year and it is on the shelf behind the sixteen books and it has not been answered and it is hers to leave unanswered, and she has looked at it about nine times since the day it came and has never once opened it on a day when she was tired, and that is not a discipline, she said, it is the only arrangement she has ever had with anybody.

"There is a thing people do," she said, to the room, "where they keep a letter for about four years and then say that they could not answer it, and that is a lie and everybody who does it knows it is a lie while they are doing it. I have decided that if I ever answer that letter I will answer it on a day when I have nothing else in my hands. I have had nothing else in my hands on about nine days out of about four hundred, and I am not going to hurry it and I am not going to let anybody hurry it either. There is a foreman of forty who has come up two stairs three times and a clerk of thirty-eight four hundred and thirty miles down a river and a man with no office who answered me a question for nothing, and not one of them has ever once asked me about the letter."

---

Then she counted, because she is a person who counts and nothing in this empire has a form for counting anything, and there are four people she has to ask and she has asked two and she has not asked the other two and she has not said she is not going to at all.

"**Four in about a hundred and forty years.** A court room in the fifth month of the year after that wanted a record. A clerk's window in the first week of the tenth, by a man who was afraid about his own household. A desk in the second week of the eleventh, by a woman of twenty who walked about four miles and nobody sent her. A rented room in the first week of the first month of the year after next, by the same woman, who walked about nine miles in a day and asked a man with no office what a word on a form means, and was answered with a price and a refusal."

Two of the four were accidents. The third was the first one anybody did on purpose. The fourth was the same person on purpose again, and she is answerable for it and can be found, and the man she asked cannot be found and is answerable too, and that is the whole asymmetry and it took the two of them a day each to get to.

And the two she has not asked are a woman of about sixty with a board and a basket, four hundred and thirty miles up this river, who is the only person alive who can say how a box got filled and who does not know that anybody knows, and a clerk of thirty-eight at a window four hundred and thirty miles down it who has not got a fund and no form anywhere in this empire has ever been drawn up for one.

---

She has not been up that river since she came down it and she is not going this year, and there is a difference between not going and saying she is not going, and she is the only person in this room who knows there is a difference.

"And I am going to say one more thing about the man I asked," she said, to the sixteen books, "and then I am going to do the eleventh house."

"He has nothing. No office, no fund, no register, a bar of his own that came off him on the twelfth of the ninth month of last year on an application of one line which he wrote himself, and a mark on the inside of his wrist about two inches long that reads nothing and does not know where the ford is. He is the most dangerous person in this matter and everybody who has met him has worked that out in about four minutes."

"**And I have worked out the other half and I do not think anybody else has. He cannot be found and that is the only thing he has got, and a person who cannot be found can be given a thing to do and cannot be charged for it, and I have had a wage held since the second week of the third month of the year after by a man who is the only person in this city who can pick it up or put it down and who cannot tell me why."**

---

"So he is the one you would ask."

"He is the one I have asked," said Nell Kest. "I have four people and I have asked two and I am not asking the other two in the eighth month of the year after next, and I am not going to stand here and work out a fifth from a chair."

She turned the first page of the eleventh house round and squared it with the edge of the table, which is what she does at about the seventh hour every day of the week and has done for about three years. There are sixteen books on that table and not one of them has an office on it, and nobody has been told about any of the sixteen, and there is a factor's letter of four lines on a bench about four hundred and thirty miles down this river that is not going to be answered. Every day it is not answered is a day nobody has been entered, and there are about nineteen people behind a bank who said no again and are not going to be asked, and about four people in a bay who cannot read a paragraph and have never been asked anything. A bill at Lowcross at nineteen pounds three and fourpence with no line against it in nineteen years, and a girl of seventeen at the back of that bay who is not thanked and is not going to start.

And that is the first week of the seventh month of the year after next, and the count is four, and she is not going up that river this year and has not said she is never going to.
