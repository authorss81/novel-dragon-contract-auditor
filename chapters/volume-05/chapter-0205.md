# Chapter 205: What The Roll Had

The return came up the corridor on the first day of the first week of the tenth month of the year after, in a bundle with about nine other things in it, and it was not going into a file. It was going into a register, and a return going into a register is a return that has to be copied, and a return that has to be copied is a return somebody is going to read the column on before they copy it.

Ivet Sarn is thirty-eight. She has been at the clerk's window of that court for nine years. The window is on the ground floor with the embankment corridor behind it, and there are four presses in the corridor, and there is a back room off that corridor that she has walked past every working day for nine years and has never once been inside, and about nine people a month ask her for a book in the wrong form and she admits it and tells them what it will cost them.

She is not hiding anything. Her caution is partly justified: the last time a docket was reopened it cost that district a winter of aid, and there is a paper about that winter in a drawer in her own room and she has never told the court why.

---

The column on the return was headed for the person, institution or thing engaged in the matter, and it was filled in, in ink, in a hand that was not hers, and it was dated the fourth day of the third week of the fifth month of the year after.

"There is nothing to copy," she said, to the bundle. "It is done."

That is not a reason to put a sheet in a press. It is a reason to look at it, and she looked at it for about four minutes, and what she found was this.

**The date on the return is the day the column was filled in and not the day the return was sent, and there is no space on the sheet for the second day.** The office of a seat had asked this court, in the ordinary way, for the person of a family who is of age and resident, and the court keeps a roll of households of that district for the water, and the roll has got no answer to that question in it, and about nine hundred buildings print a form with a box on it for the answer, and the office had filled the box in itself.

"So the box is not asking us anything," said Ivet Sarn. "The box is the office's own column coming back to us with a day on it, and we are to copy a thing we did not find."

---

The man at the window came at about the ninth hour on the third day of the week and he had a slip with a reference on the top of it, and he wanted to know about the fourth water return, and she was four minutes into it when he asked the question.

He is about thirty-four. He had moved in the second week of the ninth month from a street with a shop on it to a set of rooms over a yard, four streets off, inside the same district, and he had told nobody at the counter because there is nothing at the counter that asks him.

"What does resident mean," he said. "On that. Not on yours. On the return. It is on the return and it is a word and I have looked at it for a fortnight and it does not say whose word it is, and I am not asking you as a favour, I am asking you because you are the only person in this building who reads the forms, and I have a wife and a boy and I would like to know whether a word is going to be used about us."

---

She said the price out loud before she answered him, and she did not know she was going to until about a second after she had said it, and that is how it happened.

"I am going to answer that in a room, out loud, in about four minutes, and there is no charge on the board for it, and I am not going to write it down for you, and here is the price of answering it: a person who asks a question about a form in a room becomes a person somebody has answered, and there is no instrument anywhere in this empire that takes that back off him. I am telling you that now, at the start, before I say the words, because you have a fortnight of not knowing and I am about to take it away, and I would rather you had the price first."

"I have not asked you to take anything away."

"Nobody ever does," said Ivet Sarn. "Ask."

---

Then she answered it, and it took about four minutes, and she did not use a word of it twice.

"A roll of households is not a register of persons. It is a list of doors. This court keeps one because the water has to know how many houses there are on a bank, and the fourth column of mine has had no heading over it for nine years and I have not filled it and nobody has ever asked me why, and that is a different fourth column from the one on your return and I am not going to stand here and pretend the two of them are one thing.

"A return like that one goes out to a district, and this empire has about nine hundred of them, and the office sends one to every one, and each one asks the only question this empire knows how to ask a district about a person, which is *where is that person*, and each one comes back in a column with a heading over it with whatever the district had. A district's roll of households has a household in it. It does not have a person in it, and it certainly has not got a person of twenty in it, and it has not got consent in it, and it has not got a claim in it, and the form on your sheet says in its own words that the roll does not list rights, holdings or claims.

"So the answer that came back is correct. The answer is a household. The box wanted a person and a household is what the roll had, and the office wrote the household down because the alternative was writing nothing, and a person who writes nothing in a box is a person who has been struck out with a pen and not a rule, and I have seen that on more of these in nine years than I could put a figure on, and I could not tell you one of the people who did it.

"**And the word resident means what it has always meant, which is *on the roll at a door*, and it does not mean living there, it does not mean paying anything, it does not mean anybody has agreed to anything, and a man can be resident on four rolls in four districts in this empire and be on none of them in the sense he means.**"

---

The man did not say anything for about a minute.

"That is not what I asked."

"It is exactly what you asked, and you asked it well, and I would like it noticed that you asked it because you were afraid, and not because a room wanted a record." She turned the return round on the sill so it was facing him. "**Nobody has ever asked one of these in this building. I have been at this window nine years and the only person who has ever asked me what a word on a form means is a man who came in afraid about his own household, and he is not a lawyer and he is not a clerk.** And I would like it noticed that he has no reason that is anybody else's reason, and I will tell you what I know besides, and it is not much: it is the second time in a hundred and forty years that anybody has asked another person what a word on a form meant, and the first one was a room that wanted a record, and neither of the two of them is a plan."

"I would rather not have asked," he said.

"That is the correct answer," said Ivet Sarn, "**and I want to say to you that it is the correct answer and it is also the answer a woman of twenty did not give about four hundred and thirty miles up this river, and she said no in a room with the day on it and it did not work, and you have just said the same thing in about four words and yours is going to work better than hers did, and I would like you to sit with that for a minute before you go.**"

"I have not got a minute."

"Then take it on the walk back," she said. "And understand what you have done, because almost nobody does and it is the whole of the cost. You have not learned a thing about the water. You have become a person who asked a question about a form in a room, and in about four years a man with a copy of the right page can put a line under your name saying that a clerk of this court told him what the word resident meant, and you will not be able to say that you asked about your own household and not about anybody else, because the form does not have a place to put that, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that has a place to put that, and I have looked."

---

He went. He did not say thank you and she did not want him to, and there is no form anywhere that requires a person to be thanked, and she has never once in nine years asked for it and is not going to start.

---

Then she went and got the register, and the register is on the top shelf of the second press, and it is a book with a column in it and a day against every line, and it is heavy, and it took her about a minute and a half to get it down and about four minutes to carry it back along the embankment corridor with both arms round it.

The corridor is about a hundred and forty feet long and the floor of it is wet from about the eighth hour and there is a lamp at the north end that is out, and the door of the back room is on the left about halfway, and it was open a couple of inches, which it has been every working day for nine years.

She went past it. She has been past it about two thousand times.

**A person who opens a room nobody has asked her about is a person who has given herself a duty, and a person who has given herself a duty cannot stop.**

She has got enough of those in nine years and one of them is the reason she has a paper in a drawer about a winter. She had been in that room once, in the fifth week of the fifth month, on a matter that is somebody else's, and there was nothing in it, and she has said in her own book that she knows what the presses behind her are for, and that a person with a pen at a table can put a name into a column and take a name out of a column, and that there is no instrument anywhere that stops it.

---

Then she had to do the ordinary thing with the return, and the ordinary thing is this.

A return that is copied goes into the register and a return that cannot be copied goes in a basket under the sill, because a return that cannot be copied is a return with nothing in it that this court is the person to copy, and the form says so, and the form has said so for about a hundred and forty years, and the basket is a shallow one of wicker and it is about nine inches deep and it sits under the sill of that window where everybody who comes to the window can see it.

There are returns in that basket. There have been returns in that basket in every year of the nine.

She has never thrown one away. She has never shown one to anybody. She has never told anybody what is in it, and it is nine sheets deep, and every one of them is a sheet with a column headed for the person, institution or thing engaged in the matter and a column filled in at the other end, and every one of them is the only document in the world that says how a box got filled, and the form that the basket comes from calls them rubbish.

"I have been sitting on the answers for nine years," she said, to the window, with the register under her arm. "**And there is no post in this empire whose job is to ask me what is in the basket, and if there were one it would not be a clerk of this court, because a clerk of this court is paid and a person who is paid can be told to stop.**"

The man had gone by then and there was nobody at the sill.

She did not show him. She had thought about it while he was standing there, in about nine seconds, and the reason was not caution and she said so to an empty window afterward, in the ordinary way, at about the tenth hour, in the plainest words she has.

"Because a clerk who shows a man her basket is a clerk who has given the man a reason to be interested in it, and a man with a reason to be interested in a thing cannot be left alone with it, and about four years from now a person with a copy of the right page is going to come along that corridor and ask me what the basket is, and I am going to have to say a thing about it, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for a person to say *I have had those sheets for nine years and I never asked what they were* without that sentence being the answer to a question he came with."

---

Then she put the return in the basket, on top of the others, where it belongs, and carried the register back to the press, and put it on the top shelf where it lives, and then she took her own book out of the drawer of her own desk, which is not the court's book and has no office on it and has her hand in it in about nine places.

She wrote it in her own book with no office on it, in her own hand, with the day on it: that a return had come, that the column on it was filled in at the other end in advance, that the day on it was the day the column was filled and not the day it was sent, that no space existed on the sheet for the second day, that a man at the window had asked her what the word *resident* meant, that she had answered, and why she had given the answer for nothing.

She read it back twice, and the second reading was slower, and she did not read it a third time, and at the bottom she wrote the reason, which is the reason she has written eleven times in nine years and which has not got any better:

**A hand of mine is the only hand anybody is going to be able to put side by side in about four years, and I have now done this for the eleventh time and I have not stopped and I am not going to.**

Then she put the return in the press with the other returns and copied nothing, because there was nothing in it she was the person to copy, and the fourth column of her own register was where it has been for nine years, which is empty, and she went and stood in the embankment corridor for a minute and a half and did not go into the back room, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be told she has a duty about a room she has never been inside.
