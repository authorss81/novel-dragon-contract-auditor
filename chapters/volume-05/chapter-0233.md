# Chapter 233: Nine Years Of Being Left Alone, And A Name In Somebody Else's Hand

The man with the chain is about fifty-five and he has been on the Slade Cut for nine years, and nobody on that bank has ever said his name out loud in a room except a clerk of fifty-five in an office about nine miles off a building, and he cannot read a paragraph and has never pretended otherwise and has never once been asked to pretend it.

His name is on the list of works. It is in a fair hand and it is not his hand, and it has been in somebody else's hand for about four seasons, **and he has known that for the whole of the four.**

He had been in the bay at about the fourth hour of the afternoon on the second day of the third week of the sixth month of the year after next when a book-keeper of thirty-eight came out of the store and read a piece of his own paper to him in a yard, the third time anybody has done it to him.

---

"You have done that twice and I have got very good at it," he said, before it started. "You come out here and you read it and you do not say anything about it afterwards and I have never asked you to and I am not going to start."

"It is the same paper as in the first week of the twelfth month of the year after," said Rennick Adley. "**And I am going to do the thing I do and then I am going to tell you the reason I do it, and the reason is not because I want something.** I do it because you asked me what a day under it meant in the second week of the first month of the year after next and you have not asked anybody since. A man who has asked one question and not been answered is a man who stops asking, and I am not having that on my own bank."

He read it. It took about two minutes.

Then he put a piece of chalk on the wall at the height of the third link of the chain, because the chain was on the wall and not on him and he wanted the mark where the paper was, and the mark was a small cross and it was not his and it was not the book-keeper's.

---

"What is that for," he said.

"That is a plain question and it is not about a word on a form," said Rennick Adley, "and the answer is that it is for me, and there is nothing in this empire that keeps it. In about four years a man with a copy of the right page could put that cross and that wall and this yard together and ask a question about them, and I would be the only person who could answer it, and the answer would be that a man of fifty-five asked me a question in a yard on the second day of the third week of the sixth month of the year after next. So I put a mark that means nothing on a wall that nobody looks at, and a clerk of thirty-four in this city says the same thing about hands and I did not get it from her."

"You have got it from somebody."

"I have got it from about nine people and I did not get it from any of them on purpose, and there is a woman of sixty-three in this city who put my name in a letter in the fourth week of the third month of the year after next and asked in her own hand that it not be written down," said Rennick Adley. "**You are the only person in this empire who has asked me a question this year that I have wanted to be asked, and I am telling you that at about the fourth hour of the afternoon in a yard because I will not say it anywhere else."**

---

He talked about the chain while the book-keeper wiped the chalk off his hands, and nobody had asked him to and nobody has ever asked him, and it took about four minutes.

"It is not mine. It came off a piece of a gate that came down in the second month of the year before last and a man of about thirty-eight off the second gang cut the link out of it with a cold chisel and gave it me and would not take anything and I have not seen him since." He put a thumb through the third link. "I mend things. That is the whole of what anybody knows me for and I have been at it about nine years and there is a woman at the second end who brings me a thing about twice a week and has never once said please and I have never once said it was a pleasure and we have got that arranged and neither of us put it there."

"How do you know what the paper says," said Rennick Adley, "when you have never once been able to read it."

"I know what a hand does," he said. "I do not know what it says. I know that the fair hand that comes down that road on a counting day is not the fair hand of the man whose name is in the company book. I know that a foreman's voice goes up on the last word and a book-keeper's voice does not. And I know that a piece of paper that has been folded by a man is folded with the crease at the bottom and a piece of paper that has been folded by a woman is folded with the crease at the top." He shrugged. "**I have got about nine years of that, and there is no form in this empire for it, and I would not know what to put in one.**"

---

"You have not asked me the second question yet," said the man with the chain.

"What second question."

"Whether it costs you anything," he said. "Nine years. Somebody has been writing my name at the foot of a line four times a season for about four seasons and I have watched every one of them go on and I have never asked once and I have never asked anybody else either, and I have got a question and it is the first one I have ever asked anybody in nine years, and you have read me a piece of paper and put a mark on a wall, so here it is. Does it cost you anything."

Rennick Adley thought about it for about four seconds and he answered it properly, because it was the first question anybody had asked him in that yard that was worth the asking.

"About four seconds," he said. "Somebody has to hold forty-one names while a foreman reads them out, and I am the somebody, and four seconds of a week is a thousandth of a day's pay, and I have not been paid for one of them and I am not going to be."

---

"And you are doing it for a man who cannot read a paragraph."

"I am doing it for a man who cannot read a paragraph, and I have been for about two years, and I have told nobody except a foreman of fifty-one who found out the way everybody on that bank finds out, which is from a man who was in a shed."

The man with the chain put his hand on the wall under the cross and left it there for about a minute.

"**I have not been asked anything in nine years and I have got very good at not being asked anything.**" He said the rest of it after a moment. "I worked out why about four years ago and I have never said it out loud, and I am saying it now because you have read me a paper twice, put a mark on a wall, and told me the truth about the mark."

"I worked out that it is not kindness. A man who cannot read a paragraph cannot be served with a paper, and a man who cannot be served with a paper cannot be sent for anything, and a man who cannot be sent for anything is worth nothing to anybody in this empire. That is the whole of nine years. It is the best thing that has ever happened to me and I have never once been sorry about it and I am not going to be sorry about it now."

---

"About four people in that bay cannot read a paragraph and have never been asked anything and are not going to be," said Rennick Adley.

"I am one of the four."

"You are one of the four, and I wrote a finding about the four in my own hand in the second week of the tenth month of the year after and I read it back twice, and the reason I gave in it is that a person who is paid to read is a person who can be told to stop. That reason is about a girl of seventeen and not about you, and I am not going to explain it in a yard, and you are not going to ask me."

"I was not going to ask you."

---

"I am going to say a thing about her," said the man with the chain, "and I am saying it to you and not to her, and I want you to notice that I am saying it to a man who writes forty-one names down for a living and not to a foreman, and that is on purpose and I have thought about it for about a minute and a half."

"Say it."

"There is a girl of seventeen at the back of that bay. She is on a written engagement and she is at the same rate as me and she has not been thanked and she is not going to start. Nobody on this bank has ever spoken to her about the season. I have been on this bank nine years and I have watched about four people in this bay be answered by a foreman out loud for nine years, and she is the only one of us who is paid to do the answering and she is not allowed to."

He stopped and started again.

"**I am the reason nobody asks me anything, and I have just told you what the reason is, and it took me four years.** If a person of seventeen reads a hold out loud in that bay then the thing that has been running here for nine years stops, because a thing a person does for money is a thing somebody can write down. The first thing anybody is going to write down is a name, and the name is not going to be mine."

---

Rennick Adley did not answer it for about a minute and a half, and the yard was quiet, and then he said the thing he had come out of that store to say and had not expected to say.

"**That is a better reason than mine, and it is better than the one a foreman of fifty-one has got, and it is better than the one I have got for not asking her**," he said, "and I am not going to improve it and I am not going to put it in that ledger. I am not going to tell you why not, because the reason not is that a column of mine has no names in it and a reason of yours has a man in it, and a document with a man in it is a document about the man."

"You have got three reasons and I have got one."

"I have got three reasons against telling the four and they are all bad and I have said so out loud to a bay, and you have got one and yours is about nine years of your own life and mine are about a piece of paper and a girl," said Rennick Adley. "**I am not going to put one of mine on top of yours.** A man with three bad reasons and a man with one good one are not two halves of a thing, and I have watched four people try to make them into a thing this year and every one of them ended up with a heading over it."

---

Then the man with the chain said the other thing, and it was not about the girl and not about the hold.

"About four hundred yards up that road there is a woman who owes you half a sentence and you have wanted it since the third month of the year after," he said, "and a man of about thirty from the second gang said so in that bay this morning in about nine words and did not know that he had said anything."

"He said it in a bay and I have not been in that bay since the second day of the first week of the fifth month of the year after next and neither has he been in this yard," said Rennick Adley.

"I am telling you I said it, and I am not asking you anything about it, and you are not going to tell me anything about it either, because if you tell me about it then I am a person who has been told something and I have been left alone for nine years and that is the first thing that has ended in nine years and I am not going to have it ended over a woman's half a sentence." He took his hand off the wall. "You have not asked her and you are not going to, and she has not asked you and she is not going to, and I have been standing in this yard for about four minutes and I have watched two people not do a thing, and that is nine years of mine in about four minutes and I would like it written down somewhere and I know that nobody is going to write it down."

---

**The book-keeper did not write it down.** He went back into the hired stone store, shut the door, and sat on the bench with the ledger shut on his knee, and there is a mark in chalk on a wall about four hundred yards from a person who can be served, and it is not his and it is not anybody's. About four people in that bay have still never been asked what the hold is, and two of them have now said a thing out loud to each other in a yard and a shed, and neither of them is going to say it a second time.

And about four hundred and thirty miles down this river there is a man of thirty-eight with no office and no fund and a twelfth line in a book with no office on it that he has not filled in since the third week of the ninth month of the year after. There is a man on a bank in this city who has just worked out that a girl of seventeen is the only person there who is paid to answer, and about four hundred and thirty miles of river is what it is between the two of them, and there is no fund, and nobody is going.

The book-keeper did not write that down either, and the man with the chain did not tell it to a second person, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires either of them to do the other thing. **Two men said a true thing to each other in a yard and a shed and neither of them is going to say it again, and that is the whole of the third week of the sixth month of the year after next.**
