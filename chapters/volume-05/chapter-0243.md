# Chapter 243: Nineteen Years Of Minutes About A Thing Nobody Has Been In A Room With, And One Question Entered On Her Own Motion

The office keeps eleven minute books on a shelf in a room on the ground floor, and the room has a window about two feet wide with a pane out of it, and there is a hearing room with a rail in it at the other end of a passage that smells of cold stone whatever the season is.

She is about fifty-five. She has been a clerk here nineteen years and she writes the minutes of this office and advises nobody and asks nothing, and she has never once been asked a question by anybody who was not a person who wanted something out of her, and she has said that out loud to about four people in a year and it has never once got her anything.

**A seat is a thing in a room without being a person.** She said that to a foreman's face in a shed in the fifth month of the year after next and it is in one of the eleven books, in her own hand, with the day on it, because a clerk who says a thing like that in a room writes it down, and that is the whole of what a clerk is.

---

On the fourth day of the third week of the ninth month of the year after next she did the thing she had been not doing for about four years, which is to go and look for a thing in the eleven books rather than to write one.

She wanted to know whether there is a procedure in this empire for un-filling a box.

She looked for about two days. She went through eleven books on a shelf in a room with a pane out of the window, and about nine hundred entries in nineteen years, and she did it on her own time and told nobody and was not paid for any of it, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that pays a woman for two days of looking.

---

There is not one.

That is the whole of what two days came to, and she had known it would not be there before she started, in the way a person knows a thing before they go and look for it, and she went and looked for it anyway, because a clerk who does not look has not got a finding, she has got a feeling, and a feeling is the one thing in this empire that cannot be put in a minute book and relied on by anybody afterwards.

She came in on the second day and told the other two clerks in the building, who are not in this matter and have not been in it, that she had looked, and both of them said that they had assumed there was not one and neither of them had ever looked, and one of them is about thirty and has been in that room four years and said that he had assumed it because assuming it is cheaper than looking.

"It is not cheaper," she said. "It is about four years cheaper and that is the whole of the difference and about four years is how long a thing takes to be found in a book in this building."

---

"Nobody in this empire has ever been asked to un-fill a box, and there is no form for it, and there is no form for asking whether one ought to be un-filled, and there is no form for asking a person who is in one whether they would like to be out of it." She wrote those three sentences on a loose sheet and then she did not keep the sheet. A loose sheet in a drawer is a document with no column heading over it, and a document is the only thing anybody in this empire can be held to, and she is a person who can be found.

She read it back once before she put it in the stove, because a person who does not read a thing back before she destroys it has not decided to destroy it, she has only decided to be tidy.

---

The reason there is no procedure is the reason there is almost nothing in this empire.

A procedure is a machine for making sure that the reason is not needed, and it does that by not asking. About nine hundred people were entered in a box in this office before the year turned, and that figure is a clerk's own count off the back of about nine hundred entries, and it is roughly what a class does, and she has never been asked whether the class was right about any of them.

She has administered every one of them correctly. That is not a boast and it is not a defence either. It is that the person who fills a box is a clerk and the person who signs under the box is a person, and the person who signs is the only one of the three who could have been asked, and nobody has ever asked the person who signs, and she knows why, and she knows the reason is in about nine hundred documents and not one of them is a question.

---

Two of them were in this office and she knows the two, and she has never told either of them that she knows, and she is not going to, and that is not discretion and she is not going to let anybody call it that.

The first was entered in the fifth month of the year after and it is a woman of twenty, entered as the stand for a family, and she came into the hearing room with a rail in it and said no out loud, on the fourth day of the third week of that month, and the day went on the sheet, and a piece of paper with a seal on it told her that no further consent of that person is required.

"No further consent of that person is required." She has written that sentence out about nine hundred times in nineteen years in a hand that does not vary, and she has never once been asked what it means. The woman it was written about asked a different question about a different word in a rented room about nine miles away and got a price and a refusal, and that is the only time in nineteen years anybody has put a question to a word on any paper out of this office.

The second was entered in the seventh month of the year after and it is a foreman of fifty-one on a bank about four hundred and thirty miles down a river, and she was entered by a class and not asked, and nothing came with it, and nobody has been in a room with her about it since, and she has not been asked either.

---

**And that is the thing she has not written down, and it is the worst of the two and it is about her own job and not about either of them.**

A clerk in this office has a fixed part in a thing. About nine hundred entries came in on about nine hundred sheets and on every one of the sheets there are two hands, and the first hand writes the body and the second hand signs underneath and answers for it, and every one of the nine hundred is correct.

**And the second hand is the only one of the three who can be asked**, and nobody in this empire has ever asked the second hand, and a clerk of fifty-five has just spent two days finding out that the person who fills a box is a clerk and cannot be asked at all, because a clerk is a person who advises nobody and is a person nobody needs.

"That is not a grievance," she said, to the room with a pane out of the window. "That is the arrangement, and it works, and the nine hundred were done properly. I would not want it different. **I have just worked out that the arrangement is the reason**, and I have been in it nineteen years, and I am going to write a question about it and not an answer, and I am not going to ask anybody to sign it, and there is nobody in this building I could ask."

---

On the second day of looking, at about the sixth hour, with the lamp not lit, she found the thing that is actually the finding, and it is not about either of the two.

A class is a heading.

A class is a set of people written down as though they were one line. About nine hundred entries in nineteen years came in on about nine hundred sheets, and on every one of the sheets the people are under a heading, and the heading is the class, and the class is not a person and is not an office and is not anybody, and about nine hundred people are standing in a box under it.

And here is the part that took her about four seconds and that she has not been able to put down since, in the way that a thing you have just worked out cannot be put down for a day or two: **a heading is how a person becomes a company, and a company is a thing this empire can hold wages against and give letters to and take to law**, and a man who is a person is a thing it can only send papers to. She wrote that out in the plainest words she could find and it took about four lines, and it is the truest thing in nineteen years of minutes and it is not about the law and it is not about anybody in particular, and it is a finding about a heading.

---

Then she did the thing she had come in to do, and she did it on her own motion, which is a thing a clerk may do, and there is no form for it and there is not going to be one.

She took the second book off the shelf, and it is the ordinary book with the ordinary minutes in it, and it is not a new book and there is no new book in this office, and she turned to a page and wrote in her own hand, with the day on it:

> *Entered on the motion of the clerk of this office, of her own motion and at nobody's request. Whether an entry made in a box by a class may be un-filled; and if it may not, by whom it is to remain; and if it may, on whose application, to whom that application is to be addressed, and by what hand it is to be received.*

And then at the foot of it, her own name, in her own hand, and the hour.

---

The other clerk of about thirty came in at about the sixth hour to ask whether the room wanted shutting, and read it standing up, over her shoulder, which she has not asked him to do and cannot stop him doing, and then stood there for about a minute.

"You have written a question down."

"I have."

"Not an answer."

"Not an answer." She did not put her hand over it, which she had been about to do. "**A minute with an answer in it is an order, and a clerk of this office has never entered an order in nineteen years**, and there is no form that lets her, and that is not modesty, that is the shape of the room. A question is the only thing I am allowed to put in it, and I have put one, and there are about nine hundred entries in that book and this is the first line anybody in this building has ever entered about what ought to have happened instead."

"Are you going to send it anywhere?"

"There is nowhere to send it. There is a shelf." She turned round on the chair. "And I have had four days to think about who I am going to tell, and the answer is that I am not going to tell anybody, and that is not cowardice either, it is that a person who is told has been given a duty and a person who has been given a duty cannot stop, and I have not got anything I can stop."

---

**Naming a question is not the same thing as answering it**, and the recording of a question is a document, and a minute book with the day on it and a clerk's name at the foot of it is the only protection a question has ever had in this empire. A clause was named once in a minute book in another building about four hundred and thirty miles from this one and it has been asked in that building's own court and unanswered for nine years. The reason it has survived nine years is that it is on paper with a day on it and a clerk's name at the foot of it, and if it had been said out loud in a room it would have been in nine mouths by now and would have been answered by a wrong man in a shed.

So it is written down, and it will be read in about four years by anybody who can get at this room, and it will be in a hand.

---

And the price of putting your own name at the foot of a question, which she knew before she did it and did anyway, is that a question is the one thing in this empire that makes a person answerable, and it makes them answerable in writing, and the person it makes answerable is the person who wrote it.

**She has been a clerk in this room for nineteen years and nobody has ever been able to make her answer for anything, because a clerk who advises nobody is a person nobody needs.** She has said out loud that she would like to be asked and there is not one form in this empire for a person to be asked. About nine people in this city have made a thing out of a habit in her time at these counters and every one of them ended up with a heading over it, and she has watched it and said nothing.

**And now she has asked, on paper, on her own motion, and there is no instrument anywhere that will let her be asked whether she meant it.**

---

There is one more thing about the room, and she is the only person in this empire who knows it and she has never said it out loud to anybody.

A document that proves the absence of a name is a thing this office can use. There is a notice on a bench about four hundred and thirty miles down a river with a seal at the foot of it and nothing under the seal, and its second line asks that no inference of any kind be drawn from the absence of any name, and its sixth line declines to name whoever holds the office at all.

She has read the second of those lines about nine hundred times, because a copy of it is in the second book in this room with a day against it, and she has never once said the name, and she is not going to. **That is not discretion, it is that a clerk who puts a name into a room where a document proves there isn't one has used the only weapon the office has against itself**, and about nine hundred entries a year would be very easy to stop.

---

The lamp was not lit and it was later than it ought to be and she had not told anybody that she had spent two days in eleven books.

**Nobody in this empire has been asked to un-fill a box, and there is no form for it**, and a foreman of fifty-one on a bank and a woman of twenty in a room about nine miles off a street that goes down to a river road are both in one this morning, and the woman said no out loud in a room with the day on it and the no did not work. The foreman has never been asked, and the clerk who could have administered a thing that would take either of them out of it does not know that such a thing exists, and has entered, on her own motion, with the day on it and her own name at the foot of it, the question of whether there is one.
