# Chapter 208: The Woman Who Cannot Be Asked

She had been in this city since the second month of the year after and it was now the fourth week of the tenth month, and she had got so good at a day that she did not have to think about it any more, and that was the thing she wanted to say out loud in a room and had nobody to say it to.

Bess Tarrant is sixty-three. She keeps a house. She can write and she cannot read a paragraph, and she has never once in her life been in a room by name, and those are two separate facts and she has never joined them up until the ninth month of this year. She came up the road on a cart in the second month and she has not gone home, or she has gone home and come back, and the room is hired by the day above a shop in a street and the hiring is done by a woman who comes up at about the eighth hour with a slate.

Nobody sent her. There is no form anywhere in this empire for a person who comes on her own account, and she came on her own account, and that is the only instrument she has ever had.

---

The day is this. She gets up before it is light because the room has one window and the light comes in sideways off a wall. She does not go out before about the ninth hour because there is nothing open and she has not got the money for the eating places. She goes out at the ninth hour and walks, and there is about four miles of it she can walk in a day without being asked anything by anybody, and she has got it to a wall at the end of a street that is not long, and she stands at the wall for about an hour and a half, most days, and goes back.

She cannot do anything else. There is no work in this city for a woman of sixty-three who cannot read a paragraph, and she has looked, and there is no form on which she can write *I cannot read a paragraph* and hand it to somebody, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for a person to say what they cannot do.

**There is a form for what a person is. There is not one for what a person cannot do, and that is the whole of why a woman of sixty-three can come into a city in the second month and go to the fourth week of the tenth without one question being put to her that she cannot answer.**

---

The copy is in her coat.

It has been in her coat since the second month and she took it out once, in a room above a shop, on the fourth day of the second week of that month, and she did not read it, and she cannot read it, and it is nine lines in her own hand and she has not had anybody read it to her since a doorway about four miles off the old river road where a man who cuts kindling stood and read the whole of it out loud because it was in her hand and he worked that out from the middle of it and he did not have a form.

She knows what it says. She wrote it. That is the thing nobody has understood and it is the thing she has understood and there is nobody to understand it with. Knowing what you wrote and being able to read what you wrote are two different things, and the gap between them is a person's whole life, and about a hundred and forty years of this empire is the width of it.

"You can write a thing and not read it back," she said, out loud, to a room hired by the day above a shop in a street, on the third day of the fourth week of the tenth month of the year after. "I have known that since I was about nine. And nobody has ever asked me about it, and nobody is going to, and there is no post."

---

Then the woman who hires the rooms came up at about the eighth hour with the slate, as she does.

She is about thirty and she does about nine rooms on that street and she has never once asked Bess Tarrant anything, and she is not going to, and she came up with the day on the slate and put it against the doorframe the way she puts it against nine doorframes, and said the figure.

"I have not got it," said Bess Tarrant.

"That is all right, I have got it on the slate."

"Then read me what it says."

The woman stopped.

It is four words on a slate, four days and a figure, and it is not a form and it is not a receipt and there is no office on it and it is scratched on with a piece of chalk because the woman cannot afford ink for nine rooms.

"Four days," she said, "and so much."

"Read it again."

"I have just read it."

"You have given me one number and two words, and one of the two words is standing in for the figure on that slate. **That is not reading it to me.** That is telling me the answer. I have been in this room since the second month and I have never been able to say the difference out loud to anybody and I have just said it to you, and you are the first person in this city who has been asked a question by me since the second month, and it is about a chalk mark on a slate."

The woman read it again, slowly, and put her thumb under the line while she did it, and the two of them stood in a doorway in a street for about a minute and a half.

"There," she said. "That is what it says. **And that is everything, and it has taken you ninety seconds and me nothing, and I could have done it in the second month and you have been here ever since.**"

---

Then she said the thing, and she said it in the doorway to a woman of about thirty who hires rooms by the day, and it is the whole of what she had worked out in the ninth month and it is the reason she is going to go and do what she is going to do.

"Asking works. That is what I have got wrong since the second month and it has taken me until the fourth week of the tenth month to say it and I am saying it to you because you have read me four words off a slate.

"It works on a person. It has always worked on a person. A man stood in a doorway about four miles off this road and read nine lines of my own hand out loud to me and I have not been the same since, and there was no form and no fee and no day at the end of it.

"And it has been asked once since the ninth month of this year. I do not know who asked, and I have been told that much and no more, and that is not a conspiracy and it is not a plan either. It is two accidents and they are not four hundred and thirty miles from each other, and that is the part I have had to work out for myself, because the one I know about was in this city and the other one I have only been told was a room, and a person who wants to know why nobody asks forms anything has now got the start of an answer and it is that it keeps not happening to the same person.

"And it does nothing whatever to a piece of paper. **You can ask a person until you are hoarse and the paper does not change. And a form cannot be asked at all, and I have been sitting in this room since the second month waiting for a form to change its mind, and there is no post in this empire whose job is to ask one, and the first time anybody asked another person what a word on one meant in about a hundred and forty years it was because a room wanted a record and a person happened to be in the room.**"

"And you have tried it on paper," said the woman with the slate.

"I have tried it on paper since the second month," said Bess Tarrant. "**You cannot put a question to a form. You can only fill it in, and filling it in is what it is for, and there is not a line on any of them, in about nine hundred buildings, that says a person may ask what the words mean, and I have looked at a great many of them since the second month of this year and I cannot read a paragraph of any of them and I have had that looked and read to me and that is a different thing and it does not count.**"

---

The woman in the room below came up at about the tenth hour, which she does most days, and she is about twenty-eight, and she has a chair she carries up two flights because the stair is bad, and she came up with the chair and set it down and said she had brought the salt, and stayed, which she does about four times a week.

"Read me this," said Bess Tarrant, and gave her a sheet.

The woman looked at it for a moment and then put it down on the table face down.

"No," she said.

"You have not looked at it."

"I have looked at the first line of it. I know what it is. It is a thing you have written for yourself and you want read back to you, and I am not going to be the one who reads it."

"Why not."

"Because you are not paying me and that makes it worse, not better, and I want to be fair with you about that, because everybody says the paid one is the one who can be stopped and it is the unpaid one who cannot say no at all. A woman who is paid to read is a woman somebody can tell to stop, and she can be shown the day she read it. A woman who is asked as a kindness in her own time by a woman of sixty-three she has lived above since the second month is a woman who has said yes in a room with nobody in it but the two of them, and if anybody ever comes and asks me what I read for you, I have to say yes I read it, and I have to say it to a person, and I have no slate and no day and no figure and nothing to point at."

"You have me," said Bess Tarrant. "You can say that I asked you and that I wanted to know."

"I can say it and it will not help me, because you are sixty-three and cannot read a paragraph and in about four years that is going to be the only thing anybody knows about you, and I am twenty-eight and I will be able to read a paragraph for another forty years," said the woman, "and I would rather not be a thing a person above me was heard asking for."

She sat in the chair for about a quarter of an hour and they did not talk about it again, and when she went down she took the salt and left the chair, which she always does.

---

Then Bess Tarrant told the woman with the slate what she was going to do, and she told her plainly, and she said the reason out loud, which is the reason and not a reason with a shape on it.

---

"I am going to go and stand in a room where boxes are filled in.

"That is the whole of the plan. There is no more of it than that and I have had since the second month to make a better one and there is not one, and I am sixty-three and I cannot read a paragraph and I have no money and I have a house about four miles off this road that is not shut and that I am the only person who knows is not shut.

"And I have worked out, this week, why nobody ever came to find me, and it is not the reason everybody thinks. There is a line about me in a third column with no heading over it four hundred and thirty miles down this river, and it has been there since the sixth year, and my name is not in it and it is not a name, it is a description, and I have been unfindable on purpose, and I have been proud of it, and I have said in rooms that I was the only one of us nobody could find.

"And I was wrong, and I was wrong in the most stupid way a person can be wrong. The machinery does not need me. It needs a person who writes. There is a woman in an office about nine miles from a counter who has written nineteen years of minutes and about four thousand entries in them and has never been asked what a box on a form is for, and she is the one the whole of this runs through, and she is the easiest person in this empire to find, because she is at a desk every day of her life in a building with a door on it.

"So the arrangement did not keep me out of the way of the thing that was going to happen. **It kept me out of the way of the only thing that was never going to happen, and being unfindable is the reason I was never asked and it is not the reason I was safe, and I have had those two the wrong way round for nine years and it has taken me until now to turn them over.**"

The woman with the slate looked at her for a moment.

"You could have written and asked for the room," she said. "I do not let rooms to anybody I have not had a word with."

"Then that is what it is for," said Bess Tarrant.

"It is not for the room. It is so that a person who cannot read a paragraph is a person somebody has had a word with, and that is the only thing I have wanted since the second month and I could not have said what it was and I have said it now in about ninety seconds on a step."

---

Then she paid the day out of what she had, which was most of it and not all of it, and the woman went down the street with the slate against her hip, and Bess Tarrant shut the door of a room hired by the day above a shop and stood in it with her coat on.

There was a copy of nine lines in the coat and she did not take it out.

Nobody asked her anything that day and nobody asked her anything the next day, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for asking a woman of sixty-three the same question twice, and the first asking was in a doorway in the second month of the year after and it was done by a man who cuts kindling and who has told nobody since and is not going to be asked and does not want to be.

**And the date four years off that is in a minute book in a building about nine miles from a counter in this city is about her and she does not know it exists, and there is nobody who could tell her, and she is going to go and stand in a room where boxes are filled in and she is going to find out nothing about that either, and she has decided to go anyway, and that is the whole of what she has decided.**
