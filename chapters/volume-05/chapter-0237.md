# Chapter 237: A Wage Held By A Man Who Cannot Say What It Is Held Against

The dye end is at the back of a house in a lane in a furnace town about four hundred and thirty miles up this river from Auremar, and there are nine men in it on a vats day, and the water is hot enough in the fourth month to have a skin on it, and the end of the week is the day the work gets counted and nobody has ever asked the foreman anything about the counting.

He is about forty. His name is on nothing, and it has not been on anything since he was about nineteen, and he has never asked anybody why, and he is the foreman off a dye end and he is a person about four hundred and thirty miles from a rented room in another city where a woman of twenty keeps sixteen books, and one of the sixteen is a weigh-house ledger with her wage in it and not her name under it.

**The end runs from about the second hour to about the seventh on a vats day**, and there are nine men in it, and the ends are two long vats with a fire under them and a third that is cold and used for rinsing. The poles are about nine feet and every one of the nine men has used a pole for longer than the foreman has been in the room, and the colour goes in and comes out and nobody in that end can be asked what it is called because none of them has ever asked.

He had said it to a woman of twenty twice. He had been told to keep asking. And on the fourth day of the third week of the seventh month of the year after next he did the thing he had been working out since the second day of the second week of the fourth month of the year after next, when somebody told him that he was one of about four people in this matter who could not be asked, and that nobody had told him, and that he had not noticed for forty years.

---

The man of about thirty-four came in at about the fifth hour and stood at the end of the dye end for about two minutes.

He had come up about four miles and it was raining and he had a satchel and he was in the middle of saying something about a wage and then he stopped, and the foreman let him stand there, and did not help him, and has thought about that since.

"I was in the middle of asking you for something."

"You were."

"You could have said something."

"Yes," said the foreman. "That was the decision I made at about the fifth hour and I have not improved on it. I am not going to ask you what it is, and I am not going to say anything that would help you say it, because I have been in this end for about nine years and every man in it has come to me at some point and not been able to finish, and the one time somebody helped him finish, he never came to this end again. I do not know that it was because of me and I am not going to find out by helping one."

---

"Why will you not ask him."

"He has a wage he has not been paid and the wage was stopped in the second week of the third month of the year after and I am the only person in this city who can put it back and I cannot do that for a reason nobody will give me, and he came up four miles in the rain to be told why, and I do not have it." The foreman put the pole down across the vat. "That is the whole of what I know and I have not told one person that until about half an hour ago and I am telling you because you are the fourth man in this end today and the other three already know."

The man of about thirty-four went out into the wet and did not come back that day and nobody knows where he went and the foreman did not ask and has not asked and there is no form in this empire that requires a man to say where he went.

---

He did it at about the sixth hour, standing at the end of the dye end, with the vats behind him and nine men in the room, and he did not call them together and he did not stop the work, because a foreman who stops the work to make a speech has begun to make a speech of his own job.

Then he said it, and he said it four times over about half an hour, in the ordinary way, one man at a time, in the middle of ordinary work, and he made himself do the fourth one to somebody who had come in.

"**I am holding a wage and I cannot tell you what it is held against."**

---

The first man said nothing and went back to the vat. The second one asked what wage and he told him, a woman's, a dye-house worker, held since the second week of the third month of the year after. The third one said the same words a man of about thirty had said in a lane in the second week of the third month of the year after next, which was that somebody has to say it, and he had said it twice, and it was not him who had to say it.

Then a man of about thirty came in to fetch a barrow and he said it to him, because he had decided to say it to somebody who had just arrived, and a thing said to nine people is not a thing said to nine people. It is a thing said to the air.

---

"You have said that four times and I have counted them," said the man of about thirty, "and nobody has said one word back, and I have been in this end about eleven years and I have never once seen a foreman say a thing like that."

"Nothing came of it," said the foreman. "That is what I wanted. I have been asking about that wage for about a year and I have been told to keep asking, and I have come up two stairs three times for nothing, and I have worked out in a rented room in a city about four hundred and thirty miles down this river that I am one of about four people in this matter who cannot be asked."

"I am forty. **I have been forty for most of a working life and I did not notice that until somebody told me in a room in about four seconds.**"

---

"And what are you doing about it."

"I have just done it," said the foreman. "And I want to say the rest of it out loud as well because I have had it in my mouth since the second day of the second week of the fourth month of the year after next and I have never said a word of it to anybody. A thing said to one person is a document. A document is the only thing anybody in this empire can be held to, and I have never in my life been held to one thing that was true, and I would like there to be one."

"That is a strange thing to want."

"It is a very ordinary thing to want and every person in this trade wants it and there is no form anywhere in this empire for wanting it, and the ordinary way it is met is that a man is held to the things that are against him, and nobody in this trade is held to a single thing they have said out loud in a room with the rest of the end in it."

---

Then he said the cost, because he had worked the cost out in about four days and he was not going to be a man who has made a decision and not said the price of it.

"I am the only person in this city who can pick up or put down the whole of what she has," he said. "I have not told one person in this room what I have just done and I am not going to, and I have not asked anybody to keep it, and there is no form for that either. **I have made myself a person who has said a true thing about himself out loud, and in about four years it can be repeated, and the only two people it can be repeated to are her and me.** She is twenty and I am forty and about four hundred and thirty miles of river is between us and neither of us is going to be able to help the other one."

---

A man of about fifty came in from the second end and asked whether a vat wanted turning, and the foreman said yes and told him, and then looked at him for about two seconds, which is not what a foreman does, and the man of about fifty turned the vat and did not ask and that was all that happened.

Then the weigh-house sent down a boy of about nine with a slate, and the foreman gave him the day's count in about four numbers and did not say the thing to a boy of nine, and then he said it to the boy of about nine anyway, in about four seconds, and the boy of nine wrote it on a slate because that is what a boy of nine does with anything anybody says to him.

And that is where he stopped, and he stopped because he had got to the end of it, and not one person in that end said anything, and he had not asked them to.

"I said it to a child," he said. "**I have said it five times and the fifth one was to a boy of nine with a slate and it is on a slate, and I have not got a slate and neither has she and that is the safest it has ever been."**

---

---

And the thing that made it a decision and not a thing he had said had been in him for most of a week, and it had nothing to do with the wage at all. He got it out at about the seventh hour to nine men and a boy of nine.

"The sixth year of a guild's blank list," he said. "There was no list of works that year at all. The whole trade ran for a season on what people were told at the gate and on what a man said in a yard, and I was about thirty-four and I was in this end, and I was the only person in here who wrote anything down. I wrote it in a book of my own that is not here and has not been here for about nine years, and I have never once regretted throwing it out and I have thought about it about four times since, and every one of those four times has been since the second day of the second week of the fourth month of the year after next."

"What was in it."

"Days," said the foreman. "Who was on and who was not, and about four names of men who said they would come and did not come, and I have not got the names and I have not got the days either, and that is the correct thing and I want it said in here that it is the correct thing. **Nobody in this empire pays a man to hold a list and everybody in this empire turns out to need one, and those two facts have never once been in the same room, and I have been in both of the rooms."**

---

They came in at about the eighth hour and a boy of about nine came in at the seventh hour with a slate and did not say a word.

And on the fourth day of the fourth week of the seventh month of the year after next the weigh-house sent word down that the wage was held, which is what it has said every time, and he said back the same words he has said twice before, and it was a different thing, and the only difference was that nine men had heard him say the first one first.

---

The letter had come to the house in the lane four days before that, and it was in the inside pocket of the coat he was standing in, and he took it out at about the eighth hour and looked at it and put it back.

It is a wage-day notice and it has a day on it and no name on it, and it says a sum and it does not say against what, and he is the only person in that house who has ever seen it.

"**He is holding a document that could be held to him, and it is the first one in forty years, and he has had it in a coat for four days and has told nobody, and he is not going to, and the reason is that nine people heard him say out loud that he could not say what a wage was held against and a document would let him say it without a word.**"

He had been in a house in a lane four hundred and thirty miles up this river and had said one true thing five times in about half an hour and had not put his name under it and had not asked anybody to. There is a slate in a weigh-house in that town with a number and a half-sentence on it and nine people in a room who heard it and a woman of twenty about four hundred and thirty miles down it who does not know yet.

There is a man in a city about four hundred and thirty miles down this river that a woman of twenty walked about nine miles to, on her own account, to ask a question, and nobody sent her, and there is not one form in this empire for a person who comes on her own account, and about four hundred and thirty miles of road is what it costs anybody who wants to go and be asked a question. He is a man of thirty-eight with no office and no fund and a bar of his own that came off him on the twelfth of the ninth month of last year on a line of his own writing, and the foreman of a dye end has heard about him twice in about a year and does not know what he is called and is not going to find out, and that is a thing he has chosen and not a thing he lacks.

And about four hundred and thirty miles down this river there is a bank about four hundred and forty foot long with a guarantee standing on it that nobody has answered and about sixty children under sixteen inside it, and about nineteen people behind that bank who said no again and are not going to be asked. About four people in a bay who cannot read a paragraph and have never been asked anything, and a girl of seventeen on a written engagement who is not thanked and is not going to start, and a book-keeper of thirty-eight whose wage is held against a roof he put there himself, and a foreman of fifty-one who has said four reasons out loud and every one of them was bad.

Four askings in about a hundred and forty years and no post anywhere in this empire whose job is to ask, and the count has not moved since the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after next. It is four, and a man of about forty has decided to say one true thing about himself out loud in a room of nine people, and nobody has thanked him and nobody is going to and there is no form in this empire that requires it.
