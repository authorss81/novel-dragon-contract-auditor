# Chapter 212: A Woman Stands In A Room And Says Her Own Name

She had been in this city since the second month of the year after and it was the second day of the second week of the twelfth month, and she got up before it was light, and she did not go out before about the ninth hour as she does every day, and she went at the ninth hour and she went the other way.

Bess Tarrant is sixty-three. She keeps a house about four miles off the old river road that is not shut and that she is the only person who knows is not shut. She can write and she cannot read a paragraph. She has a copy of nine lines in her own hand in her coat and she has not read it and cannot read it and has had it read to her once, in a doorway, by a man who cuts kindling, and there was no form and no fee and no day at the end of it.

Nobody sent her. There is no form anywhere in this empire for a person who comes on her own account, and she came on her own account, and that is the only instrument she has ever had.

---

The building is off the old river road and it is about nine miles from a counter, and she did not know it was nine miles and did not care and would not have asked. What she knew about it, in the first week of the twelfth month, was what a woman of about sixty-three can find out about a building without being able to read a paragraph, which is: there is a door, and the door is open, and a man comes in and out of it carrying trays, and a tray is a thing with a flat board on it and about nine hundred of the same line in this empire are printed on the front of them.

She stood in the doorway for about four minutes and she counted the trays. Two went up while she stood there and one came down. Then she went in.

---

The room is on the ground floor and it has a window about two feet wide with no pane in it and a shelf against the far wall with books on it, and there are eleven of them, and a table, and a woman of about fifty-five at the table who did not get up.

There were two other people in it. A man of about thirty with a coat on was waiting by the door with nothing in his hands, and a man of about sixty was at the shelves with his back to her, and neither of them turned round, and neither of them asked her anything, and the woman at the table said:

"Sit down if you want to sit down."

"I have come to stand," said Bess Tarrant.

---

That was the whole of what she said for the first half hour and she has not improved on it since, and it is the truth, and it is more than she came with.

The clerk of about fifty-five put her pen down, which she does about twice a year, and asked her the questions a clerk asks, and they were ordinary questions and they were not about a form.

"Are you served."

"No."

"Are you in a matter."

"No."

"Then sit down if you want to sit down," said Orla Denning, "because I am going to be a while and there is a chair."

---

The next part took about four minutes and there were two other people in the room the whole of it, and neither of them said anything, and that is a fact and not a way of writing.

A woman of about fifty-five asked a woman of sixty-three what her name was.

It is not a strange question. It is the first question of about nine hundred every day in a building like this one, and the woman of about fifty-five had asked it about four thousand times and had never once thought about it, and she thought about it that morning and it took her about four seconds and then she had to stop for a minute and a half before she could do the rest of the work.

**She had heard a person say a name in that room.** Not a clerk, not a party, not a return, not a book, not a hand at the foot of anything. A person, out loud, in a room, and it was not copied out of anything, and it was not written down, and it was not the second time in nineteen years because it was the first time in nineteen years.

Bess Tarrant said it. She said it in the ordinary way and she did not say it twice and she did not spell it, and it took her about two seconds.

"Thank you," said Orla Denning, and then stopped, because she had nineteen years of a room in her head and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a clerk to say thank you at a table.

---

Then the old man at the shelves had his back to her and finished what he was doing and came round, and he was about sixty, and he had a book under his arm, and he said, "Are you the one for the water?"

"I have not come about the water."

"Then I have wasted your morning," said the man, and went out, and did not apologise, and meant it, and it was the plainest thing anybody said to her in that room.

And the man of about thirty by the door had not looked at her once, and she found out afterwards from the woman with the slate that he is a carrier and carries trays up and down that corridor and has for about nine years and that he has never in his life asked anybody anything, and that a woman of about fifty-five had told him in the first week of the ninth month to read a head of a file out loud on the stairs, both lines, which is not usual.

That is the whole of the room. Three people and a shelf of books and a tray going up a corridor and a window with nothing in it.

---

Then the clerk came back to the table and put her hand flat on it, which she does about twice a year, and asked her the question that was actually the question.

"What do you want."

And Bess Tarrant told her, and she told it in nine sentences, standing, because she had said she would stand.

"I have come to stand in a room where boxes are filled in. I have had that since the second month of this year to make a better plan of and there is not one. **I have read in my own coat a thing I cannot read, and I have had it read to me once in a doorway by a man who cuts kindling, and I have worked out since that asking works on a person and has never once worked on a piece of paper, and a form cannot be asked at all.** And I came on my own account and nobody sent me and there is no form anywhere in this empire for a person who comes on her own account, and I have looked for one and there is not one, and I am sixty-three and I have a house about four miles off that road and no money and that is the whole of what I have."

She stopped, and then she said the thing she had come nine miles to say, and she said it to a woman of about fifty-five and not to a room.

"And I know why nobody ever came to find me and I have had it the wrong way round for nine years, and it is that the machinery does not need me. It needs a person who writes. And you are that person, and you are at this table every day of your life, and you have written nineteen years of minutes and about four thousand things into them and nobody has ever once asked you what a box on a form is for. **Including me, until about four minutes ago, and I am sixty-three and I have come nine miles to say a thing to a person instead of to a building, and I would like it noticed that I did not write it down, and I cannot, and that is why I am standing here and not sitting down.**"

---

Orla Denning did not answer it at once, and the silence went on for about a minute and a half, and she has thought about the length of it since.

**A person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and she had known that since about the seventh month of next year.** And then, in the first week of the seventh month of this year, a woman of twenty walked about four miles to this door and asked her a question about a form out loud in this room and she answered it in about four minutes, and it was not a question about a word on a form and it is not one of the three, and she has not been asked one thing since.

"There is no form for a document about a person who did not ask to be in one," she said, "**and I know that better than anybody in this empire, and I have had a woman of twenty stand in this room and refuse to be thanked for an answer she came four miles for, and she was right to refuse and I am not going to thank her.** And I have not thanked you and I am not going to and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be thanked."

Then she stopped and said, "I am going to do something and I am going to tell you what it is before I do it, and you are not to stop me, and the reason is that in about four years somebody is going to come into this room with a copy of the right page and ask me a question, and I would rather be able to say what I did than what I did not do."

---

She got up off the chair, which nobody had asked her to, and went to the shelves, and took down one of the eleven books, which is a book of minutes, and did not open it, and put it back.

"That is a record of what a room decided," she said. "It is not a register of people and this office has never kept one and never needed one, because the people are in other districts' books and we never had to look at them. **A person who has been in a box is in a roll of households in a district about four hundred and thirty miles from this table, and it is in nobody's book in this building,** and I could go and look at about nine hundred districts' rolls tomorrow and find out whether you are in one of them, and there is nothing whatever to stop me, and I have a book of about nine hundred and forty names on a shelf behind you that I could do it out of in about four days."

"Then do it," said Bess Tarrant.

"No," said Orla Denning. "**I am not going to, and that is the decision I made at about the third minute of this and I have not been asked for it by anybody, and I would like it entered that a person of sixty-three who cannot read a paragraph asked me for the one thing that would find her and I said no.**"

---

The reason took about a minute and a half and it was not a good reason and the woman who gave it said so.

"I have kept those books for nineteen years and there is no name in any of them. That is not carelessness, that is the only shape a minute book has got, and it is the only reason a person who has been entered in a box in the fifth month of this year can be in a room this morning and not be findable, and I have worked that out in about a minute and I have not got a better account of it than that.

"**A name in a book does not go looking.** A name in a book only goes looking when somebody goes looking, and I am the person in this building who goes looking, and if I go looking then I am doing to a woman who came on her own account the identical thing that was done to a woman of twenty in the fifth month of this year, in a box, in about nine seconds, by a person who was not a villain and who is sitting at this table."

"And you would not be able to say one word against it. You cannot read a paragraph. I would have to read you what I found, and I would choose the words, and you would be stood in this room being told about yourself by a stranger who put a name in a column in about four days and never asked anybody, and that is what happened to you in the ninth month and it is what would happen to you again in the first week of the twelfth, and the difference is that the second time it would be me."

---

Bess Tarrant stood in a room on the ground floor of a building off the old river road and worked on that for about a minute and a half and she did not say anything while she worked on it, and then she put her hand in her coat and took out the copy of nine lines in her own hand and held it out, and Orla Denning did not take it.

"That is yours," said Bess Tarrant. "I am not giving it to you and I have not read it and I cannot read it, and I have had it read to me once and I did not write down who read it and I am not going to tell you who read it and that is on purpose."

"I would have read it."

"I know you would have," said Bess Tarrant, "and that is the difference between a man who cuts kindling and you, and it took me the whole of the second month to be able to say it out loud. **He did not ask to keep it.** You asked. And I have been in this city since the second month and I have watched about nine people be kind to me for no reason and I know what it is when it is on the way to becoming a document."

---

She put it back in her coat, and she did not go straight out, and the clerk did not ask her to and the tray went up the corridor and came down again while she was standing there.

"There is one more thing," said Bess Tarrant, "and I have known it since the ninth month and I have not got a use for it, and I am going to say it and then I am going to go and stand in the street for a while, because I have come nine miles and I am sixty-three and I would like to be let go."

"Go on."

"**There is a day entered in one of the eleven books on that shelf, in the first week of the fifth month of this year, with my name at the foot of the line, and it is about four years off,** and I have found out nothing whatever about it, and I am not going to find out anything about it, and I have thought about it on the walk here and I have decided that if I had found out about it I would not be able to do the thing I came here to do, and I would rather not know and stand in a room for nothing."

The room was quiet for about a minute.

Then the clerk of about fifty-five said, "I entered that day and I did not know what I was entering, and I have not known since, and you are the only person in this empire who could tell me what it is for, and you have just told me you are not going to."

"That is right," said Bess Tarrant.

And nobody asked her one word about it, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be told a thing they have asked not to be told, and about four minutes later she was out in the street with her coat done up in a street that goes down to the river road, and she stood at the end of it for about an hour and a half the way she stands at a wall most days, and the day was going, and she did not go back to the room above a shop until the light had gone off the wall.

She had come on her own account, and nobody sent her, and there is no form for it, and she had stood in a room where boxes are filled in and had given her name in it to a stranger out loud, and nothing whatever had come of it, and that was the whole of what she got, and she had said in advance that it would be, and she was right.
