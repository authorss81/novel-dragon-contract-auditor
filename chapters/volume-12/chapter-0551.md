# Chapter 0551: The Shed On The Slade, And A Foreman Of Fifty-One Who Begins Leaving The Bundle At The End Of Her Own Bench

She had begun leaving the ordinary work of that shed at the end of the bench where the two of them sit, about nine mornings a month, and on the second morning of that run a girl of seventeen put it back in the middle without saying one word.

It is the second day of the first week of the fourth month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fourth hour.

---

The reason is a sentence she said out loud in that bay about a year ago and has not said since and is not going to say again and is not going to be asked for.

She said then that a girl of seventeen at the back of that shed had not been asked one thing in about a year and was not being given the chance to turn anything down. She said that anybody in this matter includes the girl at the back of that bench. She said that a second of a second put in front of that girl tomorrow would go nowhere in nine hundred buildings, and that a person who did not want a thing had no way of holding the knowing of it on to anything.

Nine hundred buildings and not one column in any of them for the fact that a person did not want a thing.

She has been a foreman in that bay for eleven years. There is a paper on a board by that door that no hand has gone near a line of since before she came to that bench, and she knows what a class is for, and what a class is for is putting a person back where they have been by doing nothing.

The bench in the shed on the Slade is a length of stone running the width of the bay. It stands on two frames, and the wood in them has gone grey where forearms have gone over them for eleven years and is dark at both ends. What light gets into the shed comes down off the roof and puts a mark up the wall as high as a hand and then leaves the rest of it alone, so neither end of the stone stands in any of it, and each of them works in their own shade at their own end of it. The boards of the floor carry a sound and nothing else in the place does.

---

She thought about it for about a fortnight and then did the only thing available to a woman in that bay who has got no authority over anybody in it.

She did not ask. There is no paper in this empire that asks a person of seventeen whether she would rather not, and she is not going to invent one, and she has not got a heading and she is not going to get one, and a foreman of a shed is nobody's agent.

What she did was put the work where the girl could reach it.

A foreman of a shed can move one kind of thing and it is the ordinary work, and ordinary work can be handed to a girl of seventeen without a word being said to anybody, and that is the whole of the reason there is nothing else in there for her to move.

---

She did not see the first one happen. She came back up that bay from the door at about the fourth hour with an armful and the bundle was in the middle of that bench and the girl had her eyes on what she was doing, and the middle of that bench is where a bundle has gone every working morning of the four years anybody can remember.

She put it at the end again on the second morning. It was in the middle again on the third.

She was a working hour off her own arms before she had put anything down at all, and she came the rest of the way back at the rate she walks it. The girl at the other end of the stone had both hands on what she was working at and did not look up and went on at the same rate, and the bundle was sitting in the middle of the bench with neither of them at it.

Nobody in that shed saw any of it. The chain went the length of the stone and came back under a hand that has been on it eleven years. The one with the book turned a page. And a foreman of fifty-one stood at the back where she stands and worked out what had been done to her.

---

**A girl who puts a thing back has made a decision. She has decided twice and she has not been asked for a word of it. There is nothing in this empire that takes her as the one who decided. That is not a complaint about her. It is the whole of why she did it. And it is the same thing I said out loud in this bay about a year ago and put in front of her and had nowhere to put what she would have done with it.**

She had wanted a hand on a bundle of ordinary work to be a thing a person could put back. She had got it twice. She had wanted a decision in that bay to be the cheapest thing in it. It is the dearest. A person who has made one is a person a thing can be put in front of afterwards, and what she had wanted was a place where the girl at the end of that bench could have said no and been a girl who had said no and gone on sitting there.

---

She came along the bench from the end her own hand is on and she came at it slowly, because the whole length of it is the width of the shed and the girl was working at one end of it with nothing in between, and she stopped short of the girl's hands and stood there, having come the whole length of the floor to be within reach of somebody's hands, and there is no way to be at the end of the stone without it.

She went along the bench at about the half hour on the third morning of that run and squared that bundle up with the heel of her hand and put it at the end, in front of the girl, about a foot from her hands, and she did not say one word about having moved it and she did not look at her while she did it.

The girl picked it up at about the next hour. She did it the way she has always done that work, which is to get her hands under the near edge and lift it onto her own without her face changing, and there was no word at the sixth hour in that shed about who had put a bundle where, and there is not going to be one this month.

---

What she wanted was for the girl to be able to turn something down. What she got is a girl who has taken a bundle out of a foreman's place, out of a foreman's hands, out of a place a foreman chose, and nobody can be shown which of two things happened there.

She came out of that bay at the sixth hour with her own hands in a worse state than they had been at the fourth and she did not look at them.

The count at the far end of the stone is made at about the sixth hour, and she was back at the back of the shed when it was made, and she was not wanted at the far end of the bay for any part of it.

There is not one column in nine hundred buildings that takes a foreman for leaving a bundle of ordinary work at the end of her own bench. There is not one that takes the girl for putting it three feet back. There is not one that takes the woman of fifty-one for standing at the half hour in the middle of a bay and working out, correctly, that she has spent nine mornings a month wanting to give a person the right to refuse and has ended by taking away a place where a person could have refused.

The lane went on outside that door. The paper on the board by it has not been filled in. And the girl at the end of that bench had been asked nothing all morning and had refused nothing all morning and had done one thing with her hands that is not going into any column in this empire, and nobody in that shed is going to be thanked for it.
