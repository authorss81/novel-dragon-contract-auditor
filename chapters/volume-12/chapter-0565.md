# Chapter 0565: The Seventh Week Down A Lane, And A True Sentence Said Out Loud Over A Tin

She came down that lane for the seventh week to take a sheet of paper back off a top step, and the woman at the bottom of that stair was going the other way with a coin in her hand.

It is the fourth day of the third week of the seventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is between the third hour and about the sixth.

---

The boards of that passage had been dry for a fortnight. That was the whole of her errand and she had said as much on the step below the turn and the other one had not said anything back.

Then the other one picked the tin up off the top step with both hands and stood with it against her chest, and the coin went in over the rim without the lid coming off, because a lid shut straight is a lid that does not have to be opened to get at what is inside it.

She was holding about nine years of one man's rent up against her chest in that passage and she had her head bent over it and she was doing her own work.

The woman of about thirty-five was on the step below the turn with the wet fold of her own paper crushed in her hand, and she was looking at the boards, and the boards were dry.
Neither of them was in a hurry and neither of them was doing anything for the other one. The light off the lane came in at the near end, lay down along the boards in one run, reached the step below the turn and stopped there, and the two of them stood in the same run of it together. The paper had been on the top step for a week and it had gone stiff and the fold had come out of it, and she had not been near it on any morning of that week, and that was the whole of the errand she had come down the lane with.

---

**I have not ruled that column in nine years because I do not want to rule it. That is the whole of it and I have never said it out loud to anybody in nine years. I am saying it now, and you are carrying a tin, and that is all right.**

She said it in the ordinary way, at the ordinary volume, in the middle of an ordinary morning, to a woman who had her head bent over a tin and was counting a week into it and was not going to be told anything.

The woman at the bottom of that stair did not turn round and did not stop and did not put her hand on the tin any harder than she had been putting her hand on it. She finished what she was doing at the rate she does everything, and she set the tin down on the top step with the lid flat, and she took her finger off the lid before she took her hand away, in case it wanted to go again.
The lid went down flat and her finger came off the edge of it before her hand came away and the tin stood on the step making the sound it makes, and the woman of about thirty-five heard the whole of that and did not move while any of it was going on. The sentence was finished and there was nothing to add to it and no way of taking it back off the boards once it had gone out of her mouth at an ordinary hour on an ordinary morning, and she stood on the step below the turn with her arms down at her sides and waited for nothing in particular, and what she had waited for did not happen.

"That is the week," she said. "That is a week of it and not the whole of it."

And she went along the passage with the cloth in her hand, and she did not stop, and she did not look along that passage on her way.

---

There are two people in this empire who were in that passage that morning, and one of them said a thing out loud and the other one was working.

That is all. Nobody in this city can be shown having heard it. Nothing in this empire will take the woman who spoke as the one who spoke. Nothing in it will take the other woman as the one who was standing there. Nothing in it will take either of them as the one who stayed quiet.

A second ear is the only thing this matter has ever found that will put a thing into the world without anybody being asked anything, and it has cost this woman of thirty-five everything she had to pay with it, and what she has paid is the thing she has had for nine years, which is that there is no room anywhere in nine hundred buildings she can be put in as having said a thing.

She has made one.

She has made one room she is in it, on one working morning, on a step below a turn, and there is exactly one other person alive who was in it, and that other person cannot be shown to have been in it either, and so the two of them are in it together and neither of them is in it at all.

---

She did that sum on the way up the lane and she did it twice because it is the only arithmetic she has ever done about herself.

There are two of them. There is a lane and a house and a passage and two steps and a turn, and the passage goes from the step at the front door to the step at the turn and the length of it is about as long as that table, and a person standing on the step below the turn is in it the whole time and cannot be anywhere else.

She could not find one person who could have been in that passage and not have been in it, and she could not find one person who could be shown to have been in it, and those two are the same absence, and she has been living in the middle of that absence for about nine years without noticing it because she had never needed a second person for anything.
The books in her own house are on a table in her own front room, and the four headings over the four columns are in her own hand, and a fifth would have been a heading over a passage with a person in it on a working morning, and she has known what that heading would say for about six weeks and has not written it and is not going to. A thing a person cannot put under a heading cannot be given to anybody, and she has been finding it out since she was twenty-six, and this week is the first time she has wanted to hand it to somebody standing within arm's reach.

---

The sheet of her own paper was still on that top step with the wet side up and the fold gone over, and she picked it up and put it under her arm, and the boards were dry, and the tin was on the top step at the turn in the gap between the edge of the step and the skirting.
She folded nothing. It came off the step in the shape it had been left in, with the fold gone out of it and the wet side stiff, and she put it under her arm the way she had put it under the tin on the morning she brought it down the lane, and it was the only thing she ever put under anything in the whole of that house.

She went up that lane at the pace she had come down it and neither of her feet stopped on the step.

There is not one column in this city that any part of that morning can be entered in, and nobody is going to be thanked for it, and the woman at the bottom of that stair has never once in twenty years said a true thing out loud in that room and did not say one this morning either.
She has been down the lane seven weeks and has said one true thing out loud in it, and she is going to keep on doing the sum on the walk up, because it is the only part of that walk that has ever been worth the length of it. It is on the step behind her at the top of that turn, where a tin has been kept in one place in that house for twenty years, and it will be on that step at the first hour on a morning when she is not on the lane at all.
