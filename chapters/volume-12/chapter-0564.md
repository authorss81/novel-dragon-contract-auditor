# Chapter 0564: Two Steps And Round A Turn, And A Tin Set Down In The Same Place On The Boards

She had decided on the way down the lane that she would say it in a sentence and not a word, and she got as far as the second step.

It is the second day of the second week of the seventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is between the third hour and about the sixth.

---

There are two steps and then a turn and the tin is on the top step at the turn, and she has been down that lane for six weeks and the tin has been on that step for about twenty years, and it will be standing there on a morning when neither of them is in the house.

About nine years of one man's rent went into it. Nobody has ever counted it. Nobody has ever written it down and nobody has ever asked her what is in it, which is a thing she has been grateful for for about as long as she has been able to be grateful for anything.

She has never once asked that woman one thing either.
She came down the lane at the hour she has come down it every week of the six, with the book shut under her arm, and the sentence had been ready before the lane began and was about a tin and about nothing else, and she had gone over it twice on the way down and once more at the front step with her hand on the door. The way up climbs away from the step and she has walked it at one rate for six weeks whatever it is doing underfoot. The book went from under her arm into the inside of her coat at about the top of the lane and stayed there, and both her hands were empty for the length of the passage.

---

"I will take it up."

The woman at the bottom of that stair had her back about half turned and she had a cloth in her hand and she had been at the boards at the front of that ground floor, which is the only cleaning in that ground floor and is done to the boards and not to the tin.

"It goes up two steps and round a turn," she said. "It does not go up any further than that."

"I will take it up two steps and round a turn."

"It does not go up any further than that," she said again, and she had not turned round.
She was at the boards at the front of the ground floor and the cloth went over them in a long stroke and came back, and the marks are about two inches apart down the whole length of the front, and it is the only sound of a kind in the room. The woman of thirty-five was up on the second of the two steps with the book shut against her ribs and the sentence ready, and the second time she said it she said the same words in the same order as the first time, which is not a thing a person does who has not been listening to herself.

---

**She has said it twice in about as long as it takes a lid to stop rocking. I have come down that lane six weeks, four of them with nothing in my hands, and I have not been refused one thing out of any of them, and I am not going to be refused a tin, and I have not worked out yet what I would do if I were.**

The woman at the bottom of that stair put the cloth down on the boards by that door where she had picked it up from, and she came up the two steps, and she took the tin off the top step with both hands and carried it round the turn and set it down.

She set it down in the same place. On the same boards, with the same corner nearest the wall, in the same gap between the edge of the step and the skirting that has been the gap for twenty years. She put the lid down on it flat and took a finger off it before she took her hand away, in case it wanted to go again.

Then she went back down the two steps and picked up the cloth off the boards and went along the passage.

She did not say no. She has not said no to anybody in this house in twenty years and she is not going to start with a tin, and a thing carried up two steps and round a turn and put down in the same place is not a no either. It is a thing done.
The tin went round the turn in about as long as a woman takes to get a cloth off a table and put it down again, and she did not set it down in the middle of the boards to save herself the corner, and she did not set it down on the lower step where a person could get at it more easily, and she set it down where it had been standing for twenty years, and the whole of what she did was that.

---

**That was an act. I have been refused things before in my own house by people who would not look up and this was not that and I know the difference now. She carried it round the turn in front of me and set it down where it was and went along that passage, and I have been in a room with a refusal tonight and there is not one column in this city that any of it could go in.**

She put her hand on the front board of the tin where the other one's hand had been, and the tin was warm on that side and not on the other, and she took her hand off again.
She kept it there after that, longer than she meant to, and it was still warm on the one side where the other one's hand had been and had gone cold on the side hers was on, and she has worked out since that a thing somebody else's hands were on a moment ago is a thing a person cannot do anything about.

---

The boards on the top step at that turn are wet on the underside of them and dry on the top, and a tin standing on nine years of a man's rent with a straight lid on it does not care which it is, and she came down that lane on four weeks of saying nothing and one week of carrying something and this week of saying a thing in a sentence, and she has found out that the problem she came down with was about her.

---

She stood on the step below the turn for about as long as it takes a sheet to be squared.

She had come down that lane for six weeks. She had got nothing. She had asked for the one thing she had come down for and she had got a tin carried up two steps and round a turn and put back down on the same boards in the same gap, and the sheets in that passage were dry that morning because the wind had been out of the west since the dark.
The boards were dry and would be dry again on the next morning, and the whole of what she had come down the lane for had been done with a tin and had taken about as long as a cloth takes to fold. Behind her a tin stood on a step at a turn with the lid flat down on it in a gap it has been in for twenty years, and nobody in the house was ever going to be told that anything had been said in the passage or that a tin had been carried round a turn and put back.

She went up the lane at the pace she had come down it and did not turn round on the step.
