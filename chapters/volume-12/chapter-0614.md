# Chapter 0614: A Second Of That Week's Minutes, And A Man At A Sill Who Would Not Put His Other Hand To It

The second of that week's minutes has to go down that lane in a hand before the end of the week, and the man at that sill signs about nine hundred sheets a year and did not put his other hand to that one.

It is the fourth day of the fourth week of the seventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fifth hour.

---

The tin at the near end of that stone has two pens in it and the two of them are the same pen, and about three feet of that sill has had nothing set on it since a pot was moved four inches along it. The woman of nineteen has been at the far end of that stone at the third hour every morning for about eighteen weeks putting armfuls down at the end nearest the door.

The door at the back of that floor has been left standing open for about six weeks, and the second of each week's minutes goes in on the older woman's own hand behind that door. On a shelf under the window at the back of the room there is a question which does not finish, and her own name is written underneath it, and it has been on that shelf about eleven years.

He has been reaching into that pot with his other hand for about four months and cannot say why and has not been asked.

---

He signed the first of that week's minutes with his own hand at about the third hour the way he signs the other hundred and something in a month, and then he came back along that stone at about the fifth hour and stopped at the near end of it and put his own hand flat on the second of them.

"I am not signing that one."

"That is the second of them."

"I know which one of them it is."

"There is a space at the foot of it and every week for nineteen years there has been a mark in that space, and the second of them goes out of here in a hand."

"The first of them has got my other hand in it and the second of them has not, and I am not going to say why, and I have been signing sheets at that end of that stone since I was forty-two and I have never once not signed one."

---

She came to the near end of that stone with the second of those minutes in her hand and put it down on the wood in front of him and squared it.

"You have signed every one of these for nineteen years and you have had a hand that does it in about four sheets in a year, and you have not put it on this one, and I have carried armfuls up and down that floor for four years and I have never once asked you for anything, and I am not going to start this week either, and that is the only reason I am standing here."

"Then stop standing here."

"I will when that sheet has gone out of the building."

"It will not go out with nothing at the foot of it. It will go as a sheet, and everybody who opens it will be looking for a mark, and they will not find one, and then they will be looking for whoever carried it, and they will find you inside about a day."

"Then they will find me," she said, "inside about a day, and I have been found before."

---

The door at the back of the floor stood open as it has for six weeks and the woman of fifty-three came out of it and walked the whole length of the corridor to the near end of that sill, four foot further than she came last time, and stopped there and put her hand on the edge of the boards.

"It goes out in her hand," she said, "or it does not go at all. I am not carrying it and I am not asking either of you for anything, and I would like it entered that I did not ask, the way it was entered that I did not ask the last time."

The man at that sill went along the stone about a foot and picked his own pen out of that tin and went back to where he had been, and he did not put the second of those minutes down and he did not turn round.

---

**He has been reaching into that pot with his other hand for about four months and cannot say why and has not been asked, and this week he would not put it on a second of a week's minutes, and the reason is not mine to have and I am not going to take it.**

I gave up the only thing I had about four months ago, which was that nobody in that building could ever show me having been anywhere in particular, and I got back a piece of stone about three foot long at the far end of a corridor. I wrote that down at the time in a book of my own hand and did not say it out loud, and I have carried about forty armfuls up and down it in a working day for four years.

And this morning I put a sheet of paper down on a stone in front of a man and asked him, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour, for one thing, which is his other hand on it, and that is the second true sentence I have put into that building in four months and I did not want it to be one and I said it anyway.

And he will not do it and he has not said why, and the older woman came the whole length of that corridor for the first time in four years to say in one sentence that it is my hand and not hers, and she did not thank me and she is not going to be thanked and she is not going to be asked about the shelf at the back of that room.

The second of those minutes goes out in my hand and there is no mark at the foot of it, and about nine hundred sheets a year leave that floor with a mark at the foot of every one of them, and there is not a form anywhere in this city in which a woman can be shown having carried one that had none.

---

That second of that week's minutes went out in the front of that apron with nothing at the foot of it, and every other sheet that leaves that floor in a year carries a mark at the foot of it, and the weather came off the lane onto about three feet of stone that had nothing on it.
