# Chapter 370: A Clerk Of Fifty-Five, A Young Woman At The Far End Of The Room, And A Question With No Full Stop On It

It is the same ground-floor room off the old river road, and the same shelf of eleven books is under the same window, and the window is about two feet across and there is still no pane in it, and the table and the chair are where they have always been. About four thousand entries have gone into those books and not one of them carries a name.

**She is about fifty-five and she keeps the minutes of that room, and in nineteen years she has never advised a person in it and never put a question to one.** There is a woman of about nineteen at the far end of it who has been in the job six weeks.

This is the second day of the second week of the sixth month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and nothing in that room is lit at that hour, and it is about the fifth hour.

The clerk was in the chair with the pen held ready over the page, and nineteen years was how long she had been in it. She looked straight up when she spoke, and did not smile. The younger woman stood at the far end of the room with her hands together in front of her and could not settle herself on her feet, and the whole of the furniture the room had was that one chair and the patch of floor she was standing on.

---

**A minute with an answer in it is not a minute, it is an order, and in nineteen years she has entered none, and no form anywhere lets her.** A minute saying a room stood empty is a minute, and she has entered about nine hundred of those. A minute saying what a man said on his feet is a different animal. It is a direction, and a direction has a person at the end of it, and that person's name goes on the sheet.

**No form for the serving of a question on a person in writing, and there is exactly one person in this matter anybody could serve one on, and she is that person, and nobody has ever paid her for standing ready to be served.**

Nobody has thanked her for nineteen years of entering about nine hundred sheets a year in a hand that has never varied. There is no form in this empire that thanks a person for doing a job correctly, and nobody decided to withhold it, and she has never once treated the withholding as a decision.

Her hand went across the page without hurry, each letter made whole before the next one started. The ink lay black and even between the ruled lines. She came to the end of a word and touched it to see that it was there, and went on, and the pile of finished sheets grew at her left hand until it reached the near edge of the table and she pushed it square with the heel of her other hand without looking at it.

---

"You are not going to tell me what that is," said the woman of about nineteen from the far end of the room.

"No."

She did not look up off the page to answer it, and the pen stayed in her hand and went on down the line she was in. The younger woman bit her lip and glanced across at the shelf under the open window and then away again, as if the thing standing on it had pulled her eyes over against her will. She said nothing more for a good while.

"I have been in this room six weeks and I have come to know that there is a thing on that shelf under the window that is not in the minutes, and it is not a minute and it is not a sheet, and it has been there longer than I have been here."

"You have come to know a great deal in six weeks."

She came to the end of the line and put the full stop at the end of it with one tap, and the sound of that was the only thing in the room for a moment. The younger woman stood where she was with her arms crossed against the cold off the open window and did not press for any more.

"I have come to know that a question with no full stop on it is not a question. It is a note. A note can be thrown out, and a note that has been thrown out is a thing nobody ever wrote. I am not going to be the one who keeps it, because in about two years somebody is going to hand me a paper and ask whether this office has ever held a finding of six years standing, and I am not going to have to stand there and say it was in my hand."

"You are right," said the clerk of about fifty-five, and went on with the fourth number.

She entered the fourth number in its column and then the fifth, and the room was the sound of a pen and nothing else. The young woman watched the figures come up and did not say anything further for a while.

---

"I have not said a word to anybody about that and I am not going to now," the young woman said. "**I want it noticed that I said it out loud in a room and it went nowhere, and that is the second time in six weeks.**"

She said it with her chin up and her hands twisted in her skirt where they had been folded, and both of those were the only things in her that moved. The clerk looked at her steadily over the top of the page and did not put the pen down, and she went on writing.

"There is no form for a clerk kept ready on a shelf," said the clerk of about fifty-five, writing. "Not in nineteen years, and not in the six weeks before them. The difference between the two of us is that I have never once swept it off that shelf, and there is no form anywhere that lets me put a person's name against the fact that I did not."

The pen did not falter through the whole of it. The younger woman stood with her head down and pulled at a loose thread on her sleeve and unwound it again, and let the whole of what had been said to her stand where it had been put.

"You could put the day on it."

"I have got the day on it and the hour. That is all it has got and that is what I have wanted for six years, and a day and an hour and a name is a thing a person can be served with, and I have never once been asked whether I would still have written it and there is no form for asking a person that either, and I have not asked it, and the not asking is not modesty."

---

**Six years of the clerk's own motion sit on that shelf and they are still a question, with no full stop anywhere on it.** No one asked the clerk of fifty-five to write it, and the day and the hour are on it, and it is the only thing on that whole shelf anybody's name is at the bottom of.

A quarter of the clerk's own finding was answered out loud, in a room at the cold end of her own passage, in the second month, by a man who came the whole of that distance on foot and was paid nothing for it. She entered none of that either, and nobody is going to ask her about it a second time, and there is no column in any of these eleven books for a quarter of an answer, because a quarter is not a thing a minute can be about and the half of it that is left is still a question. Nothing in this empire can put a clerk of an office on notice that a person is on the way, or that a person is not.

The other ten of the eleven were full and had been for years, and they were full of minutes, and a minute is a minute whether the room stood empty or not. The one thing on that shelf that was neither a minute nor a sheet was lying under the open side of that window with a day and an hour on it and six years of her own motion under those two, and it was where anybody coming in off the flags could have lifted it and taken it out and down the corridor with it, and nobody ever had, because nobody who had ever come up those flags had a reason to want it and the clerk of fifty-five had never once said out loud that it was there.

Four sheets went into the eleven books inside the hour and the hour after it on that same afternoon, and the count of things anybody has asked out loud in this matter is the same as it was when she sat down. The woman of nineteen at the far end of that room has not thrown it out, and the clerk of fifty-five is not going to say out loud that the woman of nineteen has not thrown it out.

The light went early off the open side of that window and the cold came in with the dark. The clerk lit nothing and went on by the last of it with her hand sure on the page, and the young woman stayed at the far end of the room on her own feet, silent, keeping a place in a room that wanted nothing off her at all.
