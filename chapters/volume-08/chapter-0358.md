# Chapter 358: A Passage Four Hundred Yards Long Off A Flood Terrace At The Back Of A Building Off The Old River Road, Racks Going Down It And Coming Back Light By One, A Lock At One End And A Room With A Rail At The Other, And A Woman Of Twenty-Six Who Counts In Her Head

Off a flood terrace at the back of a building off the old river road there runs a passage four hundred yards long, and the flags of it come up cold underfoot whatever month of the year it is, and no fire is lit at either end of it.

At the end she stands at there is a lock. At the other end there is a room with a rail in it standing about four feet off the floor, and that rail is not on the passage's work and has never been on it.

She is twenty-six and she has been in that building about four years, and she is not one of the three clerks in it, and she is not in this and never has been.

This is the second day of the first week of the third month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fourth hour, and the racks go down all day and come back.

---

A rack went down at the second hour and came back light by one sheet, and one is the usual number, and one is all it has been for the whole of the time she has stood at this end of the passage.

The sheet goes on to a pile at the far end of them with whatever else is on it, and it is on the pile before she has finished standing there. She has watched that happen four years' worth of times and she has never once gone over to see what is in the pile, and there is nobody within four hundred yards of it who could ask her.

**She keeps the count in her head and has never once put a hand on a rack.** It has been four years since she touched one, and nobody ever asked her to, and nobody has ever asked her anything at all.

**Her figure has gone up since the seventh month of the year after, and she has not said by how much and is not going to.**

The watching is done without any paper in front of her, which is the only way there is of watching a number. There is nothing in this empire that is felt rather than written and then put under a heading and entered, and she is the only person in the building carrying one, and she is standing at a lock when she carries it.

She worked that out in about four minutes, standing at a lock, one time. **The figure is the only one this empire keeps on how many people are sitting inside a thing nobody can be asked about.** Everything else anybody counts here is a count of hands, or hours, or yards, or stone, or days, or persons under a heading. Hers is a count of persons under nothing.

---

Put a number in a book and you have made a column, and every column in this empire wants a heading standing over it, and a heading over that particular figure would be a heading over every room in the thing at one time.

**A person cannot be under a heading like that and stay a person**, and she is not going to be the one who puts it there, and it is not that a heading would be wrong. A heading is how this empire does its arithmetic, and it is the right instrument for most of what gets counted, and it is the one thing on earth that would take the last of the air out of four hundred yards of cold flags.

So the figure stays where it is, which is in her, and it is not written down, and it is not going to be handed to anybody this month or in any month after it.

Four hours is what a counter is open for and four hours is what a passage is, and the racks come down and go back in the middle of it. She has stood at this end through the whole of that for four years and has the whole of it by heart, and not one word of it has gone to anybody, and there is nobody here to say it to and that is not the reason.

"There is no form for answering three questions a person has not asked, and I have three of them for a man of about sixty-one who stands on the wrong side of a rail at the far end of these flags, and he has been in that room and I have not been in that room, and I am not going to be."

"There is no form for repeating what was said in a room at the end of these flags, and I am not going to repeat it, and the not repeating is not loyalty to anybody and is not a thing I have been asked to do."

---

The man in the felt apron took the rack down at the second hour and brought it back at the fourth with one sheet on it, and he has done that eleven times a day for longer than she has stood at this end.

"One," he said, coming back along the flags.

"One is usual."

"One is what it has been since I had this end of it," said the man in the felt apron. "I have never once brought a full one up and I have never once gone down with an empty rack, and I could tell you the hour of the day it happened on if I had a book for it, and I have not got a book for it, and there is nobody here for me to tell."

"You are telling me."

"I am telling a lock," said the man in the felt apron, "and a lock is not a person, and that is the only reason this passage is the easiest hundred yards of my day and not the hardest."

He had the rack by the short side of it and he turned it while he said it, which is a thing a man does with his hands when he has said more than he meant to. The apron was grey with the dust that comes off four hundred yards of flags and he had not wiped it and there was nowhere on the flags to wipe it, and neither of them made anything of the fact that he had said a true thing to a lock.

He went back down. She stood at the lock and counted eleven racks in her head for something to do with her hands, and the flags stayed cold, and the room at the far end of them had a rail in it and nothing on the rail.

**Nothing that comes down those flags goes into that room, and nothing that comes out of that room comes up them.**

The man comes down those flags eleven times a day and not one of the eleven is a journey past the door at the far end of them. She has stood at this end long enough to know which of the eleven is which by how long he takes coming back, and she has never once let herself go the whole length of it to find out what is at the far end, and she knows what is at the far end, and knowing and going are two different things she has kept apart for four years.

There is no list that reaches it and no rack that stops at it, and there is no person whose work takes her to that door. A person carrying things for a living has no reason to stop at a door and has nothing to carry back out of it, and that is the whole of the reason a thing said out loud in that room stays in that room.

**It is not because anybody is careful.**

The rail is about four feet off the floor and there is nothing on it this week, and it is not a thing anybody is standing ready for, and she has looked at it from four hundred yards off twice in four years and has not gone up.

---

**She is the only other person who knows what was said in that room.**

She was not in the room when it was said and she has never been in that room. About nine people were in it the last time anything was said in it, and people come out of a room like that and stand four hundred yards apart in the cold and say things to each other, and she is at one end of that and is one of the people they say them to, and she has never once repeated any of it to anybody.

Four hundred yards is far enough that a person can be a long way off and still be one of the people it happened to, and it is not far enough to be out of it. She got to that somewhere in the four years and she has not gone back over it since, and she has never worked out whether she stands at this end because of it or in spite of it.

There is no form anywhere in this empire that would let anybody put to her the question of what she heard. That is the whole of what her knowing is worth, and it is worth nothing, and she would not give it away if there were a form for giving it away.

She has three questions for him, and the fact that he came at all gave her no fourth. It has never once given her a question of any kind, and she is not a party to anything, and no paper any of it has ever been served on her.

The three are in her head in an order and the order has not changed since the first of them came to her, and she has never said any of the three out loud and she is not going to. There is a person at the other end of those flags who has never once put anything to her, and there is nobody at this end who could be asked what she is going to do about the three of them, and she has been getting on with them for four years.

At the sixth hour the last rack of the day came up light by one, and the man in the felt apron did not say it out loud that time, and she did not say it either, and the count of things anybody has asked out loud in this matter is the same as it was when the lock was still open, and neither of the two of them had said one word to the other all day that either of them would have to remember.
