# Chapter 0390: A Rented Room, A Hand That Could Be Named, And A Man Who Does Not Write It

The room is rented by the week and there is a table in it and two chairs and a lamp that is off until it is wanted and a window about a foot wide with nothing in the middle of it. It is the eleventh month and the cold in that room comes off the plaster behind the chair rather than off the glass.

He is thirty-eight. **Marek Kest is the name, and there is no office he holds and no fund behind him and no list and no leave, and in his coat there is a page with a line struck out of it in a hand that is not his mother's.** He has been carrying that page about two years. The name that ought to stand at the end of that line is the only thing he has wanted since before the fire and he has neither it nor the claim that goes with it, and he has looked at that page in about four lights and has never once got a better answer out of it.

There is a mark on the inside of his left wrist about three inches long. He does not put his sleeve over it and he does not do anything else with it, and the room is not the place where either of those is a thing anybody can see.

It is the second day of the second week of the eleventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is the eighth hour, and the lamp was on the table and not in the middle of the room where it usually is.

---

She knocked once and then she opened the door, which meant she had the key to the building and not to that room and had been let up by whoever was on the ground floor that evening.

**A person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and the clerk of about fifty-five has known that since about the seventh month of next year**, and she had said it out loud in her own room three times that month, and she came the whole way to say one more thing and did not say that one in her own room at all.

"You have not put the lamp in the middle," she said.

"No."

"I have never been in this room. I have been up the stairs of that building nineteen years and I have never once been up these." She stood with her hand on the back of the second chair and did not sit in it for the length of about a minute and then did. "That is not why I am here and I am not going to pretend it is, because I have spent nineteen years being a person who does not make reasons."

"I am not going to make this any easier by waiting for you to start," he said, and he did not start it.

"I am going to say it once, and I am going to say it to you and not to the wall, and after that it is said and I am not going to be asked to say it a second time by anybody including you, and **there is no form for a thing said once in a room**."

---

"The hand that struck that name out of that line," she said, "**is a hand you could name.**"

The lamp buzzed. He did not ask her to say it again and he did not ask her anything, and he did not take the page out of his coat and look at it, and there is no form in this empire for a name that has been said once to a lamp in a room, and he was not going to look for one.

**A name written down is a name a person can be served with**, and that is the whole of what she had to say about it afterwards, and she said it in the voice of somebody reading a minute out, and it was the first thing she had said all evening in that voice. "You can be handed a page with a name on it. Nobody can hand anybody a sentence that a person said to a lamp in a rented room, and that is not a thing you have on the other side of it, and I have had nineteen years to work out which of the two is worse and it is the paper."

"You have read that page."

"I have had that line in front of me in a book of minutes when it went through, in more than one of the books on that shelf, and I have written the line above it four thousand times and I have never once written the line below it, and I have had the striking in my eye for about two years and I have not said it out loud in that room because being not asked has never once been a duty I was handed."

---

"You have told me the most useful thing anybody has said to me in about two years," he said, "and I am not going to thank you for it, and you are not going to want me to, and I want both of those said out loud now while there are two of us in the room."

"I am not going to want you to." She put her hands flat on either side of that sheet of paper without turning it. "**The telling was mine and not a thing you asked me for, and you have not asked me one question tonight, and I have noticed that, and I would like it noticed that I noticed it.** And it has cost me more than the fourpence."

"The fourpence was at a counter about four miles off and you had nothing to do with it."

"The fourpence is the number I have been using for about two years for what it costs to be a person who knows where a piece of paper is. I did not invent it and I have not improved on it and I have just spent more than it." She took her hands off the page. "You will want to go and look. I am telling you now, tonight, that you will want to go and look, and I am telling you that I have thought about it more than you have, and I am not going to say that you must not."

---

He did not go and look. He sat in that room until about the tenth hour with the page on the table under his own hands, and he did not write anything, and there is no paper in that room, and there is no pen in that room, and neither of them has said what happens next.

She went back the way she had come. He sat in the rented room with the line struck out in a hand that is not his mother's and a name in his head that had not been there at the seventh hour, and there is a person in this matter now who knows that hand and does not know how long she has known it, and she is the one who came.
