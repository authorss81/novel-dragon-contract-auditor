# Chapter 364: A Bay Of Nine Foot By Eleven, Two Slates On A Bench, And A Man Who Cannot Read The Question

The bay at the top of that lane is nine foot by eleven and the bench down one side of it is longer than that. The lamp over the middle of the bench waits until the seventh hour and it is not lit. Somebody put a mark in chalk on the end wall a long time ago, level with the third link of a chain.

He is about fifty-five, and the chain has been on him for years, and a paragraph is not something he can read, and he has never once pretended otherwise and nobody has ever asked him to pretend it.

This is the fourth day of the fourth week of the fourth month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and the lamp is not lit, and it is about the fifth hour.

The links rested cold where they crossed him, and he had got used to that the way a man gets used to a thing he has decided to keep carrying. He shifted them once with a practised roll, easing the rub of a morning spent on his feet, and kept his hands loose at his sides while he took in the bench and the slate and the wall without going up to any of them.

---

**He has never once sat down with the four of them and never once pretended to read what they cannot read, and he stands up in that bay and says so out loud, to whoever wants to hear it.** He has given the reason four times, in about four sentences each, and the reason is arithmetic. Four is a number that can be leaned on. Five is a number that gets a heading, and a heading is how four men stop being four men and become a thing this empire can put a letter to.

**The one thing he can do reached him once, four years ago, at a shut gate at the end of a lane, and it has not reached him since, and he has not so much as turned it over to look at it.** Nobody at the front of that bay knows that he knows it. He has never told a soul, and that is between him and the wall and not a thing nine men have standing to raise.

He has never once been in a room with a thing he wanted to do and nine men watching, and a thing that worked once in a life, at a gate, with a man at it and nothing else in the world, is not a thing anybody is going to try in front of nine men over a slate.

He crossed his arms and tucked his hands away, out of the way of a thing he had no part in. The bay filled and emptied on a rhythm none of the nine of them kept to, and it smelled of wet cloth and of men who laboured near one another all day and did not mind. Out past the gate a voice called a measurement and was answered from somewhere further off, and stopped, and started again, and the bay did not take it in at all.

---

There were nine men in that bay this week and the two at the back of it were at the back of it, on a written engagement with the rate written out at the bottom of the paper, and not one word was said to either of them all week, and neither of them is going to start.

On the bench in the middle of the bay there were two slates. They are not his and they are not anybody's, and the second one of them was face up with a question written on it in a hand that belongs to an office and not to a person.

He stood over it for a while.

It was a slate. He knew that much by the weight of it and by the way it sat down into the frame, and there was a groove worn in the frame where hands went, and there was writing on the other one that he would not be able to read with the lamp lit either. He breathed through his nose, slow, and did not put his fingers on it, and he knew as much of it as the dark in there allowed him.

"Is that a question?" he said it out loud, to the bench, about the slate, and it was. **There is no form for handing a question to a person, and a question with no name at the foot of it is not a thing anybody can be shown to have got wrong.**

"I cannot read it," he said, out loud, to nobody, about the slate, which is a thing he has said in this bay before and has never once been asked to take back.

---

The man of about twenty-eight, who is at the front of that bench and has been on that bank seven years, did not look up off his hands.

"A boy of nine carries slates about this town," he said, "and I have watched him go past this gate twice this week with one under his arm, and there is nothing written at the bottom of that one, and a slate with nothing at the bottom of it is a thing nobody has ever been asked to look at in this bay in nine years."

The man of about twenty-eight spoke without lifting his head, and he had his own hands turned over in front of him and was going through them again as if checking them for something. The knuckles went white and then came back red, and the bay went quiet around his voice while he did it, and did not come back until he had finished.

"It has got a question on it."

The older man said it with his chin out in front of him, and his fingers curled in against his palms at his sides. He caught himself doing it and let them go, and the man of about twenty-eight sighed through his nose and rubbed his face with one hand, and the slate lay on the bench between them with the whole of its question on it and not one of them any the better for it.

"It has got something on it, and you cannot read it, and neither can I, and there are four men behind you who cannot make a paragraph out of anything, which is a different complaint from mine, and I have never worked out which of us would come off worse." The man of about twenty-eight turned a hand over and looked at it. "**You have got the one thing that would find out what is on it, and it would not find out what is on it.** I have known you seven years and this is the first time I have said that out loud."

"I know that better than you do."

"Then you will not mind me saying that you have never once wanted to know what a thing said as much as you want to know about a number, and that is not a thing anybody in this bay has standing to argue with."

Something moved at the back of the bay. One of the two seated there made a sound in his throat and thought better of it. The older man held his ground where he was standing, and worked his jaw, and let what had been said stand there unanswered, which was the whole of what he had come into the bay to do with it.

---

He said the shape of it out loud. Not the words on the slate, which he has not got, and not the name of anybody, of which there is none, and not any figure, of which there are none.

"There is a question on that slate," he said. "It is in an office hand. **Nobody has put a name under it, and a question with no name under it cannot be shown to have been got wrong.**"

"That is four sentences about a thing you cannot read."

"It is four sentences about a thing that you and I cannot read, and it is not four sentences about a number, and I have said it in a room." He sat down on the bench in the place he sits in. "A thing said out loud in a room is the only instrument in this empire that costs the man who says it nothing and reaches nobody at all. **No form for trying a thing that worked once and no form for handing a question to a person.** Four hundred yards up this lane there is a counter in a room and four people a day go up a stair to be told what a form means, and none of the four of them can read what is on a slate."

Twice that afternoon he put two fingers on the bench beside the slate, the way a hand goes to a bench it has been going to for years, and twice the bay went on being a bay, and the second time he took his fingers off and looked at the chalk on them and did not put them back.

He wiped his hand down his trouser leg, once, the way a man wipes a hand he has not decided about. The mark came away in a pale smear and stayed there, and he left it, and he did not look at what was left on the cloth either.

Where a man who cannot read a paragraph got chalk from, when the only chalk in that place was a line up on a wall, was not a thing nine men had standing to raise. He had the one thing in him that would have settled what was written on a slate and he was not going to spend it on a mark that meant nothing to anybody including him. He sat where he sat until the lamp came on.

Nobody in that bay said one word back. The man of about twenty-eight went on with his hands, and the two of them at the back went on sitting at the back, and the lamp over the middle of the bench did not get lit until the seventh hour, and it got lit at the seventh hour, and the chalk on that end wall still stands exactly level with the third link of what he is carrying, and it means nothing and it is nobody's.

**The slate is on that bench face up, with a question on it and no name under it.** The man who cannot read it has not used the one thing he has and is not going to this week, and nine men heard the shape of a question go out of a room at about the sixth hour, and it went nowhere, which is the whole of what a bay is.
