# Chapter 0371: The End Of A Yard, A Bar Of Iron, And A Cold Morning At A Gate

The bar came up as far as it always comes up, and the man on the far side of it did not lift it the rest of the way.

He is thirty-eight. His name is Marek Kest, and he has **no office and no fund and no list and no leave**, and he has been coming to this end of this yard since before the turn of the year, and on two days a week he has been stood at this bar. This is the first week since he started coming here in which he was told in advance that he would not be stood at that bar at all.

"You are not going to be stood at this bar," said the man at the gate. He was about forty and he had a hut at one side of the iron and a greatcoat hanging on the back of a chair inside it. "I have said it out loud to two men already this morning before you came round, so you are the third one, and I would rather you had it from my mouth than stand there finding out."

"That is all right. I did not expect it either."

"I know you did not, and nobody has ever heard me argue with anybody about anything, and I am not going to start in the cold with a man who has not said one word against me." He put both hands on the iron. "Three shillings is the wall that is not on a district's list. A shilling is the wall that is on one." Both of those came out of him without any weight on them, the way a thing comes out of a man who has been saying it at this bar for years. "I am saying both of those to you and to nobody else, and the man who decided between them is not at this bar and will not be out in weather like this."

**He has wanted a word with that man for about two years and has not had it, and there is no form for a hearing about a rate at a man who is on no list.**

He has a page. It came into his hands about two years ago out of a bundle that was going to be burned in a yard two streets off a street that runs down to the river road, and it is a page and not a bundle, and the line he is after is on it, and the name at the end of that line is not on it in anybody's hand. **The line is struck out.** The striking is not his mother's and he does not know whose it is, and he has looked at it in about four lights and has never once got a better answer out of it.

This is the fourth day of the third week of the sixth month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is the second hour, and the cold is the kind that gets into the gap between a glove and a cuff and stays there.

---

The wall is at the far end of the yard past the bar, and it is not the wall this bar is usually for.

He had stood at this bar two days a week for as long as he had been coming here and he had worked out one thing about the place, which was that the cold in it came off the iron and not off the sky. The bar came up as high as a man's chest and it was the width of a hand, and it had been painted at some point in a colour nobody could now name, and the paint had gone at the two places where hands went and came back every morning. Past it the yard went about the length of a good walk and the wall stood up at the end of that, and the space between the two of them was empty in a way that had a sound of its own, because a yard with nothing being done in it at the second hour is a different place from the one a man gets used to.

"The wall we keep men on is the one on the list," said the man at the gate. "You know that as well as I do. Yesterday they had four of the list men on it and the four of them went off at the second hour because the mortar would not set, and there is nobody to stand at a bar in this for men who are not coming."

"So the bar is down."

"The bar is where it is and I have not put it anywhere. Nobody is stood here this week and that is not a thing I decided and it is not a thing you did." He looked at the man's hands, which were in his pockets, and then at the yard behind him, which was empty of everything including the cold. "**There is no form for standing at a gate, and there is no form for a rate that changes with a list, and both of those are the same hole and the hole is the size of the whole trade.**"

There was a thing about the mortar which Marek Kest had worked out for himself over the weeks he had been coming here, which was that it never set in a cold week, and everybody who stands at the bar knows it, and not one of them has ever stood anywhere it could be looked at from. The man in the hut had the door of it open and had come out of it once and gone back in, and had not come out again, and a man who keeps a bar open on a morning with nothing coming down the lane has nothing to do with the hours until the bar comes down, and Marek Kest had watched him not do it for long enough now to know that it was not laziness.

"Then I will not be one of the two you have told."

"You will be the third and I have said so, and I would rather be the man who says it than the man who lets a person find it out at the second hour with a coat on." He turned back into the hut and then stopped with his hand on the frame. "I have got the fire going in there and I have got nobody to put it in front of."

He got as far as the frame and stopped with his hand on it and stood there. The cold came in over his shoulder into the hut and the man did not shut the door, and Marek Kest watched the back of him and said nothing, because there was nothing standing between the two of them that a word could go on to. Then the man went in first, and the door of the hut stayed where it was, and a bar that is not going to be lifted again in a morning is a thing two people end up standing on opposite sides of all day.

---

He did not go in. He went back out through the gate, which was open because the bar was not across it, and he stood on the other side of the iron with his back to the wall of the yard and looked at the ground between his own boots.

The ground between his own boots was the only part of the yard nobody had walked on all winter. He stood on it and let the cold come at him from the side the wind was on, and the iron was still up behind him across the lane, and a bar left standing where it is put has nothing behind it this morning. He put his hands in again and then took one of them out and looked at the yard the way a man looks at a place he is about to walk out of, and the yard had nothing in it to look at.

There is a mark on the inside of his left wrist about three inches long. It came off him in a room on his own application, on one line which he wrote himself, and **it reads nothing and it decides nothing**, and it does not know where anything is, and he is not going to try it this week, and a correct non-use is entered as a use in every office that would keep a record of him and no office keeps one.

The sleeve of the coat was longer than the mark and he had got into the habit in the cold of pushing it back far enough to look and then pushing it down again without doing anything about what he had looked at. The cold got in on the skin at the wrist and sat there while the rest of him warmed against the wall he was leaning on, and he noticed it going and did not move the arm.

The labour is the worse of the two positions and he has known that for years, and knowing it is not the same as putting it down. He is not thinking about that this morning. He has not thought about it since the last time he was stood at this bar, and what he has thought about since then is a bar of iron and a line in a coat.

He got the page out of the inside of his coat without unfolding it, which he could do because he knew where the third fold was with his thumb, and the paper had gone soft along the fold and had worn through at it until there was a hole in the page that a person could put a finger through and choose not to. He did not unfold it and he did not put a finger through it. He put it back on the side it had been on for months and pushed it down under the lining with two fingers, and the coat held it where it was, and he went on being aware of the weight of a thing weighing nothing at the exact place where a hand goes.

What he is thinking about is a struck line. **Somebody struck out a name on a page**, and the striking is in a hand that is not the hand that wrote the line, and the person who struck it is somewhere inside a building about nine miles off where a man of sixty-one puts his hand at the foot of about nine hundred sheets a year and has never been asked one thing.

**The page has been in his coat since the second hour of the fourth week of the second month, and it is still in his coat at the end of the week, and the man at the bar who keeps no list of anybody has been the only person told.** He was told by a man who could not ask him and did not ask him, which is the way everything in this matter has gone for nineteen years.

He did not thank the man at the bar, because the man at the bar had not done a thing for him and would not have wanted it, and because there is not one form in this empire that requires a person to be thanked for a morning they were not at work.

He stood at the bar a while longer after the man had gone in, on the side of the iron he had been standing on two days a week since he started coming here, and the cold came through the front of his coat at the rate it had been coming through it all morning. Two days had become none, and there was no form for the two of them either. He had a private notion that a morning like this one is not offered back at a bar later in the same week, which he had never tested and was not going to, and he had come round the corner expecting to be stood at a bar for the length of a morning and he was going back the way he had come with the whole of the morning still on him and none of it earned.

He walked back out the front of the yard. There was a man of about fifty at the far end of it loading nothing into nothing, and he looked at the bar and then at the man walking out of the yard, and neither of them said one word, and there is nowhere on either side of that iron for one of them to write down that he had.

Between the second hour and the fourth the bar did not come up once, and that was not a thing anybody had decided. The lane simply had nobody on it, and a man who keeps a gate cannot lift a bar for somebody who is not coming, and there was nobody coming all morning. The light went off the top of the working wall at the far end of the yard by degrees and the man in the hut stayed where he was with his back to the door of it, and the fire he had said he had going was behind him and was doing what a fire does in a hut nobody is sitting in.

The bar came down at the fourth hour from the inside, the way it always does, and the man in the hut did not come out to watch it.
