# Chapter 367: A Bay With Its Gate Open, Nine Men At The Front Of It, And A Foreman Of Fifty-One Nobody Asked

The bay stands at the top of that lane, off the Slade, and it measures about nine foot by eleven, and the gate at the bottom of it is open this afternoon, which it is not on an ordinary week and which nobody in that lane decided.

**She is fifty-one. Her name is Halla Wray, and the foreman's job on that cut was settled for her by other people.** Nine families put it to a vote in a shed behind the second bay in the first week of the seventh month of the year after and were finished inside two hours. She was not in that shed and nobody went and got her.

This is the fourth day of the third week of the fifth month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and the gate is open, and it is about the fifth hour.

She wore a heavy apron over a dress gone grey at the seams from washing. Her hair was wound plain and pinned, a few strands escaping at the nape where the wind caught them. She stood with her feet planted against the slope of the ground, arms crossed, watching the front of the bay with eyes that missed nothing and claimed nothing.

---

**With the gate open that bay is louder and nothing changes.** There are about nine men in the front of it and a man went past the gate twice and stopped at it the second time, and there were four minutes in which nobody at the front of that bay said anything at all, and then the noise started again and was louder than it had been.

The stranger's boots scraped to a halt on the ground outside, loud in the sudden quiet. Every head at the front turned by a fraction, then turned back by agreement, as if some signal had passed among them. The man lingered a moment at the opening, seeing nothing meant for him, and walked on, and the bay let him go.

Nobody has asked her about the box she is in for two years. **Every man at the front of that bay knew it by the middle of that morning and not one has ever raised it.** None of them did it as a favour to her. It is not a courtesy and it is not anybody's decision.

A voice called out a measurement and another answered. Feet shifted on the ground, and the murmur of the front rose and fell. The work went on around her without pause or deference, and she stood inside the noise the way a stone stands inside a stream, letting it part and close again.

"**A foreman who is findable is a foreman who can be served.** Being findable is not the problem. Being served is the problem, and being served needs somebody to serve me with, and there is nobody on that bank who can be given it."

---

The man of about twenty-eight was at the front of that bench and had been on it seven years and he had not been one of the four who cannot read a paragraph, and he put his hands down on his knees.

"That is your sentence," he said. "You said it to me one afternoon and you said it to the man who keeps the books in a doorway, and I have thought about it since and I have not worked out whether it is a fact or a thing you say to keep the shape of a thing."

He sat forward with his forearms on his knees, hands hanging loose between them. Dust paled his sleeves to the elbow. He looked up at her sidelong while he spoke, then back at his own hands, waiting to see how she would take it.

"It is both and you knew that when you said it."

"I did. **There was a man at that gate four minutes ago and I said one word to him, and that is the whole of what I have to say about it.**" He turned his hands over. "So either the gate is shut or about nine men in this bay have to be careful every time a stranger stops at it. There is no form anywhere for shutting that gate, and none for putting a name into the open air where a stranger happens to be standing."

"You have worked out in seven years the thing I have been saying out loud for two, and I am not going to be flattered by it and I am not going to tell you to stop."

She answered dry, with the ghost of amusement crossing her face and gone. Her arms stayed crossed, her stance unmoved. The younger man had the grace to look abashed, ducking his head, and then he grinned despite himself, quick and unwilling, and looked away.

"I am not asking you to stop. I am telling you that a shut gate does the work of about nine men and it does it for nothing, and I would rather we had nine men."

He straightened as he said it, rolling his shoulders against the stiffness of sitting. A gust came down the lane and lifted dust off the ground in a sheet, and both of them narrowed their eyes against it until it passed.

---

The gate was shut again on the second day and the bay was quiet on the second day in the way it is quiet, and the class she is in is a heading, and what a heading does is turn one person into a firm that can be sued, and can be written to, and can be made to answer for things a person would never be asked about. The form was never asking her whether she would stand for anybody at all. It wanted a name put in a box. **There is a column for the answer and no column for a refusal**, and she is not going to say that again.

Four hundred yards up that same lane there is a man who keeps the books of that Company, and what he wants from her is the rest of a sentence she began in the third month of the year after and has never finished. **He has not asked and she has not sent for him, and the second half of that sentence did not arrive this week.**

She had spoken the first half standing in a doorway out of the wind, weeks gone now, and the words had hung unfinished between them ever since. He kept them the way he kept all unfinished things, filed and waiting. She kept them the way she kept everything of his, at arm's length, and neither moved to close the distance.

At the back of that bay the two of seventeen sit on an engagement in writing with a rate set out at the foot of it, and neither of them was spoken to this week, and neither was thanked, and neither was sent for, and neither is going to start. Nobody has ever offered that girl a way of saying no, because not one paper in this empire puts a question to a person of seventeen about whether she would rather not be in the room, and having nothing written down about it is not the same as no.

The mark in chalk on that end wall is at the height of the third link of a chain and means nothing and is nobody's, and the chain is on a man who is not one of the four and says so out loud to nobody about once every few months.

Chalk dust had sifted down the wall face and lay in a pale drift along the foot of it. The mark itself had weathered soft at the edges but held its place. No hand had refreshed it and no hand had rubbed it away, and it stood there through every shift like something the wall remembered.

**It went on being a bay with a gate at the bottom of a lane, and the class was still a class.** The whole of what the open gate cost that week was four minutes in which nobody in that bay said anything at all, and nobody has put a name at the foot of that either.
