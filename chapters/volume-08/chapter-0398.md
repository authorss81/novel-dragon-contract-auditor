# Chapter 0398: Four Lines At The Top Of A Column, The Same Four Lines, And A Woman Still In A Box A Class Put Her In

He has written those four lines out eleven times in two years. He has never once written them out with anybody else in the room, and this week he has done it with a clerk at the other end of two joined tables who said a thing about that column once and has said nothing since.

He is thirty-eight and his name is Rennick Adley and he has kept the books of that Company since the second month of next year. **No office, no fund, no list and no leave.** What he wrote out on the top of that third column of a sheet of fool's-cap are his own copy of half a sentence a woman of fifty-one gave him in a doorway in a month that is gone. Under the four lines there is nothing at all, and the ruled space runs from the bottom of the four lines to the bottom of the page, and that wanting dates from the third month of the year after and has never once turned into a question.

He ruled that sheet into three himself, with his own hand, and printed nothing over any of the three, and there is no form in this empire for a column a man has ruled for himself with no heading over it. **The four that come up that lane bring their figures on the first of the two days and he carries them into the Company's accounts himself. A clerk of about twenty-nine has been at the other end of those two joined tables for the whole of the time he has been here, and there were one person who knew what stood at the top of that third column and there are two.**

It is the second day of the second week of the first month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is the second hour, and the window in that room has a shutter on the hinge that has not been oiled since before he came.

---

He wrote the four lines out again at the top of the third column and they came out the same.

That is the whole of what he did with them. They are a copy and not a quotation, and he does not put them in quotation marks anywhere, and he does not put them in front of anybody, and they came out in the same four lines in the same order in the same length of handwriting, and he stopped where they stop, which is in the middle of a clause, and the middle of that clause has been the end of it for two years.

The clerk at the other end of the tables did not look up. She has a sheet squared in front of her and a pen in her hand and she has not said a second thing about that column since the day she said the first one, and she said once on that day that she was not going to say it again in that room and was not going to carry it out to the yard or the lane or her own kitchen.

---

"You are going to do that every week now," she said.

"I am going to do it every week until it stops being the same, and it has not stopped being the same in two years."

"**There is no form for telling a person what has not moved**, and that is what you have just written four lines of, and I want to be exact about the order of the two halves of that, because the first half is mine and the second half is yours." She put her pen down. "The woman at the Slade end is still in a box that a class put her in, and she has been in it about a year. A box that is entered by a class does not come out of itself, and it has never come out of itself in this building, and it is not going to come out of itself because anybody in this lane has a bad week."

---

"I know she is in it."

"I know that you know and I have never asked you how, and you have never told me, and that has been an arrangement between us for two years and I am not ending it." The clerk did not raise her voice and did not look at the third column. "**There is nobody you could tell, and no form for telling, and no standing for telling, and the not telling is not a thing you decided this week.** It is what a man on no list is like on any ordinary morning. You have no office, so you cannot send a note down a lane. You have no fund, so you cannot pay a man to walk it. You have no list, so you are not on one, so there is no list you could be struck off. That is not a moral position. That is a morning."

---

"There is a man of thirty-eight in this matter who audits contracts for an empire," the clerk said, "and nothing whatever stands behind him, no office, no money, no roll, no leave. He is the only person anybody knows of who could walk down that lane without it being a record of it, because a man nobody serves is a man nobody can hand a paper about."

"I know who you mean."

"He is not in this building. Nobody in this building knows where he is this week and nobody is going to be told, and he is not going to come down that lane on his own account either, because he has not come down it once." She picked her pen up again. "**There is no form for a man on no list.** There are about four hundred yards between the top of this lane and that box, and that is not a distance anybody in this room is going to walk this week, and that is not a decision either. That is an ordinary morning as well."

He did not walk the four hundred yards. He put the sheet into the side of his coat that the other paper is not on, and the four lines went in with it, and the second half of the sentence did not arrive, and nobody thanked anybody.

---

The four that come up that lane were in that Company's accounts before the fourth hour and the shutter on that window was still not oiled, and the third column of that sheet of fool's-cap is still ruled with nothing printed over it, and the two of them at those two ends of those tables know what is at the top of it and are the only two who do.

He has wanted the rest of that sentence for two years and has not asked for it.

**The box is still shut, the four hundred yards is not walked by anybody this week, and there is nobody in this empire who could be asked about either of those.**
