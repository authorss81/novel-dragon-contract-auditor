# Chapter 0373: Four Rooms Let By The Week, Four New Lines In A Day-Book, And A Woman Of Twenty-Two

The four rooms are let. That is the whole of the good news and there is nobody in the house it is good news for except the woman behind the counter, who has been keeping that day-book about nine years in her own hand.

She is about thirty-five. **There is a heading over every column in that book and the headings are room and week and name and what was paid, and there has never been a fifth column, and she has never once ruled one.**

It is the afternoon of the second day of the first week of the seventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and the four of them came in over two hours and each of them paid a week and went up without saying anything that needed answering.

The counter had a cloth on it and a cup on it that was the counter's and nobody's, and both of the women behind it were in the places they had been in since the middle of the week. Outside, the lane was wet from one end to the other and the water came off the eaves at both sides of it and went along the middle. In a house like this one the weather is the first thing a person hears when they come in and the last thing they stop hearing, and the woman who had been behind the counter for nine years could not have told anybody now when she started to notice it.

The stair is the whole of what is behind the counter and it goes up in one run past four doors, and a person who has been up it eleven weeks knows the sound it makes behind her better than she knows the sound of her own name, and knows which of the four doors at the top of it is the one she has to shut hardest in a wind. The light off the lane comes up the well of it as far as the turn and then gives up, and everything said across the counter is said at the bottom of it with the dark of the top of it a foot over their heads.

---

The girl of twenty-two came down off the stair in her sleeves. She has been in the back room eleven weeks and she pays by the week like the other three, and she is the one who has read that book upside down from the counter for eleven weeks without anybody catching her at it.

She had seen it from the top of the last three steps and she came the rest of the way down at the pace of a person who already knows what is on the page and is coming down to hear what is going to be said about it, and she got her elbows onto the counter before her hands were out of her sleeves, and she did not sit down, because a person who sits down at the counter has decided to be there for a while and she had decided the opposite.

"You have got a full one," the girl said.

"I have got a full one."

"All four. **A fifth?**" She put her thumb on the last line in it. "That has happened to me once and I did not understand what it meant when it did." She put her hand flat on the counter and looked at the book rather than at the woman. "**A full day-book is the only thing in this building that could be read out in a minute to a man with a paper in his hand, and you have known that for nine years and I have known it for eleven weeks, and it has never once been a thing between us until now.**"

"It has never once been a thing because there has never been a fifth thing to keep in it."

The four of them had gone up by then and the afternoon had the sound in it of a house with people in it, which is boots on a stair and a door at the top of one and then nothing else, and the woman of thirty-five heard all four of them go up without turning her head, because she had been hearing the house for nine years and could have gone on doing it in the dark. The girl of twenty-two was still standing with her hand where she had put it, and the book was open in front of both of them at the page it was always open at, and neither of them had turned it.

---

That is the whole of it, and it has been the whole of it for two years. A woman came about nine miles on a day she has never put a day to, and stood on the step, and was not let in, and the woman behind the counter heard all of it and has written none of it down and has not been asked about it and is not going to be.

The lane was wet enough by then to be audible and the woman behind the counter had the sound of it in her ears all afternoon and has had the sound of the lane in her ears in nine years of wet afternoons, and she has never once told anybody in the building what came up it or did not, and there has never been anybody in the building to tell. A coming that is not written down is a coming that has to be carried, and she carries it the way she carries the day's figures, which is without putting it anywhere, and she has got so good at that she could not tell anybody now what carrying it feels like.

"**I have said it out loud just now.**" The girl of twenty-two had not moved her hand. "In the house. To the room. And I am not saying it twice and I am not saying it to anybody who walks in, and I have said it to the only person in this building it can be said to, and that was you."

She said it without any lift on her voice and she did not take her hand off the counter while she said it, and the stair behind the two of them was empty and stayed empty for the whole of it, and the words went out into a room with four rooms off it and came back off the cloth on the counter and nothing else. She had had eleven weeks to pick a day and she had picked the one on which four people paid a week and went up, and she had told nobody in the house what she was going to do, and there was nowhere in a house like this to tell anybody what she was going to do.

"You have said it to the four walls and to me, and I am the part of that which will remember it."

"Yes," said the girl of twenty-two. "That is what I did it for."

The two of them stood with the book open between them and nothing written in it, and the woman of thirty-five found that she was not angry, which she had expected to be, and she had time to notice the not being angry and to be annoyed about the noticing instead, and she was still standing at the counter when she noticed it. She had been in the house nine years and the last person who had taken her by surprise was the girl of twenty-two coming down the stair in her sleeves on a first week, and that had not worn off, and she had assumed it was going to.

---

The woman of thirty-five put both hands flat on the counter, which she does when she is going to say something she has not decided to say.

Putting her hands flat on the counter was a thing she did often enough for the girl of twenty-two to have read it, and it was the only thing about her the girl had ever been able to read at all. The girl had worked it out in a first fortnight and had never once used it, and here was the first time in eleven weeks that it was going to be used on her instead of by her, and neither of them had any way of stopping it and neither of them tried.

"You have taken a thing that has been mine alone for two years and you have put it in a room with a person in it, and I want you to hear what that is, because you are twenty-two and you will not have had to think about it. A thing one person is carrying is heavy and it is private. The same thing in two people is a thing two people can be asked separately about, and asked separately is the way it is done."

"I know that. I did it on purpose."

She did not say it loudly and she did not say it as though it were a fact she had come across. She said it as a person says a thing they have done and mean to go on having done, and she stood square at the counter while she said it, and the woman of thirty-five found that she had been spoken to at last by somebody in the building who was not somebody being served, and that the difference between those two things had just cost her what she had been keeping to herself for two years.

"Then you did a thing to me and you did it kindly and you did it without asking me, and there is no form in this empire for what I would have had to put in the book to have stopped you."

"There is not. I looked for one on my first morning and there is not one and I did not find out what to do about it and it has been eleven weeks."

She said the last part looking at the counter and not at the woman, and it was the first time in eleven weeks that she had said anything to her with a length of time at the end of it, and the length was eleven weeks, and both of them let it go past. Nothing in a house like this could take it, and it was not said out of any arrangement. It was said because the alternative was to go on saying she had looked for one, and a person can only say it once.

---

Then the girl of twenty-two went up the stair, and the afternoon carried on the way an afternoon in a house like this carries on. A man of forty came in off the lane with money already out of his hand and went up two steps at a time. A woman of sixty came in off the wet lane wanting a room, was told there was not one, and went back out without a word about it.

The afternoon came on the way an afternoon in a house like this comes on, which is to say that nothing in it was different and all of it had got further away. The four rooms were still let by the week, and the two people behind the counter had put more out loud into one morning than into all the ordinary mornings before it put together, and neither of them had used a word for what it was, and the day-book was shut and had been shut for a good part of the hour.

There is no form for a day-book, and there is no form for an entry for a coming, and the two of them are the same absence and it has been the same absence since the day the book was bought.

A day-book in a house like this is a thing nobody reads and a thing anybody can be handed, and those two facts have been sitting on one shelf for nine years in a building with four rooms in it, and the woman who writes it knows both of them and has never put them together in a sentence, because putting them together would make a form, and a form is what a thing acquires when somebody other than her is going to be able to make a use of it.

At the fifth hour the woman of thirty-five took the day-book down off the shelf and turned it to the page she always turns it to. The four headings and the ruled spaces under them had four new lines in them that day and not a fifth. She put her thumb on the last line in it and left it there for the length of a breath, and then she shut it and set it down on the shelf with its spine flush to the front edge, which is the way it has gone back on that shelf every day for nine years.

She did not rule a fifth line. She did not write the coming. There are now two people in this house who know why there is no fifth line in it, and one of them is at the top of the stair.
