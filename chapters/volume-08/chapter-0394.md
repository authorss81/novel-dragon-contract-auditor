# Chapter 0394: The Counting Room At The Back Of A Dye End, Nineteen Days In A Coat Four Miles Off, And A Rule Nobody Picked Up

The room is at the back of a dye end, four hundred and thirty miles up this river, and it has a stove behind the bench. The light in its window stopped coming years before either of the two women who work in that room was born. Eleven houses off that lane keep their figures in that room, and the figures arrive on the first of the two days and leave again on the second, and the rhythm is older than either of the two women in it. What is on that bench is borrowed out of other rooms, every one of it except a single volume, and that one has her name on the head of it in capitals, and nobody has ever set a question against it anywhere.

She is twenty. **Her name is Nell Kest, and what she is owed came to nineteen days of a crew's money, and the pay of that bench gave out in the second week of the third month of the year after, and every one of those days is folded in a coat that is four miles off this building in a room she has not stood in since she was seventeen.** The coat went four miles on the second of the two days and came back four miles on the fourth, and the nineteen days were in the pocket the whole of that time and were not spent, and the notice that came to that bench with a printed ruled space on the back of it went out in the same pocket, and the space on it is as clean as the day end put it.

**There is no form for a rule said once**, and a rule said once in the open in a lane in the cold is a rule that has left the room it was thought of in and has nowhere to be filed, and there is a difference between a rule that is written down and a rule that has been said, and the second kind is the kind that works.

The second of the four women had not come into that room for four days after the second of the two days, and this week she came in on the first.

---

She came in about ten minutes after Nell Kest and put her shawl over the back of the second chair and did not sit down in it, and the stove had not been lit and Nell Kest had not lit it.

"You did not ask me where the coat went."

"I did not ask you where the coat went and you have been away four days and you have not sent anybody to say." Nell Kest squared the pad on the bench. "The first of the two days is the first of the two days and there is no form for a person who has been away four days coming back in on the first instead of the second, and I am not going to make one up at this bench."

"I am not asking you for one. I am telling you the other half of a thing I said to you in a lane." The second woman had her hands on the back of the chair. "**Nobody picked it up.** That is what has happened to it. I said a thing to you out of the open on a cold morning and you have not used it, you have not given it to a third woman, you have not put it in that room, and it is four days and there has not been one person in this building who has heard it, and the not being picked up is the most expensive thing anybody has done to me this winter."

---

"I did not use it because you did not ask me to."

"That is the correct answer and it is the one that has cost me the most." She took her hands off the chair. "**A rule a person says out loud once in the open is a rule that lives in the person who heard it and not in the person who said it.** That is not a thing about rules. It is what happens to a room when somebody carries a thing out of it, and I have now done it twice, and the first time it cost the fourth house off this lane, and the second time it has cost me the only thing I had, which was the not being asked to explain it."

She sat down in the chair at last and drew the shawl up over her knees.

"You are going to be asked to carry it," Nell Kest said. "That is what you have just told me. I am not going to pretend that you have not."

"Yes." The second woman looked at the stove and did not go to it. "And you are not going to hand it to the third one, and you are not going to hand it to me, and there is nowhere in this building for a rule to be put down, and so it is on you, and you are twenty years old and you are sitting in this room with it and that is what I have done to you in a lane on a cold morning for nothing."

---

Nell Kest lit the stove.

The stove took about half an hour to come and goes out again by the seventh hour, and the figures of the first of the eleven houses came in at the tenth hour and were gone through by the second hour, and the two women did the work in the ordinary way and one of them said the sum out loud twice and the other one wrote it down and neither of them said one word about a lane for the rest of the day.

It is the second day of the second week of the twelfth month of the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is the first hour, and the coat is over the back of the second chair in that room four miles off with the nineteen days in it and the notice with the clean space on the back of it, and the woman of twenty is not going up this river this season, and a season is not a life.

The rule was in the room and it was in neither of them, and the two of them had the whole of a working morning to be in a room with it, and no short while is spent in a room with a thing belonging to nobody in it at all. Nell Kest was twenty and she had been at the bench since she was a girl, and the whole of what she had done this morning was light a stove the other woman had not lit, and nobody was going to thank her for the stove, and the stove was not the thing.

**The second of the four women came in on the first of the two days and she will come in on the second of the two days as she comes, and she is not thanked and she is not forgiven and she has not asked for anything, and the rule is in the room now and is in neither of the two of them.**

The one on the bench with her name on the head of it had been on the bench since before the second woman came in to work there at all, and nothing else in the building carried her name across the head of it, and the entire purpose of a name standing across the head of a volume is to have a question set against it by somebody, and nobody had ever set one, and she had come to the conclusion some time ago that a name standing at the head of a volume is the same as a name standing at the head of a volume with nothing under it at all, and that the whole of the difference is in the hands of whoever comes to ask, and that nobody was coming.

Nobody has set a question against the one on that bench with her name on the head of it, and it has not been opened, and the fifteen that are borrowed go back out to the rooms they came from on the second of the two days.
