# Chapter 253: Four Hundred Blanks Under A Counter, And The First Question Put To A Clerk Of Thirty-Four That Was Not A Request

The fourth counter of the Notaries' Table in Auremar is at the top of a stair, and the stair is the only way up, and there are about nine people a day who come up it, and the counter is open from the second hour to the sixth and the fee board is on the wall behind it with four things written on it and none of the four of them is time.

She is thirty-four and she has been a clerk here eleven years and she is not a lawyer and does not want to be one, and about nine people a day come up that stair and find out that a clerk is a person who can be asked things.

It is the fourth day of the third week of the twelfth month of the year after next. There are about four people in that room: a clerk of thirty-four at the counter, a woman of twenty-four at the other end of it, a man of about thirty who does the copies, and a woman of about twenty who has been there a year and who is not in this matter at all.

---

About four hundred bought and unfilled blanks are in a box under that counter, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says she may keep them, and she keeps them, and nobody has ever asked her to account for them.

She said out loud about four times a year, to the box, that a blank is the only piece of paper in this empire that cannot be held against anybody, and she said it once to a colleague and it is the only true thing she has said to another person in eleven years that was not about a price.

**A blank is not a document, because a document is a thing somebody can be held to, and a blank has nothing on it, and a person can walk into a room holding one and say plainly that they never filled it in.** That is the whole of what is in that box. It is worth about four hundred blanks' worth of nothing and it is fourpence a time to the people who buy them and it has never once been explained to her by anybody.

---

The four things on the board behind her are a copy at fourpence, a question of hers at her own table at a shilling, a certificate at four shillings, and a line of writing she has never worked out what it is for.

She has never issued a certificate. There is no form in this empire that requires her to issue one and there has not been one since she came, and the four shillings on that board have been on it for the eleven years she has been standing under it.

The shilling is hers and not the Table's. A question of hers, at her own table, on her own time, is a shilling, and she has done one of her own in about a year, and the price of it is that the answer is hers and cannot be got out of her afterwards by anybody, and she set that herself, and there is no form anywhere that says she may.

The fourth line she has asked two people about. Both of them said they did not know, and one of those two is the woman of twenty-four at the other end of the counter who has been there two years, and the other is the man of about thirty who does the copies and who said it in a way she has thought about since.

"It is on a board," he had said. "It has been on a board longer than I have been in this room and I have never once been asked to explain it, and I have taken a reading that if a thing is never asked about it is not anybody's business, and I have had four years to find out what that is worth and it is nothing."

---

The ordinary work went on in front of all of it, and about nine people came up that stair in that day and about four of them wanted something out of her and got it and said so, and one of them did not.

A man of about sixty came for a copy and paid fourpence and she read it back to him twice and he read it back once and said thank you, and she told him that was the ordinary way and not a favour, and she has told about nine hundred people that and it has never once made one of them come back.

A woman of about twenty-five came and asked whether a boy of about nine could have a copy of a thing that was not his, and Marn Ottery said yes for fourpence, and the woman said thank you, and Marn Ottery said again that it was the ordinary way.

The woman of twenty-four at the other end of the counter said nothing at all for the whole of the hour before the fifth hour, and she squared a stack of copies twice that did not need squaring.

**He came up the stair at about the fifth hour and he had been in the room before, twice, and had not bought anything either time.**

He was about forty-five and he was not a carter and not a clerk and he was the kind of man who looks at a board for a long time before he looks at a person. He came up, looked at the board, and looked at her, and then he asked her a question, and it was not about a copy and it was not about a price.

"I want you to say the shape of what I am about to ask before I ask it," he said, "and then I am going to ask it, and if you have got a way of doing that I would rather you used it than not."

---

She said the shape of it, because she has said the shape of a question out loud about nine hundred times in eleven years and it is the only reason a clerk of eleven years is still standing at that counter.

"**It is not about a copy and it is not about a price, and you are not asking me what a form says.** You are asking me about a line of writing on a wall behind me, and you have not asked anybody else in this room because the other three of us have told you we do not know and you have not believed us."

"I have not believed you because it has been on that wall for longer than my trade has."

"Then ask it."

"What is the fourth line on that board for?"

"I want the shape of the answer as well, and I am not going to get it, and I have made my peace with that in about four minutes."

---

She did not answer it for about a minute and a half, and she did not help him, and then she said the thing she had said out loud about four times a year to a box, and she said it to a person, and it is the first question anybody has ever put to her that was not a request for something out of her.

"I do not know," she said. "**I have asked two people and both of them have told me the truth and neither of them knows, and you are the third person in about a year and I have told you the truth, and you can stand there and be owed an apology by nobody.**"

---

Then she told him the other thing, and she told it because he had asked the shape of it out loud and had waited, and a person who waits is a person you can say a thing to.

"Look at the four lines. Three of them are a price for a thing you can walk out of this room holding, and you can read the price off the wall without asking a person, and that is what a line of writing is for." She did not turn round to look at it. "The fourth one has not got a price against it and it is not a thing anybody has ever walked out of this room holding. The reason the three above it work is that there is a person at the bottom of them and the person is what the price is for. Take the person away and it is a line of writing."

"That is a wall."

"It is a heading." She put both hands flat on the counter, which is a thing she does and has done about nine hundred times. "A heading is a set of people written down as though they were one line, and about nine hundred of them a year come in under one in about nine hundred buildings, and a class is not a person and is not an office and is not anybody, and the people are standing under it. And a person in this empire is a thing you can send papers to. A company is a thing you can hold wages against and give letters to and take to law, and a man who is a person is a thing it can only send papers to, and the whole difference between those two is a heading."

---

"And how long have you known that."

"Nine minutes, on and off, for about eleven years, and I have said it out loud about four times a year to a box, and I said it once to a colleague and she thought I had come in with a complaint about the rent."

He put his hand on the counter and looked at the board again for a while.

"You are the first person in this city that has said that to me," he said, "and I have asked about nine men and about nine women and every single one of them has told me what their own line was for."

"Is that supposed to be a compliment?"

"It is not a compliment and I am not taking it as one."

"I know it is not. I am saying that about nine hundred people in this city can tell you the price of a thing and not one of them can tell you what the word above it is for. I have been doing it for about four years and I have just been told that I am part of a heading, and I would like you to understand that I am not going to do anything about it."

---

That was the whole of it, and it took about nine minutes, and she had said the shape of it out loud before she answered it and he had asked her to and he had waited.

Nobody thanked her. He did not thank her and she did not expect to be thanked and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be thanked for the truth at a counter, and the other three people in that room did not thank her either, and the woman of twenty-four at the other end of the counter had her head down and had heard all of it.

"You will want to know what I am going to do," he said.

"I do not want to know that and you should not tell me."

"I am going to go down that stair and I am not going to do anything, and I want you to hear me say that, because in about four years somebody is going to come up this stair and say that a clerk told him about a heading, and I would like it on this counter, spoken, that there is nothing to come back for."

"There is nothing to come back for," said Marn Ottery, "and there never was, and that is not a fault in the stair."

---

He went down without buying anything, which he had not done either of the two times before, and the door at the bottom of a stair does not shut, because stairs do not have doors that shut.

She stood at the counter for about four minutes afterwards and did not do anything, and the man of about thirty who does the copies said, without looking up, that it was the first time in eleven years he had seen her not know what to do next. She said it was the first time in eleven years she had been asked a question that was not a request, and he said that was a different thing, and she said it was.

The woman of twenty-four said one thing then, without looking up, and she said it to the copies and not to Marn Ottery.

"You told me once that a person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and I have known that since about the seventh month of next year, and it has never once applied to me, and I did not know that until about four minutes after a man of forty-five asked you a question and you gave him an answer in front of me." She squared the copies again. "There is not one form in this empire for a person to be asked, and I have now been in a room with two people who have been asked something about the same wall and neither of them asked me."


Then she knelt down and put the box back under the counter where it goes, and there are about four hundred blanks in it and it has been there eleven years and there is no form for it and there is no register and there is no heading over it and no clerk's name at the foot of it and nobody has ever been appointed to anything about it.

**And it is not a post, and it is not going to become one, and the reason is the only reason worth giving and she gave it out loud to a man on a stair for the first time in eleven years: it would stop on the day the first person with a form wanted it to.**

The fourth line was still on the wall behind her when the counter shut at the sixth hour, and it will be on it on the second day of the first week of the first month of the year after the year after next.

There are three people in this empire who cannot account for it. There is a clerk of thirty-four at this counter, and a woman of twenty-four at the other end of it, and a dye-house worker about four hundred and thirty miles up this river who found that out nine days ago, standing at a door, from a woman of thirty. **There is not one instrument anywhere in this empire that would put any two of those three in a room together, and there is no form for asking a person to look themselves up, and there is no form for asking a person who works beside you.**

A Table is the only place in this empire where a person can come up, ask a question, and walk away with a piece of paper that says they asked it. That is not an office and it does not behave like one and about four hundred times a year somebody does it and nobody decided anything and nobody can be told to stop.

And she had just told a man of about forty-five standing on a stair that there were about four hundred of them under the counter, and he was not going to report it, and there was nothing whatever she could do to stop him, and that is what not having a heading over a thing buys a person. She has worked that out and she has not worked out whether she would rather have had it the other way.
