# Chapter 252: A Column With A Heading Over It, And A Woman Of Thirty Who Asks What The Column Is Called

The room is rented by the week and it is off a dyers' lane in a furnace town about four hundred and thirty miles up this river, and it is about eleven feet by fourteen. There is a bench along one wall, a window with a broken light in it, and about forty books on shelves that were made for taller books, so that a woman of twenty has to reach up for the top of every one of them.

She is a dye-house worker, which is what she is paid for, and she keeps the books of eleven houses on that lane and of four houses in three other districts, and she has kept every one of them from this city since she was seventeen and has not been up the river since she came down it, and that is what she is not paid for.

It is the second day of the second week of the twelfth month of the year after next. Her wage stopped in the second week of the third month of the year after and it is stopped now, and the word for it is held. The money for the nineteen days she has worked since the year turned is in a weigh-house ledger in this town with her wage in it and not her name under it. She has never once written down what she is owed, and she has counted what nine people in that lane are owed about nine hundred times.

---

She had told one of four women and she had decided to go and tell the other three, and this was the second woman of the four and the first of the three she had not told. It was about four hundred yards down the lane, and she did not add that up either, and she has not added up the going in three years.

There is a reason for that and it is not a good one. A woman of thirty-five tried to add a sum out loud once in a room in this city and came out wrong by about a week's rent, and was then asked in about nine minutes by a man at a counter to say where the number came from, and could not, and went home about four hundred yards on foot and told nobody. **There is not one form anywhere in this empire for a woman to be asked to show her working**, and a woman who shows her working shows a person the inside of her head as well, and a person who has seen the inside of your head can say anything about it afterwards and there is nothing that will make him prove it.

And the deeper reason is duller than that one. She does not do the arithmetic because the arithmetic is not hers, and she found that out at seventeen and has not found it out again since.

---

The woman of thirty was called out from a back room with her sleeves up and did not sit down, and there was a child asleep on a bed that was also a table, and the room was warm and it smelled of wet stone.

"Tell me again the second part," she said. "Not the part about the water. The part about the sixteen."

"You asked me that about three weeks ago."

"I asked you that about three weeks ago and I have thought about it every working day since and I have not worked out what it is for yet. You keep numbers for nine houses in this lane and you are not paid for any of them, and not one of the nine has ever asked you what you are doing it for. I want to know which of the two of us is going to be angry when it comes out."

---

Nell Kest told her. It took about nine minutes and it went the way she had worked out it would go and not any other way, and the woman did not cry and did not offer money and did not ask her to prove it.

Then the woman went and got the book, which took about four minutes, because she had asked for it once before and had been refused politely, and she had a page marked with a knotted string, and she put it on the bench and put her own hand flat on the line that was hers.

"Tell me what that is called," she said.

"That is the lane," said Nell Kest, "and that is the word over the top of it, and the word over the top of it is what your street is called, and then your house is under your street and your name is under your street, and that is the whole arrangement."

"Then you are telling me that I am not in there. I am in there under a street."

---

**And here is the thing she has known for three years and had never once said out loud to a person who was going to be able to use it.** A column with a heading over it is the same book with the machine left in. A column with no heading over it is the same book with the machine taken out, and the two of them look the same on a bench and the difference is whether a person can be found under one of them by somebody who has never met her.

"I am telling you that you are under a street," she said, "and I have been the one who put you there, and I have been doing it since I was seventeen, and I have never asked you and I am not going to start."

"Take them out."

"No."

"You have just told me they are the machine."

"I have." She put both hands on the bench. "And I am not going to take them out, and I want it said in this room that I understood the whole of what you just asked for and said no. It is not that I did not understand it."

---

The woman of thirty stood there for about a minute and a half and then she asked the question that made it worse.

"How much is it, then. Of mine. I have asked you three times and you have said you would tell me and you have not."

"I will tell you what I do not do first." Nell Kest put her hand flat on the closed book. "I do not say sums out loud in a room. I have never said one out loud in three years and I am not going to start with you, and it is not secrecy and it is not for me."

"Why not?"

"Because a sum said out loud in a room is in about nine mouths by the second hour, and about nine months later there will be a person at a counter who has heard the number and not the name and will write it in a column under a heading, and you will not be able to say where it came from. **There is not one form anywhere in this empire that says a number heard in a room may not be written down in a book.** I have watched it happen to a man about four years ago and I did not know at the time that what I was looking at was the machine."

"Then I cannot have the number."

"You can have the number out of that book, on that bench, in that room, and not anywhere else, and that is the whole of what I have to give anybody and I have given it to about nine houses on this lane for about three years."

"Then how do I find my own?"

"By the knotted string, and by the number, and by the hand, and by the fact that I have never in three years got a page wrong for anybody in this lane." Nell Kest turned the book round. "If the headings come out, there is a street at the top of a page and nine houses under it and no name anywhere, and you would be in the ninth house from the top until you died, and I would be the only person in this city who could tell which one, and you would never once in the rest of your life be able to look your own up without me in the room."

"So I need you."

"You need a woman of twenty who is not paid about four hundred yards down a lane and whose wage has been held since the second week of the third month of the year after. That is not the same as being owed a fair thing and I am not going to pretend to you that it is."

---

And then the woman said the other half of it herself, in her own mouth, and she said it as though she had been carrying it about three weeks and had not known what it was.

"They are all under streets."

"They are."

"So if somebody came into this room with a paper and a seal, they would not have to ask about you at all. They would ask about the lane, and the lane is a thing, and a thing can be asked a question and a woman cannot."

"That is the whole of it and I have not improved on it in three years," said Nell Kest.

"And you have never once got anybody out of anything by saying it."

"Never once. I said it to an empty room about four times a week for about three years and it has got nobody out of anything, and it has never been wrong either, and I would say it again in the same words in front of anybody."

---

She said it a second time, out loud, to a person, and she had said it to forty books and to four people and to a foreman off a dye end in this city who has a piece of paper in the inside of his coat, and she had not said it twice to the same living soul.

That is the difference and it is not a small one, and she worked it out about four minutes after she had said it, standing in a warm room with a child asleep on a table, and what she worked out was this. **A thing said to a room goes into a room, and a thing said twice to a person goes into that person, and that person can be asked about it afterwards by anybody who knows she heard it.** There is no form in this empire that will let a woman be asked whether she would rather she had not.

"Well," the woman of thirty said. "Now I have it as well."

"You have had it for about three weeks."

"I have had a feeling about it for about three weeks. I have not had it in a sentence with my own name in it, and that is the difference and I did not know it was a difference until you said it in front of me."

---

**And the third of the four is on a lane about a mile from this one and the fourth is in a district, and Nell Kest has not walked to either of them and is not going to this month.** There is a foreman off a dye end in this city who has said one true thing about himself out loud five times, and the fifth of them was to a boy of about nine with a slate, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that will take a slate off a boy, and a slate is not a document and cannot be produced and there is no clerk's name at the foot of it.

She has asked him once, on a stair, about the third of the four, and he said that it was not his and that he was not going to make it his. She did not ask him twice, because a person who has said no once to a question has said it to themselves and not to her, and she is not going to be the reason a man of about forty in a coat finds a reason to say yes.

Nobody thanked her. The woman of thirty did not thank her, and she did not expect to be thanked, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be thanked for sixteen books and a refusal, and there is not going to be one.

The woman asked her one ordinary question before she went, and it was about which day a sum was wanted by, and it took about four minutes, and neither of them mentioned the sixteen books again.

---

Then, at the door, the woman said the thing about the lane and the counter, and she said it the way people say a thing they have been carrying and have decided to set down because they have found somebody holding something heavy.

"About eight weeks back I was in a city about four hundred and thirty miles down this river, and I had a sum that would not come out right, and I went up a stair at a counter and I asked a clerk what a line of writing on a board behind her was for." She put her sleeve up. "And she said she did not know, and that she had asked two other people and neither of them knew either, and then she told me the half of it that is the worst half. **She said that a person is a thing this empire can only send papers to, and a company is a thing it can hold wages against and give letters to and take to law, and that the whole difference between the two is a heading.**"

---

Nell Kest stood in the doorway of a room she had rented by the week for about three years and did not go out into the lane for about a minute.

"Three people," she said. "You, and a clerk of thirty-four at that counter, and a woman of twenty-four who works beside her and who has been there two years. Three, and that is the whole of the people in this empire who cannot account for that line, and one of the three is a dye-house worker in a rented room in a furnace town and the other two are at a counter four hundred and thirty miles down this river, and there is not one instrument anywhere that joins this lane to that stair."

"Then go and ask them."

"There is no form for asking a person who works beside you either," said Nell Kest, "and there is no form for asking a person to look themselves up, and I have looked at every one of these columns for three years and not one has a line in it for a person who is not sure."

---

**And a woman of twenty went back up about four hundred yards to a room of about eleven feet by fourteen and put a knotted string on a page ruled by hand in a book of her own.** One of the forty books on shelves made for taller books has her own name at the head of it. She has never been paid for any of the sixteen, and her wage is still held, and the two streets and the two counters and the four hundred and thirty miles are still four hundred and thirty miles apart.

She had decided to tell four women that she is not paid. One is told. One is told and has said no to her and said why, in her own words, in a warm room with a knotted string in a book. Two are not told and will be. Nobody was thanked. Nobody is going to be thanked, and there is not one form in this empire that requires it, and the road is four hundred and thirty miles long and it is not being walked in either direction by anybody in this matter, and the second of four is on the board for the fourth day of the second week of the first month of the year after the year after next.
