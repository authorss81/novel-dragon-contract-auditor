# Chapter 265: A Man Of About Forty-Five Up A Stair For The Third Time, Four Hundred Bought And Unfilled Blanks Under A Counter, And The First Time In This Matter That Anybody Has Written Down That A Clerk Said No

The fourth counter of the Notaries' Table in Auremar stands at the top of a stair, and there is no other way up to it. It is open from the second hour to the sixth. Behind the counter there is a fee board with four things written on it, and not one of the four is time, and about nine people a day come up that stair.

Marn Ottery is thirty-four, has been at that counter eleven years, is not a lawyer, and has never wanted to be one. Nine people a day go back down that stair knowing something they did not know when they came up, which is the ordinary product of a counter and the only thing this room sells.

This is the second day of the third week of the third month of the year after the year after next, and the second hour, and there are about four people in that room. Marn Ottery is at the counter. A woman of twenty-four is at the other end of it, and a man of about thirty does the copies, and a woman of about twenty has been there a year and is not in this matter at all.

---

Under that counter there is a box of about four hundred bought and unfilled blanks. No form anywhere in this empire lets her keep them. She keeps them. Nobody has ever asked her to account for them.

Nobody has ever explained the box to her either, and a person who cannot account for a thing and cannot be asked about it is in the ordinary position, and the price of a blank is fourpence to whoever buys one, which is the whole of what anybody knows about them.

**A blank carries no document, because a document is something a person can be held to and a blank is nothing written on paper at all. A man can walk into a room holding one and say in plain words that he never filled it in, and then there is nothing left to hold him with.**

---

He came up the stair for the third time and he had been in the room twice before and had not bought anything either time, and he came up it at about the fourth hour and he did not look at the board first, which was a change, and she noticed the change.

About forty-five. Not a carter, not a clerk, and the sort of man who will stand and study a board a long while before he looks at a person. He had stood at that counter once before, on the fourth day of the third week of the twelfth month of the year after next, and asked her what the fourth line on that board was for. She had said the shape of the question out loud before she answered it, and she had told him the truth, and he had said out loud that he was not going to do anything about it.

He had been in the room twice before and bought nothing either time, and on both of those two times he had looked at the fee board first and then at her, and on this third time he had not looked at the board at all, and she has watched people walk into that room for eleven years and she knows what it means when a man comes up a stair not looking at the wall.

He came up the stair to tell her he had done something about it.

---

"I want you to say the shape of what I am about to ask before I ask it," he said, "because the last time you did it and I have not stopped thinking about it."

"It is about a piece of paper, and it is not about a price, and it is about the box under this counter." She had not moved. "**And you are going to ask me to enter them somewhere, and you have thought about it for about eleven weeks, and you are not a man who does that for nothing.**"

"Go on."

"Ask it."

"I want the blanks entered," he said. "Every one that goes out of that box, a number, and the day, and the name of the man who bought it, in a book, in your hand, kept under the counter with the rest."

---

And she said the shape of the answer out loud before she gave it, which is a thing she has done about nine hundred times in eleven years and is the only reason a clerk of eleven years is still standing at that counter.

"It is no," she said. "And I am going to tell you why in about four minutes and you are going to think it is a hard time about four days and then you are going to think it is the truest thing anybody has said to you in a year, and both of those are fine and neither of them is a favour."

"Then say it."

"You are trying to save them."

---

He did not answer that and she did not need him to, and she went on, and this is the whole of what a blank is, said out loud in a room with four people in it, and it is the second time in eleven years she has said it to a person.

"A blank is the only piece of paper in this empire that cannot be held against anybody. Not a sheet, not a notice, not a copy, not a line in a book, not a mark at the foot of a page. A blank. Nothing has been written on it, so there is nothing on it that anybody did, and a man can stand in a room and hold one up and say that he never filled it in, and everybody in the room has to go on believing him, because there is nothing to say back."

"That is a good thing to want to keep."

"It is the only good thing about that box and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that says I may keep it, and I have kept it for eleven years, and you are the second person I have said that to out loud."

---

"And you are wrong," he said, "or you are not, and it does not matter, because I have worked out what I actually want and it is the other side of what you have just said."

"Then say it."

"I want to be able to prove it."

"Prove what?"

"That a blank is a blank." He put his hand flat on the counter. "I have a man who is owed money by nine people and he has been owed it for a year, and the nine have all got paper, and every piece of paper they have got can be held against him, and he cannot show anybody anything, because there is nothing to show. If he had a number on it and a day on it and a line saying who bought it, then the paper that is held against him would be a piece of paper, and not a blank, and he could stand up in a room and say, that one, that day, and then it is a document and I can be held to and so can they."

---

She stood at that counter for about four minutes and it is the longest four minutes of the eleven years and she did not look at the board and she did not square anything.

"**That is the best reason anybody has given me in eleven years and it is also the reason I am not going to do it**, and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to pretend the second half is smaller than the first."

"Go on."

"The day that box gets a number and a day and a name against every one of them, it stops being a blank." She put both hands on the counter. "Not that day. That is what a blank is, and a blank is the only paper in this empire that cannot be held against anybody, and the second it has a number on it and a buyer's name under it, it is a document, and it can be held, and it can be produced, and it can be served. And then a man who bought one of these eleven years ago and never filled it in cannot say that he never filled it in, because there is his name at the top of it and a day on it, and the whole of the thing I have just told you it is for is gone, and gone for good, and gone for everybody at once."

---

"And you are doing it for a reason," he said, after a while. "You have had a reason ready."

"I have said out loud about four times a year to a box that a box of about four hundred of these is the nearest thing to a post anybody has built in this empire, and that it is not a post, and that it is not going to become one."

"That is not a reason."

"It is the reason and you have not got to the end of it. The only thing that would turn that box into a post is a person asking for it. That is what a post is, a thing a person asked for, and the asking is the whole of it. **And a person who asks for it gets told no by a person, and the reason it is a no is that it would stop on the day the first person with a form wanted it to.**"

---

Nobody thanked anybody. He did not thank her and she did not expect to be thanked, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be thanked for the truth at a counter, and he said thank you at the top of the stair on his way down and she said that was the ordinary way and not a favour.

He went down without buying anything, which he had not done any of the three times, and the door at the bottom of a stair does not shut, because stairs do not have doors that shut.

The box went back under the counter where it goes. It has about four hundred blanks in it and it has been there eleven years, and it has no register and no heading and no appointment and no form and no list of holders and no clerk's name at the foot of it. Nobody has ever been appointed to anything about it. That is what came out of about four minutes at the top of a stair, and it is the second time in eleven years she has said the whole of it to a person, and the first time somebody had gone away and built something on it.

---

And the woman of twenty-four at the other end of that counter asked her, at about the fifth hour, whether she wanted it written down.

"No."

"I am going to write it down."

"You are not."

"I am going to write it down in my own book and nobody will ever see it, and I am not asking you to be in it and I am not asking you to say a word about it." Tamsin Rook had a pen in her hand and she was not looking at her. "You are the first person in about four years I have heard say no to anybody, and I have been in this room two years and I have not written that down either, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that says a clerk has to put down that another clerk said no, and I have just made one up, at about the fifth hour, at the end of a counter, and I know exactly what I have done."

---

**And Marn Ottery did not stop her, and she wants that said in a room**, and the reason she did not stop her is the reason she gave out loud and it took her about four seconds to find.

"Do you know what you have just done to yourself?"

"I have put a person in my book."

"You have put a person in your book, and about four days ago the whole of what you had was that there was nothing about anybody in it." She did not put her hands flat on anything. "**And I cannot take it out, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for taking a line out of a book of your own**, and you have done it out loud in a room and I did not stop you and I am not going to stop you now."

"I know."

"You do not know. You have a wall, and it is a good one, and it is the only thing in your life nobody has been able to take off you, and I have never once in eleven years been able to get at it, and you have just put a person in a book in a rented room and there is a clerk in this building whose name is at the top of it. That is not a wall any more. That is a window."

---

**And it is the first time in this matter that a refusal has been written down by a person who was not the person refusing.** Neither of them is going to thank the other for it, and the woman of about twenty who has been there a year and is not in this matter read nothing at all of it.

When the counter shut at the sixth hour the fourth line was still on the wall behind her, and it will be on it next week. Three people in this empire cannot account for it. One of them is a dye-house worker about four hundred and thirty miles up this river, and there is not one instrument anywhere in this empire that would put any two of those three in a room together.

And a woman of twenty-four in a rented room two streets off a street that goes down to the river road copied the four lines off that board into a book of her own at about the seventh hour, because nobody had ever written them down.

The first three of them are all in the hand that put them up. The fourth one is not. She does not know whose hand it is and she does not know when it went up, and she wrote that down too, and that is two things in that book that were not there in the twelfth month of the year after next.
