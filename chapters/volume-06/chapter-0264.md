# Chapter 264: The Third Of Four Women, A Book With Her Own Name At The Head Of It, And The One Page In This City Where Nobody Was Put There By A Street

She rents this room by the week. It is off a dyers' lane in a furnace town about four hundred and thirty miles up this river, and it is about eleven feet by fourteen, and it holds a bench along one wall, a window with a broken light in it, and about forty books on shelves that were made for taller books, so that a woman of twenty has to reach up for the top of every one of them.

She is paid for the dye house. The books are the other thing. She keeps the accounts of eleven houses on that lane and of four houses in three other districts, and she has kept every one of them from this city since she was seventeen, and she has not been up the river since she came down it, and nobody pays her a penny for any of it.

It is the fourth day of the second week of the third month of the year after the year after next. Her wage stopped in the second week of the third month of the year after and it is stopped now, and the word for it is held.

---

She told the second of four women in the twelfth month of the year after next and she has not walked to the third, and she walked about four hundred yards this afternoon and she did not add that up, and she has not added up the going in three years.

**There are eleven houses on that lane and Nell Kest keeps the books of all eleven, and the book of a house has the street at the top of the page and the houses under the street in the order they were built.**

That is not her decision and it is not anybody's decision in this city that she knows of. It is the way a lane is written down. She has looked at the order about four hundred times and it has not moved in three years, and the two end houses have not moved in thirty, and the woman at the top of the lane is the ninth house from the top or she is nothing at all.

There are four houses in three other districts that she has never been inside and has never been asked inside, and their pages have a district at the top of them, and a district is a bigger hat and works exactly the same way.

The third of the four lives at the top of the same lane. She has been in this room twice before, about a year ago and about a year before that, both times for an ordinary question about a day a sum was wanted by, and both times she had stood in the room while Nell Kest worked.

She did not sit down.

"I have come about the money and I know you are not going to say a figure in here and I have stopped minding that," she said. "**I have come about the outside of one of them.**"

---

She had thought about it for about a year before she did it, and the thing she had worked out in the year was that the whole of her job is invisible and that invisibility is not the same as being safe.

Eleven houses, four houses in three districts, a lane at the top of each page, and a woman of twenty somewhere underneath all of it who is not on any of them by name. She is in about nine hundred places in this city as a thing that is not written. Nobody can be found that way and nobody can be held to anything that way, and that is the whole of what she has, and it is the same shape as the thing the woman of thirty-five has just asked her the name of.

She decided in about four minutes that she would rather be written down once. She did not decide that it was safer. She has never once claimed it was safer and she is not going to start.

It is the second shelf down on the left and it is about the same size as the other thirty-nine and it is bound in nothing in particular and there is nothing on the spine, and it is the first one anybody coming into that room would reach for, and about nine houses in this lane have had it open on that bench without knowing what it was.

**Nell Kest has her own name at the head of it.**

She put it there herself, three years ago, in her own hand, in about four minutes, having thought about it for about a year, and she has never told anybody, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that says a woman who is not paid may keep a book with her own name at the head of it.

"Show me the page," said the woman.

Nell Kest turned the book round and did not open it and did not need to, and did not say the word on the top line, because the woman could read.

---

"You are in it."

"I am in it once."

"You are in all of them. That is what I have been trying to tell you for a year. You are in the middle of that one and at the bottom of that one, and when I asked you for the number out of mine you said ask me and I asked you, and both times it came out of a book that had you somewhere in it."

"That is what a lane is," said Nell Kest. "Put a heading over a column and it is the same book with the machine left in. Take the heading away and it is the same book with the machine taken out. They look identical sitting open on a bench, and the entire difference is whether somebody who has never met you can find you under one of them."

"And you are under a street in eleven of them."

"I am under a street in eleven of them and I put myself at the top of the twelfth one on purpose, and I have never said that out loud in a room before today, and I have said it to a lamp about four times a week for three years."

---

And then the woman of about thirty-five got it, and she got it in about four seconds, and it is the same sentence that came out of a warm room in the twelfth month of the year after next with a knotted string in a book, and Nell Kest heard it a second time in a different mouth.

"So you are the only person in this city who put herself there."

"I am the only person in this city who is on a page because she wrote herself on it. Everybody else in these books is on a page because a street put them there, and a street does not know it has done it, and it does not know it can be asked to take them out."

"Can you be asked?"

"Yes."

"Then what is it for?"

---

And that is the question Nell Kest has been asking herself for three years and has never once said out loud to another person, and the answer took her about four minutes and she gave it to a woman standing in a room about eleven feet by fourteen with a broken light in the window.

"It is not for anything," she said. "**A column with no heading over it is a person, and I have got one, and it is the only one of these forty that is a person, and I made it, and it is not a wall and it is not a door and it is not going to get me out of anything.**"

"You said that to a woman with a string in a book, in the twelfth month of the year after the year after next."

"I said it to that woman with a string in a book in the twelfth month of the year after the year after next and she said it back to me about four minutes later and that is the second time in my life it has been in two mouths, and I have not told anybody which mouth."

---

The broken light in that window has been broken since before she came into the room and she has never had it mended, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that requires a woman who rents a room by the week to have a window mended, and the landlord is a man in another city who has never seen the room.

She has worked out that the light is the reason the books on those shelves are hard to reach, and that it is the reason she has to stretch up for the top of every one of them, and that after three years her arm knows which of the forty is which without her looking.

That is not a skill. It has no form attached to it and it cannot be entered anywhere and it is the only thing in three years of unpaid work that has come out of the room of its own accord.

The woman of thirty-five did not ask for the number. She had said before she came that she was not going to and she did not.

"I want to ask you one thing and it is not about the money," she said. "**If I wanted to look you up, how would I do it.**"

"By the name and the street and the number, and there is only one of me in this city and I have been here since I was seventeen, and that is the whole arrangement and it has not once been hard."

"Could anybody else look me up the same way."

"Yes."

"Then what is yours for."

"It is for me being in it," said Nell Kest. "**Not for me being hard to find.**"

---

**And the four that come every week are always the same four, and the woman of about thirty-five asked for all four of them before she left, which she has never done, and the fourth one was whether the book with the name on it is a book of the lane.**

It is not. It is a book of hers and it has never been a book of the lane and it is not going to be, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says a woman who is not paid may keep one, and it is the only one of the forty that is anybody's.

"So it is yours and not ours."

"It is mine and it is about you," said Nell Kest, "and I have never been able to work out which of those two is the more dangerous and I have had three years."

The ordinary questions went on afterwards for about nine minutes. A day a sum was wanted by, and whether a thing wanted in the district wanted in the lane, and whether the third house down had paid. Those three are hers and about four of them every week and she has been asked them about nine hundred times.

She did not say a sum out loud in that room. She has never said one out loud in that room in three years and she is not going to start, and the reason has not changed, and it is that a sum said out loud in a room is in about nine mouths by the second hour and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that says a number heard in a room may not be written down in a book.

**Nobody thanked her.** The woman of about thirty-five did not thank her, and she did not expect to be thanked, and there is not one form anywhere in this empire that requires a person to be thanked for three years of sixteen books and a refusal and a sentence out loud in a warm room.

The woman went up the lane. Nell Kest did not add it up and did not count the days and did not work out how long it is since the second of the four said a thing about a street in a room where a child was asleep on a table, and it is twelve weeks, and she knows that without doing it, which is the only arithmetic she has ever been any good at.

---

And her wage is still held and it has been held since the second week of the third month of the year after, and the word for it is held, and the money for the days she has worked since the year turned is in a weigh-house ledger in this town with her wage in it and not her name under it.

Three of four women now. The third said no to the sum in a room and asked instead how she could look a person up, and that is a worse thing to have been asked and a better one to have answered.

The fourth is in a district. Nell Kest has not walked to her and is not going to this month, and there is a foreman off a dye end in this city with a piece of paper in the inside of his coat who has said no once on a stair about a thing that is not his, and she is not going to ask him twice, because a person who has said no once to a question has said it to themselves and not to her.

**A person who is not asked has not been given a duty.** She has known that since about the seventh month of next year and she has not said it out loud to anybody in this room and she is not going to, and about four hundred and thirty miles of road is what it would cost to say it to somebody who was not already in one of these forty books.
