# Chapter 146: The Column Nobody Could Give Them

The clerk's window of the Mosswake Roll Court is on a flood terrace and it has a board above it with chalk on it, and the chalk is gone in about four places because it is about eleven months old and the terrace gets weather.

Above the sill there is a book, and in the book there is a register of pending duties, and in the register of pending duties there are five headings and only the first four have ever had anything under them.

**Ivet Sarn is the clerk of that court and she has been for nine years and she is the fifth instance of a thing two different courts have now said four times, and she entered that herself, in her own hand, on her own initiative, without being asked, in the eighth month of last year.**

The window is not in the building. The press is in a corridor behind the building, about forty steps back, and the corridor is cold in the eighth month in a way that the terrace is not, because it is below the water line, and about a hundred and forty files are in the press, in a fourth press, and about nine hundred and forty names are in them.

---

The form came up the road on the twelfth of the eighth in a hand and not in an envelope, at four pence, carried by a woman of about twenty who had walked six hours and would not take anything for it, and it was two questions on the back of a form and the first was:

> *Is there a column this court could add to the register of pending duties in the matter of the sixth year, that would be of use to the households on it.*

And the second question was on the other side of the sheet, and it was the one that mattered, and it was in the same hand and it was in the same words, and it was not a different question at all:

> *And if there is not, what is the reason, in a form, with a day on it.*

---

She read both sides standing up at the window with a courier waiting, and then she went and got the register, and she was gone for about an hour and forty minutes, and she came back and said, "Ask the first one again, slowly."

The girl of about twenty read it again, slowly.

"Right."

And Ivet Sarn wrote the answer in about eleven minutes and then wrote it again in a form, and the form is one of the ordinary forms of that court and it has a box for a reason and no box of any kind for a why, which is a fact about about nine hundred forms in this empire and about forty of them on this terrace.

---

Here is the whole of what she put in the box, and it is longer than the box.

> *Answered: no.*
>
> *Refused, and the reason is on a page in my own hand in this court's own minute book with the day on it, and the box is two lines deep and this is longer than two lines and I do not care.*
>
> ***There is no column this court could add that would be of use to them, and the reason is that a column with a date in it and a person against the date is a hearing, and a hearing is the only thing in this empire that makes anybody answerable to anything, and this court cannot make one.***
>
> *A person who has not been heard has not been given a duty. A duty given to a person who has not been heard is the thing this court has spent nineteen years declining to do, in about nine hundred rooms, with about four people in them on an ordinary afternoon. A hearing requires a person to be summoned. **This court cannot pay the carriage of a person to be summoned, cannot compel a person to attend, and has no power to make anybody come; and there is a standing offer from a seat, made in the sixth month of last year and still open, to pay the carriage of any person this court thinks should be heard — and that offer has been inoperable for about sixteen months, because it pays carriage and not attendance, and because there is no form in this empire for compelling a person to be brought to a room.***
>
> *And I am asked, in the second question, for the reason, and the reason is above, and I am going to say one more sentence and it is not part of the reason and it is my own. **If this court had a seventh column and put a date and a person in it, then about nine hundred and forty people would be summoned about four at a time to rooms about four hundred miles from where they live, and this court would be doing to them the exact thing the district of this court did to them in the sixth year, with a better motive and a worse timetable.***

---

The second part is not the answer. The second part is a clerk's own sentence and she said so to the girl of about twenty before she wrote it.

"I am putting my name at the foot of the box and the day. **And I am putting that sentence in because I have been asked for a reason and a reason that makes a person look prudent is not a reason, it is a decoration, and there are about a hundred and forty files in a press four steps behind this room with my hand in them and I have not read them as a set in nineteen years and I am not going to start in the eighth month.**

"**And the answer to the first question is no and the second question says *and if there is not, what is the reason*, so a clerk is entitled to give one, and I am giving the true one and it is not comfortable and it does me no good.**"

The girl of about twenty read the whole of it back twice, and the second reading was slower, and then she said, "**Are you refusing, or declining to answer?**"

"**Refusing.** And those are two different things and the form has one box and it says *answer* in it, and what I have written in the box is a refusal and not a declination, and the reason for the difference is that a declination would leave it open for somebody to ask the same question in about four years in a different room and get a different answer, and a refusal is a refusal and a person can be shown it."

---

Then the girl went and looked at the board above the sill, because nobody had told her not to and it is nine feet away.

It is a board in chalk and it has thirty-one numbers on it in letters about four inches high, and about four of the chalk marks have gone, and there is a second figure above it, and above that a column head in a clerk's own hand, and the column says:

***next review: none named***

Thirty-one times.

The girl of about twenty read it out. She read it out twice, and the second reading was slower, and she read the thirty-one as well.

"What is the second figure."

"**The number of houses out of about sixty that a court wrote to in the sixth year and that came back with a copy of what the court had said, with a door on it.** Thirty-one, and of those, twenty-two had been seen by somebody and nine had been told, and a court in this district entered on the record that seeing a person and telling a person are not a hearing and are not the same as each other, and I did that entry and I would do it again."

---

Then Ivet Sarn did the arithmetic, out loud, because that is what happens in a room where she is in it, and she wrote the figures in a book with no office on it and read them back to the girl of about twenty twice.

"Fifty-four filed copies for three cycles. **Thirty-one with a name and a door, of which twenty-two seen and nine told. Eleven with a word and no name. Twelve came back with the post stamp on them and nothing else.** That is thirty-one and eleven and twelve, and that is fifty-four.

"And the fourth cycle of the sixth year was never made, and there were six copies for it, and the clerk who was going to make them had a child and eleven weeks of docket and no replacement, and that was in the second week of the second month and the flue for the press was cut on the fourteenth of the same month. So fifty-four and six is sixty.

"And there are **eighteen returns that did not come back at all**, which is a different number, and the two are not added together and I am not going to let anybody add them together, because a return that did not come back and a copy that was never made are two different facts and the day somebody adds them is the day this board becomes a number instead of thirty-one people."

"And the fifth heading," said the girl.

"**The fifth heading is *Copy sold* and it has been *Copy sold* since the sixth year and it is the only honest thing anybody ever put in that register, because a copy that has been sold is a copy somebody wanted and paid four pence for and took away, and there are about two hundred and forty of them in this district and there is no list of the buyers and there never was.**"

---

Then she went into the corridor.

There is about a hundred and forty files in the fourth press and they are in her hand and she has never read them as a set. In the fourth month of this year a man of thirty-eight came down the steps of this corridor and put his hand flat on the spine of one of them — a file of the sixth year — **and did not open it and did not pull it out and did not ask what was in it.**

The clerk who was at the window wrote down that a person came and did not open it, and entered the reason on the face of the file, and the reason is the only thing about that file in nine years that is in a clerk's own words about a person rather than about a household, and it says:

> *A person came and put his hand on this and did not open it. **It is not that this file is a secret. It is that nobody has ever asked me, and a person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and I am not going to be asked by a man with a hand on a file at about the fourth hour of the afternoon.***

---

The file is still there. It is on a shelf in the fourth press and it is shut and it is between two others and it has a number on the spine and the number is a number of a book.

Ivet Sarn stood in front of it for about nine seconds and then walked out of the corridor without touching it, and said so to the girl of about twenty at the top of the steps, and the girl wrote it down on the back of the form with the day on it, and that is now the only record in this matter of the not-opening being witnessed by anybody but the person who did it.

"I am not going to open it," she said.

"You have read the reason on the face of it."

"**I have read the reason on the face of it, and the reason is a sentence I wrote in the fourth month, and a sentence you have written about yourself is not a thing that gets better by being true.**"

---

Then she did the thing the whole of the corridor was for, and it took about eleven minutes, and she did it at the window with the board behind her and the wind off the terrace.

"**What this court can give them instead.**"

She took the register of pending duties to the window and opened it on the sill and put a finger on the fifth heading, which is *Copy sold*, and then went below it, into the ruled space where there is no heading at all, because the form has five and there is a sixth ruled space at the bottom of every page of that register that has never had anything in it and has never had a heading over it, and about two hundred and forty people in this district have never known it is there.

And she wrote one line in it, in her own hand, with a day on it.

> *Houses on this register: 31. Days this court has held the matter open without a hearing and without a date: — (no return is kept and this is entered as a fact and not as a figure). Person to be summoned: none named.*

And she signed it, and she read it out to the girl of about twenty twice, the second reading slower, and then she read the heading that is not there out loud, which she had to do, because there was nothing to read.

"**There is no heading over that and there is not going to be one,**" she said. "**And I want it entered that a foreman of a district works four miles from this building arrived at this in the ninth month of last year by accident, kept it for nine months, and threw away a paper with a heading on it twice, and I did not know he had, and nobody told me, and that is the whole of what is wrong with the last two years of my life.**"

---

And then she said the other thing, and it is the reason the corridor is in this chapter, and it is the part that has to be said in plain numbers because it is the largest unpaid debt in the book and it is not going to be paid in a chapter where somebody is refusing a column.

"The fund's quarter came round on the first of the ninth."

"**Six pounds and eightpence committed for the quarter,**" said Ivet Sarn, "**and about six pounds on the average of the four quarters, and the bill is nineteen pounds three and fourpence, and it is unpaid, and there is no line for a bridge in that fund and there has not been one in nineteen years.** A fund that had a line for a bridge in it would have to answer for the bridge, and so the line is not there, and that is not an oversight, and there is a minute about it in the sixth year in a different book.

"And the real cost of the crossing is about a penny a day. **Four hundred and eleven people live on the far side. The district's return for four weeks says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings.** Those are two figures and they are never the same and the reason they are never the same is that a count of a bar omits everybody who crosses without the bar moving, and there is a plank at the mill end and a padlock broken for longer than anybody living and a yard and men fishing the shallow and a boy of nine.

"**Nobody is liable. It is open. And it is not paid at the end of this month, and it is not paid at the end of any month I have heard of.**"

Then she said, "**And I am a clerk of the court that wrote the letters in the sixth year, and I do not know which hands wrote them, and I entered that I do not know, and nobody has ever asked me about it, and I am the person in this building who could answer it and I cannot.**"

---

The form went back down the road on the fourteenth of the ninth in a hand and not in an envelope, at four pence, and the answer is in a book with no office on it with the day on it, and the girl of about twenty carried it and would not take anything and was not paid and was told by a clerk of about thirty that that was a rule of the lane and not of the court.

**The seventh column is refused and the reason is on a page in a clerk's own hand with a day on it and a name at the foot of the box, and thirty-one names are still in chalk on a board on a flood terrace, and the fourth column still reads *next review: none named* thirty-one times, and about a hundred and forty files are in a press four steps behind a window, and one of them is shut between two others, and there is a sixth ruled space at the bottom of every page of that register with one line in it and no heading over it, and it is worth nothing at law and it is the only thing anybody has been able to give them since the sixth year, and both of those are true.**
