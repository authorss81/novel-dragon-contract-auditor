# Chapter 136: The Names in the Press

The Mosswake Roll Court is a low building on a flood terrace with a public bench outside it and a clerk's window and a door that opens onto the embankment, and the embankment is four feet above the paving, and the mark of the water from the nineteenth of the fourth month is a line about four inches up the stone of the terrace that has been there for about ten weeks and will be there at the next one.

**Leda Rell is fifty-one and has been a magistrate in this district for nine years.** Her files are in a press in the corridor behind the window, in her own hand, and there are about a hundred and forty of them, and she has never read them as a set and has never once wanted to.

She entered, on the ninth of the second month, on her own motion and asked by nobody, that a body of persons may not be made a party by her court or any other. She entered, on the same day, that a balance has stood in about a hundred and forty files for nineteen years and has never been added up and is owed by nobody, and that the figure stays out of the minute on purpose, **because a clerk who thinks somebody owes something is the beginning of everything that has gone wrong in that district since the sixth year.**

She said out loud in the second month of this year, in a room, that she does not know whether she was right. Nothing has happened in between that has told her anything, and she has said since that she is not going to manufacture a feeling about it in front of people from a lane.

On the thirteenth of the sixth month a seat's notice was handed to her at about the tenth hour by her own clerk, standing up, and its fifth line offers the carriage of any person whom **the district court** thinks should be heard, and that is the first time in nineteen years anybody has asked this court that question.

---

She read all six lines twice, standing, and then she said, "I have been asked in writing. Write that down, in my own hand, and put the day on it, because in about four years somebody will read that this court had never been asked and the person who says so will not be me."

Ivet Sarn wrote it down.

"**A seat's notice asks me who I think should be heard about a water in my district.** That is a proper question and it is a question this court is competent to answer and has never once been asked. I am not going to treat it as an insult and I am not going to treat it as an honour. It is a piece of administration and I am an administrator and I have nine years of a reputation for being the second one."

"Will you answer it?"

"I will answer it in a form, with a reason, and there will be no why, because there is no space for a why and I am not inventing a box for this court any more than a keeper invented one on a water office form in the fifth month." She sat down on the bench outside, which she does not do when people are waiting. "**And to answer it I have to know who is in this district in respect of a flood, and I have got two ways of knowing and one of them is four hundred names in a press behind me and the other is the woman at my window who has been writing them down for nine years.**"

"I would rather the second," said Ivet Sarn, "and so would you, and neither of us is going to get it."

---

She went into the corridor alone. The press is in a cold room and the flue was cut on the fourteenth of the second month and the room is cold all year because the flue is not lit for anything but paper.

About a hundred and forty files. Nineteen years of her own hand, in bundles of ten, tied with tape, and on the spine of each one a year and a matter number, and in the inside front of each one the same four lines in the same order, which she wrote herself in the first year because nobody showed her a form:

> *By whose authority this is enforced. The instrument. The person. The person heard.*

The fourth line is the one that has been in about a hundred and forty files for nineteen years, and in most of them it is the same eleven words, and in about nine of them it is different.

She read the eleven words in a bundle from the sixth year, and then the nine different ones, and then she sat down on an upturned crate in a cold room with about ninety files out on the floor around her.

**She had not opened the corridor to answer a seat's notice. She had opened it because a man from the district works had said nine words to a woman of sixty-four in a bay four miles away and she had heard them from a window.**

That is a different reason and she wrote it on the top of the first sheet she used, and it is the most honest thing in this chapter and the least useful.

---

Here is what is in the files, and it is nine years and about nineteen years and it is a body of her own practice with a date on it.

**In nineteen years she has enforced continuation clauses against about nine hundred and forty people in this district.**

Not in one flood. In nineteen years, in about a hundred and forty matters, she has taken an instrument, found a person, and held that person to a duty the person had inherited, and about nine hundred and forty people have been made answerable in her own hand.

**And in the same nineteen years she has not enforced them against about four hundred.**

Four hundred and some. She is not going to put a figure on it and she is not going to add it up, and the reason is in the second paragraph below and the reason is not cowardice.

The three categories are this.

**The first: people who cannot be found.** There are about two hundred and ten names in her files of persons who were served and could not be found afterwards, and the service is on the file and the file says the person was served, and no court in this empire has ever gone back and said *this person was not served, they were not there.* So they stand served for ever. They were never heard. They are on about nine hundred and forty people's record of what is owed and nobody will ever ask them.

**The second: people who are of a household and the household is of the household.** About a hundred and forty. A person is of a household and the duty falls on the household, and the household is not a person, and she has entered *household* in the third line of about a hundred and forty files in nineteen years, and a household cannot be put in a room, and so about a hundred and forty people have been made answerable by a word she chose because there was a column for it.

**The third, and this is the one she has never written down and is writing down now: people she chose not to.**

She did not leave them out through negligence. **She left them out on purpose, twice, and both times in the same fortnight of a flood season, and both times she can name the year and cannot name the file.**

In the first of them she had about a hundred and thirty people in front of her who could have been held, and a river coming, and she held about forty of them. She chose which forty. She chose them because a predictable claim in the right place stops an eviction in the wrong place, and she wrote that reasoning down in a minute in her own hand in that year and it reads like sense.

In the second of them she had about two hundred and forty people in front of her, and she held about nine, and she chose those nine because they were the nine who had water standing in their ground and everybody else had somewhere to go.

**She chose. Both times, by hand, on a day, in this district, and the choosing is in her own hand, and the reasoning in the first one is the reasoning that every flood authority in every district in this empire uses and has always used and that is right.**

That is the finding and it is in about four words.

**Nine hundred and forty people made answerable in nineteen years, by choice, and about four hundred not made answerable, by choice, and the second four hundred are the ones that make the first nine hundred and forty indefensible as a rule, because a rule is a thing that is applied the same way each time, and this was not the same way each time, and she chose, and the reason she chose is good, and a good reason applied unevenly by one person in a district for nineteen years is not a rule and cannot be defended as one.**

She wrote it out on a sheet of her own. It is about a hundred and sixty words. She read it back once and did not read it twice.

Then she wrote under it, and the under is the part that matters and is the reason this chapter exists:

> **The three categories above are not three wrongs. Two hundred and ten could not be found and about a hundred and forty are households and both of those are forms failing and I could have written a heading and did not.**
>
> **The third is not a form failing. The third is me. I chose which forty in one season and which nine in another and I can name the years and not the files, and I am the only person alive who can say what I chose on, and the reason I am not putting the names in is that if I put the names in then a person in about four hundred and thirty miles who is entitled to be heard about it will be able to find me, and I have a warrant on a different matter, in a different city, that says a person from an office may read anything I write, and I am not going to hand them a list of about four hundred people I decided not to hold.**
>
> **So it goes in as this. A finding of fact about my own practice, made by me, on my own motion, in my own court, naming no person, against myself, and not appealable by anybody, and not a finding about anybody else, and not a discovery, and not a seam, and I am aware that the word I want to use is *arbitrary* and I am not going to use it, because I am not arbitrary and I was consistent to a rule, and the rule was mine and I made it and I applied it and it is the rule that is not defensible and not me, and I do not believe that and I am writing it down anyway because it is the version of it that is true in about four years.**

---

She came out of the corridor at about the fourth hour and answered the notice at the bench outside it, in a form, with a reason, and there was no why on the form and she did not invent one.

> *Answer: the persons this court thinks should be heard about the instrument are these, and they are five. Two of them are named on a form filed at this window on the last day of the third month and two of them are described in that form and cannot be un-named. The third is described there as a person of about seventy-one at the fourth house at the north of the ford who cannot read a paragraph. The fourth and fifth are the foreman of the Slade Cut, whose name is at the foot of a list of works held in a hired store, and the person who holds the book of that Company.*
>
> *Reason: those five are the persons this court can identify as being touched. This court cannot identify any other and says so. It has about a hundred and forty files in its own press in which about nine hundred and forty persons have been made answerable in nineteen years, and it is not able to name them now and does not offer to, and anybody who wishes to be heard may come to this window and ask, and this court will hear them, and this court has said so before in writing and will say so again.*

"**That last sentence costs me something and I want it in.**"

"It costs about four hundred and thirty miles of carriage," said Ivet Sarn, "and that is the only cost on the sheet and it is the smallest one and you are going to be judged on the other one."

"Copy it out twice," said Leda Rell. "**Once for the seat and once for the file, and the file copy goes in the top drawer, not in the corridor, and the corridor is for files I made in other years.**"

And then she entered, on her own motion, in the minute of the day, four things, and the clerk read them back and they are as follows.

**One.** That she was asked in writing and that she answered in writing and that this is the first answer this court has ever given to a seat and the first time in nineteen years that anybody has asked it.

**Two.** That the seat's offer of the carriage of any person should be heard is live, and remains inoperable, and that this court has now said who it thinks should be heard, and that the offer has still not operated, and the reason is that the offer pays carriage and not attendance and there is no form in this empire for compelling a person to be brought to a room, and that this court is not going to write to the seat about it again.

**Three.** That a finding of fact about this court's own practice in the enforcement of continuation clauses is entered above and names no person, and that the finding is made on the motion of the magistrate and not asked for by anybody, and that it is not a finding about any other court and is not a discovery and is not a seam, and that the undertaking this court entered on the ninth of the second month was used in this matter **for the first time with the magistrate in the building and having been asked**, and that the first three uses of it were without her and she was told about them and was not asked.

**Four.** Nothing is ordered against anybody. Nobody is liable. No fund is found. Five persons are named or described and none of them is served, because this court cannot compel a person to attend, and any of the five may come to the window and ask, and two of the five have already come once.

She read the four entries back and then she did the thing she has done five times in nine years.

"I am not going to say anything about whether I was right," she said. "**I said in the second month of this year that I do not know, and nothing has happened in between that has told me anything, and I am not going to manufacture a feeling about it in front of five people from a lane.** I have read a hundred and forty files in a cold room this afternoon and I have found out one thing about myself that I did not know at the seventh hour this morning, and I am entering it, and that is all I am going to do with it today."

She did not open the file of the sixth year. Nobody mentioned it, and it was not in her hand, and a person had put a hand on it in the fourth month and had walked out again and had written nothing down about why.

At about the fourth hour of the next day, a sheet went four hundred miles up a river in a hand and not in an envelope, at four pence, and it went to a person who was told by a courier that a seat had offered the carriage of anybody this court thought should be heard, and who read the second entry of the minute with the word *inoperable* in it and laughed out loud, once, in a room with two people in it, and would not say why, and it was not a funny thing.
