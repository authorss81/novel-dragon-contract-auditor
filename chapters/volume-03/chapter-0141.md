# Chapter 141: A Duty That Ends

The lane at the north of the ford is about a mile and a half from the mill and it goes up, and the four houses are not all at the top of it, which is a thing about lanes that nobody who has not walked one believes.

The first house is eleven hundred yards up. The second is a gate with a cottage behind it. The third is back down about two hundred yards and round. **The fourth is the furthest, and it is a mile and a half and about forty feet higher, and it is the house of a woman of about seventy-one who walked four miles of towpath in about two hours each way in the fourth month of this year on the advice of nobody, and who cannot read a paragraph, and who has asked in writing to be asked again.**

**Rennick Adley is thirty-eight. He has kept the Slade Cut Company's book for six years. He cannot read a chapter of law and reads a paragraph perfectly, and about nine people in ninety-one households cannot read a word, and he has made it his business in four months to know which nine.**

He has a slate and no form. That is the whole of what he took.

He wrote down on the thirtieth of the sixth month, in his own hand, at the bottom of a page, that he was not going up that lane a second time on his own account, and he read it back to about nine people in a bay that evening and made the second reading for the people who could not read it, and it is at the bottom of a page in a book with no heading at the top of it and it is four sentences long and the last of them is the reason it is there.

The first of the four is about the twentieth day of the fourth month, when a woman of about thirty-one came down a towpath in the mud and was told there was nothing. The second is about the twenty-third. The third says that nobody has sent for her and that nobody is going to.

**The fourth says that he is not going to go up that lane a second time on his own account, and that he has written it down so that in about four years there is one sentence in a book that says a person was not asked twice, in a thing where everybody else in this matter has been asked two or three times and did not know it.**

He has not read that last sentence out of the book since the evening he wrote it. He has read it out in his own head about nine times.

He read that sentence nine times between then and the seventh of the seventh, and by the seventh it had changed from a thing he had written into a thing he had done, and he has not worked out when.

---

The water came down over the twentieth and part of the twenty-first of the fourth month and the bank held at eleven feet and nobody drowned, and since then there has been about nine weeks of nothing on that cut, which in nine weeks of the seventh month is not nothing at all.

They have cut. There is about one hundred and forty feet of ditch on the south side and the scour at the third-of-the-way place and both of them are on nobody's list, and the list of works on the bench in the bay has four things on it and two of them are done and one of them is a season's hold by a person in a city four hundred and thirty miles away with a review on it, and the fourth is the word **held** in a weigh-house ledger about three pounds eleven and threepence that a clerk paid out of his own wage in the second month of this year.

A chair of sixty-four fixed a day in the third week of this month for asking a body whether it will accept a duty that stops.

Rennick Adley worked out on the second of the seventh, in about an hour, sitting on the bench with the slate on his knee, that he could not sit in that bay on that day and be any use to anybody, because a body cannot be asked, and because the only thing that can be asked is a person, and because there are about sixty of those wanted and there is no list of them, and because the only list anybody has got is nine hundred and forty names over about sixty years with no heading at the top of it and about four hundred of them dead.

So he went up the lane.

---

He did the arithmetic out loud on the way, because he does it out loud, and because there was nobody to hear it, and because a man who says figures under his breath on a lane in the seventh month is a man who is not surprised by them later.

"Forty-five pence a day. Five men at eight and a boy at five, and that is the Company's rate and it is on a lapsed list about a hundred yards down the road with a foreman's name at the foot of it and a day on it, and it is two days of a crew for seven shillings and sixpence, and nobody in ninety-one households has seven shillings and sixpence.

"A season is about ninety days. Ninety days at forty-five pence is three pounds fifteen and threepence and that is what a spring bank cost a clerk in the city out of his own wage, and he was three weeks of it at seventeen shillings and a penny the week.

"So. **The whole of what a season of that bank costs the Company is nothing.** The Company has four shillings a year and that is the rent of a stone store and it takes a shilling out of the book four times a year to keep the roof on.

"And the only thing that costs anything is a person. **A person's wage is the price. And the price is the point, and everything else in this matter for four months has been free.**"

He got to the first house at about half past six in the morning and it was shut and there was washing out and two children were in the yard and neither of them was hers, and he knocked, and a woman of about forty came to the door and said he wanted what, and he said he wanted to ask the person who lived there one question and that it was one question and that he would not come back without being asked.

The woman of about thirty-one came to the door about four minutes later with a shawl over her shoulders and her hair wet.

---

He asked it in the ordinary way. He did not read it off the slate, because a question read off a slate is a question somebody has written and she has not agreed to be asked by a piece of paper.

"**If there was one person on this cut who held the bank for a season — one person, not a household, not a company, a person, for about ninety days, at forty-five pence a day — would you take it.**"

She looked at him for a while.

"You have come up that lane," she said, "and you wrote down in a book that you were not going to."

"Yes."

"Then say the question again and slower, and I will tell you the answer to the first one and you can decide about the second."

---

So he said it again, slower, and about nine words of it were the same nine words.

Then she said this, and it took about four minutes, and she said it standing in her own doorway with the washing behind her, and he wrote all of it on the slate and read it back to her twice and the second time was slower.

"**You are asking me to hold a thing for a season.** And I know what a season is, and I have had a season and about nine of them, and I know what it is to be the one who is holding it, and I know what happens to me after.

"So I will tell you the thing you have not thought of, and then you can go away. **In three months there will be a day. And on that day it is not mine any more. And I will still be the one they come to.**

"That is what happens to a person who holds a thing. **The duty does not stop. The person stops being asked.** And you have spent four months in that bay telling a room that a name in a book is a chain, and it is, and a chain round a person who is holding a bank is not a chain round a person who is not. That is the whole of what I am saying and I have been saying it since the fourth month and I am not saying it to be difficult."

---

Then she said the thing that is the chapter, and she said it as a fact and not as an accusation, and Rennick Adley read it back to her three times because the first two times he got it wrong off the slate.

"**You want about sixty of us. You are going to ask sixty people one question sixty times, and you are going to do it in sixty doorways, and that is right, and do that.**

"And I will tell you what the sixty is. It is not a list. **A list is a thing you make once. This is a thing you have to keep doing.** Every season. Sixty doors. And the day somebody does not do it, the bank is a bank with nobody on it, and the water does not know the difference and neither will about nine houses behind it.

"So I will take a season. **And I will not give you my name again**, and you may write me down the way you wrote me down the first time, and it is a description and it is harder to serve and I know that, and it is still better than a name, because a name is a thing that can be used about me and about my two children, and a description of a person at the first house is a thing that can be used about a person at the first house."

---

He wrote the description and read it back. She made him change one word in it. The word was *household* and it became *person at the first house* and she said that was better and that she wanted it exactly like that.

Then he asked her the second question, which he had not written on the slate, and she answered it before he had finished asking it.

"**You are going to ask me again.**"

"About once a season."

"Then put it in what you write down that I have been asked twice and that both times were you and neither time was a form." She looked at the lane. "**And put in that I said yes the first time this morning and that I did not want to.**

"Which is the only interesting thing anybody has written in that book in four months and you know it is."

---

Then he went to the second house and there was nobody in it, and he went to the third house and the woman of about forty-four was there and she said no in about nine words and gave a reason, which was that she has four in the house and a husband on the wall, and he wrote the no down with a day against it, and she asked him to read it back and made him read the reason twice.

Then he went the mile and a half and forty feet up to the fourth house, and it took him about fifty minutes because he stopped twice, and he got there at about half past seven in the evening.

---

She opened the door before he knocked, which he wrote down afterwards as the first thing that told him anything.

**She is about seventy-one. She has a leg. She walked four miles of towpath in about two hours each way in the fourth month on the advice of nobody. She cannot read a paragraph. She is the fourth house and not the first and she has been one of five people whom a clerk in a city four hundred and thirty miles away wrote on the back of a proof sheet in the fourth week of the sixth month, and she has never seen the proof sheet and does not know it exists.**

He said his name and the name of the bay and what he was, and then he said the thing he had come to say, which was not the rotation.

"**You wrote to us in the third week of the sixth month. Nine words. A person carried them and the person was not paid.** I have come to ask you the question, the way you asked."

And he read it out twice, and then he read her own nine words back to her, slowly, twice, and she made him do the second one again because he came to the end of it too quickly.

Then he said, "**How would you like to be asked.**"

And she said, "**Once a year is plenty and it can be four times a year if somebody comes up here and does not expect me to come down.**"

"Then that is what will happen. **And it goes in the book with the day on it, in my hand, and you have heard it read out twice now and I am going to read it out twice now and not once, and the reason I am doing that is that I have been told by four people in four months that a thing read once is a thing a person cannot check.**"

---

Then he asked her the rotation question, and he asked it the same way he had asked the other three, in the same words, and he noticed himself doing it and wrote that down afterwards too.

She said no.

She gave the reason she has already given once, in a book, in the last week of the sixth month, in about nine words, and she said it exactly the same, and then she said:

"**And that is the last time I am going to say it. I have said it twice now and in four years somebody is going to read that book and count how many times I was asked the same thing, and the answer is twice, and I have said it twice because the second one was different in one way and the same in every other, and if I say it a third time it is not a reason any more, it is a habit, and I am seventy-one and I do not have time to make a habit out of the only thing I am good at saying.**

"**Write down that I was asked. Write down that I said no. Write down that I said the same words twice and would not say them a third time.** And write down the other one, which is that I would rather be asked at this door four times a year than be in that bay once, and that both of those are my choice and I have been able to make one of them and not the other, and I do not know which one that is, and I have told a person in that bay that in the sixth month and I am telling you it because you are the fifth person and it is the last time it is any use to anybody."

---

He read all of that back. Twice. The second time slower, and he did not come to the end of it too quickly the second time either, and she told him so.

Then she asked him the question, and it is the reason he thought about this chapter for nine days afterwards before he wrote any of it down.

"**You are the book-keeper.**

"**Then what happens to the thirty-one of them.**"

And Rennick Adley stood in a doorway a mile and a half up a lane and found out that he had walked up it four times in eleven weeks for a chair's day in a bay, and that a woman of seventy-one who cannot read a paragraph had asked about thirty-one people in a different district whom he has never met, because a clerk in a city wrote about them on the back of a sheet and told four people in a bay, and a bay is a place where things get said.

He said: "**They are on a board in chalk in a room four miles from here and I have seen it and I cannot do anything about it and I have not tried.**"

"Then why did you come up here."

"Because a chair fixed a day," he said, "and I worked out that on that day there was going to be a question and nobody who could answer it, and you are the only person within a day's walk who has asked me for anything in eleven weeks."

She shut the door about four inches and said, through the gap, "**That is the right answer and it is a bad reason and I would have said both.**"

---

He got back to the bay at about the ninth hour and put the slate on the bench in the middle of the room and there were about eleven people in it and none of them could do anything about any of it.

He wrote it up in the book that night, in his own hand, with the day on it, and then he read it out to the bay twice, the second reading slower, for the people who could not read it. Here is the whole of it.

> *The seventh day of the seventh month. I went up the lane to the four houses at the north of the ford and asked the same question at three of them, in the same words, and the fourth is answered in the second paragraph.*
>
> *The first house: a person of about thirty-one with two children, who refused her name once in a doorway in the fourth month and has refused it a second time this morning. She is entered as a description and I read the description back to her and she changed a word in it and asked that it be left exactly as it is. She says yes to a season and says she did not want to. She says a person who holds a thing stops being asked and the duty does not stop, and that is the finding and I have it from a person who has not got anything to gain by saying it.*
>
> *The third house: a person of about forty-four, no, with a reason, entered with the day.*
>
> *The fourth house: a person of about seventy-one, no, with the same reason she gave in writing in the sixth month, said twice and not a third time, and she asked to be asked at her own door four times a year and that is entered.*
>
> ***And here is the thing I have been sitting in the bay with for four months and have not turned round to look at. A rotation is not a list. A list is a thing you make once. A rotation is one question, asked sixty times, in sixty doorways, by a person who can be asked why, so that sixty answers exist and every one of them is one person's and nobody's else.***
>
> *And the question has to have a day in it, and the day has to be a day the person chose, and there is exactly one kind of day anybody on this cut can choose, and it is the day a thing comes to an end.*
>
> *I have written at the bottom of a page in this book on the thirtieth of the sixth month that I was not going up that lane again on my own account. I have gone up it four times in eleven weeks. **I did it because a chair fixed a day and there was going to be a question on it and nobody who could answer it, and that is a bad reason, and I would have had to do it, and both of those are true and the second one is worse, because being made to do it and being bad at it are the two things that have happened to everybody in this matter for four months.***
>
> *The day a chair fixed is in eight days. About sixty are wanted. I have three answers and two of them are no.*

---

The bay read it out twice and nobody said anything, and then a woman at the back said the thing that about nine people in that room were thinking and nobody wanted to be first at, which is what Rennick Adley has been in four times this year and does not enjoy.

"**Then what is on the list.**"

"Nothing yet," he said.

"**There has to be something on the list inside a week.**"

"There has to be something on the list inside a week," he agreed, "**and the thing that is going on it is not sixty names, and if anybody in this bay writes sixty names down I am going to read it out twice and then I am going to write underneath it that a name in a list is a name a person can be asked about, and I have been the one who said that, and it will read strange.**"

And the book in the stone store has, that week, its first two entries that are not work, and both of them have a day against them, and one of them is a no, and the other one is a question.

Eight days.
