# Chapter 137: A Figure, and a List With It

The landing is a hundred and forty yards below the ford and it is about thirty foot of timber decking with a wagon on it and a shed with a weigh-house in it and an end post with a slate in a wooden frame nailed to it in the open where a customer can see it.

**The slate has been on that post about nine years because a customer wants to know whether his stone has gone.** It is in a man's own hand and it has his name on it and it has his age on it, and it is the first person anybody in this matter ever made findable with a record nobody made on purpose, and a girl of seventeen got it off the nail in a lane in the fourth month without asking him.

**Hob Bray is thirty-eight.** He sells stone off that landing and has done since he was twenty-nine and he is not in this chapter except as a fact about a slate.

**Aldis Kern is forty-six and is the factor of the Kingsway Road Guild for the western reach.** He has a contract with nine months on it, about four hundred yards of road that a flood has just turned into a season's work, and about nine men in three gangs, and a wage bill, and a toll, and a road that a man in a good coat in a city will come and look at in the eighth month.

He came up the road on the third and fourth weeks of the sixth month and he was not in a hurry, and that is the first thing anybody noticed about him, and it is because he had already decided what he was going to say and the deciding took the whole of a wet week.

He had been a factor for nineteen years and he had never once in nineteen years been in a room with about thirty people in it who were not a crew, and the deciding he did in a wet week was not what to say. It was that he was going to have to stand in a room and be told no by people who had nothing, and that a man in his position who has never been told no by anybody who cannot afford to is a man who has not been in a room.

---

He put a slate on the bench in the north bay and it is not a printed thing.

> *Kingsway Road Guild, western reach. A barrow of stone, a man and a boy, one day: one shilling and sixpence, paid at the landing.*
>
> *Ninety days, weather permitting, on the four hundred yards from the ford to the mill wall. Terms as below.*

The bay read it out. About thirty people were in it and about nine of them could not read a word of it and were spoken to by a person sitting next to them.

Then he said the price out loud, in the ordinary way, before he offered anything, and it is the only time in this chapter that he does anything right that is not usual.

"**That is the guild's rate and it is not the cut's rate. The cut's rate is five men at eight pence and a boy at five, which is forty-five pence a day on the five and one, and it is written on a lapsed list about a hundred yards up the road and I have read it, because I came up here to be told things and not to tell them.** The guild pays a man with a barrow more than the Company pays five men, and I am not going to pretend that is generosity, and I am not going to let anybody in this room say it is.

"**The guild is in business. I am a factor and I have nine months and a road, and a road with a ford in it that cannot be crossed in the eighth month is a road I am told about in the eighth month.** That is the whole of why I am standing here."

---

Then he said the price of it, and it took him about four minutes and nobody interrupted him.

"Here it is, and I am going to read it out twice because about nine of you cannot read what is on that slate and I have been told about that this afternoon and I have been told it by a man of thirty-eight who said it four times.

"**The guild cannot pay a man it cannot prove did the work.** That is not a rule of the guild. That is a fact about a man who has a wage bill and about a guild that has about nine hundred years of accounts. If I pay ninety days at one shilling and sixpence and nobody can produce a record that the work was done, then in about four years somebody at the counting end of this road is going to ask me for a list, and there is no list, and I will have been paid out and they will not, and I will be a man who took money and did not deliver and there is no form anywhere for defending that.

"So the guild keeps a list of the persons who did the work. **Not of the households. Of the persons.** A name, a day, and a day rate against it.

"And I am telling you that before I show it to you, and I am telling you what it does, and then you can decide, and I want it understood that I am not able to take the money back off the table and I have known that since the third week of the fifth month and I came anyway."

---

The list was on the second slate and he turned it round himself.

> *The person who did the work. A day. A day rate. Nothing else.*
>
> *A person may be entered on this list who is not of the Company and who is under no duty of the Company, and about nine of the ninety days in this term will be that, because a road is not a bank.*
>
> ***At the foot of this list there is a line. Any person may ask to be left off it. There is no figure against that line and no reason is asked for and nothing follows from it.***

"Nobody has been entered on that," said Aldis Kern. "**It is blank. It is blank because I have not had it filled in and I could fill it in this afternoon in about nine minutes, and I have had it in a wagon since the third week of the fifth month and I have not filled it in, and I would like somebody to ask me why.**"

A voice at the back said, "Because there is no list of ninety-one households."

"There is not," he said. "**And there is not going to be one, and I would like it entered that I said that in a room and was not argued with.**"

---

Here is what he had worked out in a wet week, and he put it out plainly because he had decided that a factor who explains his own price is a factor somebody can say no to, and he had been told by a book-keeper in a bay in the second week of the fifth month that a thing kept in one place is findable in one place, and he had quoted it, and he had not said whose it was.

"Your book has nine hundred and forty names in it and no heading and it is sixty years old and nobody has ever been chosen from it. **That is not a list and it is not a register and it is the best thing anybody on this cut has.** It was not made in order to find anybody. It was made because work got done and somebody wrote it down, and the difference between that and a list is the whole of the difference between a record and a register, and I have been keeping registers for nineteen years and I know which one I am holding.

"**A list of persons is a thing that exists so that somebody can be found.** And that is not a bad thing to make. I want to say that before I go on, because everything I have said in the last four minutes has sounded like I am telling you not to make one, and I am not. I am telling you what it is for.

"If the list exists, then in about four years a person can go to a guild strongbox about nine hundred miles up the road and ask a question about the seventh of the seventh and get the name of whoever was on the bank that day. **And they can do that about ninety times a term. And it is not ninety names in one room, it is a name a day, for about nine hundred days, in a book, that anybody with four pence can come and read.**"

"So it is like the miller's name in our book," said Rennick Adley.

"Yes," said Aldis Kern. "**And I know he has not slept properly since the sixth of the third month and I am not going to pretend to him that a name in a margin is a good thing. I did it in about four seconds and I did not know what it was. You have had nine weeks to find out what it is and I have had nine days, and I have found out less, and I have brought money.**"

---

Then he said the part he had come to say, and he said it slowly, and he said it twice, and the second time he turned the slate round again so that about nine people could follow it.

"**Here is what I am not able to do and I am not able to do it for the guild and not for myself and not because anybody told me to.**

"I cannot offer you the money without the list. **I cannot offer you the list without telling you that I cannot refuse it.** The guild is a body. I am a person and I am instructed. If the treasury at nine hundred miles says the western reach is to have a list of persons working the Slade ford for the term, then there is a list, and I have been the man who made it, and the fact that I stood in a bay and told you I could not refuse it will be in my own letter of instruction, which is a thing I write every week and which I have to write in the last four days of it.

"So I am telling you, and I would be a poor factor if I dressed it up: **I am putting you in a position where you can say no to me.** And I am aware that about nine people in this room have learned in the last two months that a person who is instructed is not a person who is free, and that I am the first one of those who has come up here and said so about himself before anybody asked.

"And I am not able to tell you what the answer is. **I have asked, and I have been told that a company is not a party, and that a company cannot be asked, and I have been told that a thing put on a body is put on everybody in it, and I have been told that by four people in four different rooms in three days and I have believed all four and I have come here to be told it a fifth time by the people who will have to live in it.**"

---

Somebody asked him who had told him to do it, which is the fourth question on a board in a lane in a different district and which nobody on that bank has ever thought to ask a stranger.

It was a man of about thirty in a bay coat at the back, and he asked it in the ordinary way, and Aldis Kern answered in about nine seconds without sitting down.

"The western reach surveyor. He is called — " and he stopped, and it was the first and only time in about two hours that he had to stop. "**His name is on a letterhead and I have said it nine hundred times and I cannot tell you what it is.** He is a person. He writes at the end of every week. That is the whole of what I know about who I am and I have not met him and I have never been in his room and I have never seen a face and I have had about four hundred letters from him and I could not tell you one thing about him, and I could not tell you where he lives, and I could not tell you his mother's name, and if he were to die this morning the guild would be a different shape in about nine months and I would be told about it in a letter.

"**And I want to say the next thing about that, because you have all got very quiet and I would rather say it than have it thought.** When I came up this road I was carrying two things from two places that are four hundred miles apart and neither of them is a person, and both of them are bodies, and both of them have sent me here. A seat sent me a form with a seal on it. A guild sent me a list with a column for a person. **They are the same form. Somebody in one of the two buildings decided that there must be a person at this end of it, and the two of them have not met, and I have met both of them, and I am the only place in this empire where the two of these are in the same pair of hands.**"

Nobody said anything, and he said, "I have said more than I came to say and I am going to stop now, and I am not going to fill in the list this afternoon."

---

They were about a quarter of an hour getting the arithmetic done and it was done out loud, which is the rule of that bay written on the twenty-eighth of the third month and entered a second time in the second week of the fifth month.

Ninety days at one shilling and sixpence for a man and a boy is a hundred and thirty-five shillings, which is six pounds fifteen shillings, which is more than the Company's whole money for about three years, and more than the spring bank cost, and the bay went quiet at that figure and stayed quiet for about eleven seconds.

And then a woman at the back, who is about forty-four and is the third house at the north of the ford, said, in a room of about thirty people:

"**It is a great deal of money.**"

"It is a great deal of money," said Aldis Kern.

"And there are about sixty of us under sixteen."

"There are about sixty of you under sixteen and I have not been in this bay four minutes and I know that because it is on the list of works on the bench and a man of thirty-eight read it out in this room in the second week of this month and I was not here and he read it to about nine people who could not read it."

"So you knew."

"**I knew when I got off the wagon. I came up that road on the eleventh and I stopped at the gate and read the list of works off a bench through a door, because a thing on a bench is findable and I have heard that sentence three times this month and I have begun to believe it.**"

---

Then he said the last of it, and it is in this chapter because it is the only time anybody in four hundred yards of that cut says the name.

"The same paper went to the Sorn border households on the northern marches in the second month of this year. Not this one. **The same form, the same device, the same offer, at no charge.** House Sorn has the most restrictive border law of the nine and it requires that a thing offered to a border household at no charge be answered, and the Sorn households answered it, and they refused it, and the refusal is entered at a waystation because their law says a refusal is entered.

"Nobody on this cut has seen that entry. I have seen a list of its title at a trade counter in a city about nine hundred miles up the road and I have not read it and I cannot get to it and it is not mine.

"**And it is the only document anybody has mentioned to me in four months in which a House was made to put something in a book about itself and could not leave it out.** I am telling you that not because it is any use to you. I am telling you because I am about to hand you a list of persons, and I would like it to be on the record that I know of one list that went the other way."

Nobody asked him what the other way was. He said it was a refusal and that a refusal is a thing that has a person in it.

Then he put the two slates on the bench together, and asked for the list to be read out twice, and asked that the second reading be the slower one, and said that he would wait while it was done and would not go out to the wagon.

And it was read out twice, by a book-keeper of thirty-eight and then by a girl of seventeen who is not paid and will not say what it means, and about nine people in that bay could not read a word of it, and the line at the foot of it with no figure against it was read out last, and it was the last thing anybody in ninety-one households had heard in six weeks that they were allowed to do.
