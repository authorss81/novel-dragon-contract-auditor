# Chapter 117: Nine Stones

The seventh work on the page from ninety-one years back is the ford, and the stones of it, and the two posts. It is the seventh of eleven and Rennick Adley read it out in a store on the second of the third month and Tavi Venn read it out after him, twice, and it is not on anybody's list in this matter and it has not been on anybody's list for about thirty years, and the only reason it is on a piece of paper anybody has read this month is that a girl of seventeen could read a paragraph and a book-keeper of thirty-eight had a lamp.

Somebody at the Slade end said the ford wanted looking at, in passing, about the seed. That was on about the twenty-fourth of the third month, to a man of about thirty-eight who was in a yard for another reason, and it was said in the way people say a thing they have no standing to say, and it was said to nobody in particular and it has not been repeated since by anybody.

Tavi Venn walked up the towpath on the twenty-ninth of the third month at about the sixth hour in the morning with a pail and a loaf and no money, and it is two hours each way and she is seventeen and she has an examination passed in reading and writing and arithmetic and ditching on the level and the setting of a gate linkage to a maker's gauge, and **there is no form of any kind in this district or the district above it that says a person walks two hours to look at a ford.**

Nobody asked her to come. Nobody paid her. She has been the person who turns up since the twenty-seventh of the first month and she is the only person in this matter who is never going to be paid, and the whole of the cost of that is four words long and she said them in a kitchen in front of eleven people and she has not changed them.

---

The north of the ford is a lane of channel-side houses with the water on one side and about nine feet of beaten earth between the water and the doors, and the ford itself is where the lane crosses, and the crossing is the reason the houses are here and the reason there have always been houses here.

She got there at about the eighth hour and she had about a minute and a half of looking before she understood what she was looking at, and then she stood in the lane for a bit.

The two posts are down.

That is the first thing and it is the thing a person who has not seen a ford does not understand. **The two posts are not broken.** They are out of their sockets, lying on the low side, squared off at the ends, one on top of the other, and beside them are nine stones, and the nine stones are out of the throat of the ford and they are stacked too, four and five, in the way a man stacks things he intends to come back for.

Depth marks. A post in a ford is not a fence and it is not a decoration; it is a mark that says to a man with a barrow at four in the morning in flat light how deep it is going to be where he is about to put his wheels, and **a ford with no depth marks and no hard stones in the throat is not a ford. It is a place where the water happens.**

And a man of about thirty-eight was standing by the stack with a barrow, and he had a coat on and he had clearly been standing there a while.

"You have been a long time getting here," he said.

"Two hours."

"I have been here about an hour and a half." He said it without unkindness. "I did not know anybody was coming. I want to say that first, because I know what a thing looks like when somebody has come about a thing."

"What is that," said Tavi Venn, "and who took it, and when."

---

He told her, and he told it straight, and he did not once ask her who she was or what she was about.

"I took them. Fourth week of the second month, and I came back in the first week of the third, and I have not been back since, and I have thought about coming back about four times.

"**Nobody has asked me to stop.** That is what I have got to say about it and I have been saying it to myself for about a fortnight. There is nobody in this district who knows the ford is down. I have been up and down this lane four miles in my life in a fortnight and I have spoken to about nine people and not one of them has said anything about the stones, and one of them walked past the stack twice.

"**I am not going to pretend I did it because of the seed. I did it because I had a cart and a boy and no other hard stone within two miles, and because the ford fills at the shallow place now and does not fill at the deep place, and because if I had a load on the ford in the last week of the second month with the water where it was, I would have come off that ford in about four foot of it in about nine seconds and I would have lost the cart and possibly the boy.

"And I am not going to pretend I thought about the depth marks. I thought about the water. **I had not looked at the posts in about eleven years and I could not have told you they were there**, and I have since been up here in the dark with a lamp to look at where they go, and I have found out where they go, and it is in a hole in the bed, and I can put them back, and I want to put them back.

"And I am not doing it. That is the part I have been carrying for a fortnight and it is why I am standing by a stack instead of asleep. **If I put them back then the ford is the ford again and I am the man who took nine stones out of it, and the ford is the ford whether they are back in it or not.** And somebody will come eventually. Everything comes eventually in this district, in about four years, and the water office sends a memorandum and it goes to a dissolved thing, and my name is not going to be on it and I am not going to put it there.

"So I have left them. And I have been standing here about an hour and a half waiting to be told off, and you have not told me off, and I would like to know what you came about."

---

She thought about it for longer than a person of seventeen is supposed to think about anything.

"I am not going to ask your name," she said.

"Why not."

"Because the moment a name goes on a paper about a ford, it is a name anybody can ask about the ford." She said it in the plainest words she had and she did not soften it, because she had decided in a kitchen in the first month not to soften this class of sentence. "**That is the whole of it and I have said it to about eleven people already this month and I am not going to say it to you differently because you have been decent about the stones.**

"And I know what a box is, because I have watched two names go into one and I have watched one man say no to it in nine words in front of forty people, and I have read that sheet out twice in a kitchen, and there is nothing at the top of it and everything in the middle of it and it has a keeper's name at the bottom of it and that is the whole of what is in that paper. **And I am not going to be the person who asks a man at the top of a lane what his name is, and writes it down, and hands it to somebody, and that is not modesty, that is a condition I stated in a kitchen in front of eleven people and I have not changed it and I will not care whose name it is.**

"So you do not have to give it to me. Nobody is going to ask you for it. I have walked two hours to look at a ford and I have looked at it and you have told me."

The man of about thirty-eight looked at her for a while.

"About right," he said.

---

And then a woman came out into the lane from about the fourth door and stood about nine feet away and did not say anything for about a minute.

"Where did the stones go," said Tavi Venn.

"They have gone down to Brenn's landing," said the woman, "because that is where hard stone comes from when a man wants it and there is nowhere else on this reach. He went down with a barrow in the fourth week of the second month and got nine of them, and he paid for them, and he wrote it in the book, because he writes everything in the book, and I have been up and down this lane for about fifty-five years and I can tell you that there is a nail on that landing with a book on it and anybody in this district may read it and has done so, including me, and including the woman at the second house who cannot read a paragraph and goes and looks at it anyway because she likes the look of the writing."

"Thank you," said Tavi Venn.

Then she said, to the man standing by the stack, and she said it out loud in the lane and not to the woman and not to herself:

"**Nine stones. Two posts. And about thirty years.** That is the arithmetic of it and I have got no more than that and I am not going to make it into more than that, because that is the other thing I do not do. I do not tell anybody what it means."

And then she did the other thing she does, which is the thing she stated in the kitchen, and she did it in a lane, in daylight, to a man she has never seen before, and she said it out loud and she said it twice.

There is a bench against the north wall of a bay off the Slade, and on that bench since about the eighth hour last night there has been a piece of paper with a miller's name at the top of it, and it is about four hundred and forty foot of the outer face of a bank, and it has nine dates on it in a miller's own hand on which he saw the face move.

Tavi Venn read it out, in a lane, in daylight, standing in about nine feet of beaten earth between the water and the doors, and she read it twice, and she would not tell him what it meant, and she had carried it two hours up a towpath in her own bag and she had not been asked to and she was not going to be paid for it and there is no form in the empire that says she is to be.

The man listened to it the whole of both readings.

"Who is that," he said, at the end of the second one.

"A man about two miles down the channel who has been trying to give that to the district since the sixth year and has sent it four times and it has come back four times and nobody has ever come for it."

"And his name is on it."

"His name is on it," said Tavi Venn, "and he worked out on his own in about six years, in his own kitchen, with nobody to ask, that a name at the top of a paper about a bank is a name anybody can ask about the bank. **And nobody has ever told him he was right, and I have not got time this morning and I am not going to try.**"

---

The woman in the fourth door had not gone in.

She was about seventy-one and she had come out into the lane in her own door while Tavi Venn was reading and she had not asked what the paper said, and she had not left, and she stood about nine feet off for the whole of the second reading with her hands at her front and did not move.

"You read it twice," she said, at the end.

"I always read it twice," said Tavi Venn. "Once for the people who can see the words and once for the people who cannot."

"I cannot read a paragraph," said the woman, "and I have not pretended to for fifty-five years, and you are the first person who has ever read anything to me twice about a thing that was going to happen to me, and I would like you to know that I heard every word of it, and I have not got one question, and I am not going to ask you what it means, and if you told me I would not be able to put it anywhere."

"I am not going to tell you," said Tavi Venn.

"I know you are not. That is why I am still standing here."

And the woman went in, and shut her door, and it was the only door on that side of the lane that was shut when Tavi Venn left it.

---

The landing is a hundred and about forty yards below the ford on the low side, and it is a stone face about nine foot long with a wet rope and a bollard, and it is where stone comes off a barrow and goes into a cart, and it is not an office and it has no clerk and no book of record and no seal.

The book is on a nail.

It is a slate about two foot long with a wooden frame and it hangs on a nail in the open on the end post where anybody coming off the water can see it, and it is nailed there because a man who buys stone wants to know whether his stone has gone, and the man who sells it has worked out that the cheapest way to answer that question is to write it where the buyer can see it, and he has been doing it for about nine years.

It is not a register. It is not a court record. It is not evidence of anything against anybody. It is a list of what has gone off that landing and when and who took it, kept openly, on a nail, in the weather, for customers.

It had about forty lines on it. Most of them are about corn and about cob and about a load of stone for a wall. And eleven lines down, in a hand of thirty-eight, with the day:

> *Third week of the second month. Nine stones, hard, off the ford, north of the crossing, the throat. H. Bray. Paid.*

Tavi Venn read it standing in front of it with her hand not touching it, and then she read it again, and then she turned round and looked up the hundred and forty yards at the lane, where a man of about thirty-eight was still standing by a stack of nine stones beside two posts that were squared off at the ends.

**Nobody had ever put that name anywhere on purpose.** It was on a nail because a man wanted to know whether nine stones had gone, and the man who took the stones wrote his own name in the ordinary way, in the ordinary place, on a day when it was nothing to anybody but him and a customer, and it has been on that nail since the third week of the second month, in the open, in the weather, for about five weeks, and it can be read by anybody who comes off the water.

She read that line a third time, because there was a first name on it, and she had to lean in to get it, and a man who wants to know whether his stone has gone does not want initials:

*Third week of the second month. Nine stones, hard, off the ford, north of the crossing, the throat. Hob Bray. Thirty-eight. Paid.*

**That is how she has his name and she did not ask him for it, and she told nobody in that lane that she had it, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that says what a person may do with a name they did not ask for.**

Tavi Venn had walked two hours to a lane to tell a man she did not know that she was not going to ask his name.

She did not go back up. She stood at the landing for about four minutes with her hand on the wet rope, and then she went home, and she is not going to be paid for any part of it and there is no form.

And standing there, with the slate on the nail about nine feet above her head and the wet rope cold under her hand, she worked out the second half of an answer she has been carrying since the twentieth of the last month of the year before, and she has never said the whole of it to anybody, including in a kitchen, and she is not going to say it to anybody now.

The first half is a person who turns up. She gave that in a kitchen in front of eleven people and she was right and it cost nothing to say.

**The second half is that a person who turns up has to be told what she is turning up about.**

Nobody told her. Not a keeper and not a book-keeper and not a girl of seventeen's own mother; a man at the Slade end said the ford wanted looking at in a yard, to nobody in particular, in passing, about the seed, and that is the entire sum of what was communicated in either direction about four hundred and forty foot of bank and nine stones and a boy of fifteen, and she got up at about the fifth hour and walked two hours each way on the strength of it.

And she went. And that is the finding, and it is not a good one, and she has not got anybody to put it in front of.

"**The going is the whole of the cost and it is not paid and it cannot be.** Not by a fund, not at the end of the year, not in the fourth month. The person who turns up pays the two hours out of her own legs and nobody reimburses it because there is nothing to reimburse it against, and if the water is in the fourth month and she turns up for that as well then it is four more hours, and the answer to *and who is it that reads it to the ones who cannot read it* is a person of seventeen with a pail and no money, and the reason that person is there is that nobody with money was told."

She has not told anybody that. She will not tell anybody that. She has a condition about names in boxes and a condition about what a paper means, and this is a third thing, and it is the only one of the three she has not stated in a room, and the reason is that stating it in a room would be asking somebody to pay her, and she would then be a person who reads for money, and that is a different person.

---

A ford's posts were down on the twenty-ninth of the third month and the stones were out of the throat and stacked neatly on the low side, and the man who took them said the reason out loud in a lane in about nine sentences and said that nobody had asked him to stop, and a girl of seventeen read a miller's paper out loud twice in the same lane and would not say what it meant and did not ask the man's name, and a woman of about seventy-one stood in her own door and heard the whole of it and went in and shut her door.

And about four hundred and thirty miles from that lane, at about the fourth counter of a notary's table in a city, a shilling's worth of good paper with four lines on it was lying in a book with no office on it, and the fourth of the four lines was blank, and the whole of the way into it runs through a person the instrument touches.
