# Chapter 115: What It Is For

The Docket of Powers is three rooms off the corridor behind the third gallery, with a card in a window and no sign on the door. The first room holds the register of persons who read and a bench for waiting on. The second room holds nothing anybody can name and is used for things that are not the first room. The third room has a long table in it, and the table came with the room about sixty years ago, and it is oak, and it is scarred in a way that four generations of men have made and none of them wrote down.

Marek Kest came down the corridor at about a quarter before the second hour of the afternoon on the twenty-first day of the third month, with a sheet of foolscap in his coat that he had ruled himself at his own table, and Tamsin Rook came down the corridor behind him a minute later, and neither of them said anything about the minute, and the minute is a fact about two people who have walked corridors together for fourteen months and have never once discussed where they go.

The thing he had come about is the fourth line of a four-line form, and the form is a third of a sheet of good paper bought at a shilling at the fourth counter of a notary's table in this city two days ago, and the fourth line is blank, and all four lines are blank, and the paper is in Tamsin Rook's bag.

Oswin Marrow had the day's book open on the long table.

"I am going to say the three powers, and then I am going to do the thing you have come about, and if I do the thing you have come about second then you will think the first three were a formality. They are not. **They are the whole of this office and they have been the whole of this office for sixty years and there is not a fourth and I have looked for one for five months.**"

"One. I can enter a finding in a public register. Two. I can bar a person from any audit in any jurisdiction until examined. Three. I can refer one question to the court sitting in room four. I have used all three on you. The first and the second were the examination in the first month of last year and the finding in the ninth of the ninth, and the third was the question that produced the sentence that put you under a watch, and I have not used the third since and I am not going to pretend I have anything else in the drawer."

"You have come about a fourth line."

"Yes."

"**Then I am going to do the third one and it is going to cost me a day and a half of my own afternoon, and there is no fund for it in this office or in any other, and I am saying that before I start for the same reason I said it in the first month of last year.** You came here on the twenty-second of the first month and I told you that a man who has had an afternoon taken off him should be told at the beginning. You are the tenth. There have been four since you, all of them examinations of files, and one of them was a woman of about thirty-four from a landing office who read nine pages and went away with a headache, and I have not been thanked for that either and I am not going to be."

---

He did not open the register. He opened a different book, on the shelf, which is the reference book, and which is not the register and has never been the register.

"You bought a form at the fourth counter and you did not fill it in. I want to know why, and I am going to guess, and you will tell me if I am wrong.

"**Because the fourth line is the line you become a party on, and you cannot write it, because what the instrument does was done to ninety-one households at once.** Yes or no."

"Yes," said Marek Kest.

"Right. Then what you have is a question, and a question is the one thing this office can do something with, and it can do almost nothing with it, and I am going to tell you the almost nothing in about four minutes and then do it, and then it will take the afternoon, and then the thing that comes back in about nine days will be nothing, and I have sent four of them since the ninth of the ninth and got four nothings and I have kept all four in a file on that shelf that nobody has ever asked me for."

He put his hand on the reference book.

"**One. I can refer one question to the court sitting in room four. That is the third of my three powers and it is the only one of the three that does anything.** It goes up a stair into a room on a day fixed about six weeks off, and the registrar takes it and does one of three things, and in nine years he has done the third thing four times.

"What he cannot do is answer it. **He can refer it. He can read it out. He can enter it as a question examined and not answered.** And when he enters it as a question examined and not answered, then it goes on a page, and about four people sit in a room on an ordinary afternoon, and that is the whole of the supply of hearings that sentence of the eighteenth of the seventh has created in this empire in eight months. Four. On a good afternoon. And every one of the four is a person who came, and about nine hundred people did not come, and there is no form anywhere in this empire for compelling a person to be brought to a room, and there is no fund for the carriage of a person who lives four hundred and thirty miles away, and I have tried to get both of those for four years and there is no form for either.

"And the whole of the work that order creates — every person that is now a person who must be heard before anything binds them — is done by clerks at counters and women with barrows, and **not one of them is in the room where the order was made, and not one of them has read the order, and the order has been read in about nine rooms in this city and I have been in two of them.**"

He said the next part without any particular weight, which is how he says the things he has thought about longest.

"And a person who turns up to be counted is not a person who has been heard. I have wanted to write that on the back of the order for four months and I have not done it, because I am a register and I keep what is put in front of me."

---

"Your question is going up in these words, and I am writing them and you will read them and if you do not like one of them you may say so and I will change it and enter that you said so.

> *Referred on the twenty-first day of the third month on the applicant's own initiative, and asked for by nobody. Whether a form of general use which binds nobody who has not been heard may be put in, in the name of one person, before it takes effect upon any person in a household of about ninety-one; and if it may not, what may be put in; and if nothing may be put in, then whether that is a finding of an office or only of this office. The applicant is a person of about thirty-eight in the city of Auremar, four hundred and thirty miles from the cut the question is about, and is not a party to any matter and is not a person an instrument has touched. There is no fund.*

"That is a long question and I have made it long on purpose. **A short question gets a short answer, and about four people a year bring me a short question and I refer it and it comes back in nine days in four lines and none of the four lines is about their bank.**"

He wrote it out. It took him about nine minutes and he read it back and made Marek Kest read it back, which he does with everybody, and then he put the day and the day and a half in the day's book, and the day and a half is in his own afternoon and there is no fund.

"There. That is the third of the three. And I want to tell you what it is worth, because nobody ever tells people.

"**It is worth nine days, and it is worth a page, and it is worth nothing else in this empire.** In about nine days the registrar will read that question out in room four, or he will not, because there is about one sitting in six weeks and I have missed one in two years, and he will enter it as a question examined and not answered, and I will file the paper on the shelf with the other three, and the four of them together in about four years will be the only record that anybody ever asked this question in a room.

"And in about four years somebody who wants a bank and not a man will be able to go and read that page and find out that four hundred people asked, and the four hundred will be on it, and their names will be on it, and the man who asked for it to be entered in a book with a name in it will be on it, and that is the trade. That is what a referral is. It is a way of writing a question down in a room with a witness in it, and it is the only thing in this matter that anybody has ever been able to do."

---

Then he put the pen down and said the thing he had not come to say and had been in the room for four minutes to say.

"Now the register, and then I will let you stand up and go down the corridor, and I would like you to hear the register first.

"**The first room keeps persons and not records.** That is the whole of why it is safe and it is also the whole of why it cannot help you, and I have never had anybody in twenty-two years sit in that room and work out both of those at once, and you are the first and you did it in four minutes, so I am going to skip to the end of it.

"About fourteen people a year agree to have a name in it. About fourteen. Out of everything that comes up this corridor, which is a great deal more than fourteen. **Because a name in that book is a name a man with a tray can ask about, for ever, for anything at all, and it is the one door in this empire that a person cannot shut.** I have been putting names in that book for twenty-two years and I have never once been asked to answer for one and I have never wanted to, and I have watched about a hundred and forty people decide not to, and about nine hundred people not come at all.

"And you. **The only procedure for being entered in the register of persons who read is a written application of one line stating the authority under which the person reads.** One line. It has been that line for about four hundred years and it is a good line and I have never thought of changing it.

"You have no authority. You were discharged on the twelfth of the ninth by your own hand and the authority in that entry has ended. You were found eligible on the nineteenth of the ninth in one sentence and that sentence carries no reading in it and no authority to read under.

"**So you do not write the line, and you are not entered, and you have not asked to be, and I have not asked you to write it, and I am not going to.** And I want you to notice that the reason I am not going to is not that it would be refused. It would not be refused. **The line is one line and you could write it in about nine seconds with a false authority in it and I would have entered you and I would have had a good day.** And a good day is not worth a name in my book that I put in for a man who was not entitled to it."

Marek Kest said: "I know. I have had four months to think about that line and I have not written it and I am not going to, and you are not the reason."

"I know you are not," said Oswin Marrow. "I said it out loud because I am sixty and I have been the master of a register of persons for twenty-two years and in four months of watching you not write one line I have worked out that I would rather be asked for a thing and refused than have it, and that is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me about that book in a decade."

---

The nine minutes were in the third room with the long table and the copyist of the third gallery, who is not named in this matter and has written the time in the margin in ink on about four hundred sheets and has never once been thanked and was not thanked on the twenty-second of the first month either.

Marek Kest stood at the end of the table and stated the price of the one instrument he is not permitted to write, and it took four minutes, and he stated it as a price and not as a plea, and the copyist wrote all of it down because a copyist writes down what is said in a room, and a training is a reading of a file in a room with a person in it who is not the person being trained, and what the twenty-first of the third month was is not a training and nobody is going to call it one.

"**Here is what it would cost, and it is four lines, and it is the four lines I wrote in my own book on the sixteenth of the second month in a weigh-house and did not send.**

"One. It moves one specified consequence off two named persons onto ninety-one named persons in rotation.

"Two. It gives the rotation a term and a date and a right of refusal, and it says the refusal costs nothing, and the refusal is the whole of the instrument and everything else in it is bookkeeping.

"Three. **The whole of its cost is named labour and named liability.** Not a fund. Not a levy. Not a mark. Labour and liability, and both of them have a name and a face on them, and a person can be asked whether they will do the labour and can be told what the liability is on a day they can be shown it.

"Four. **It is not a gift and it is not a fund and I am not paid.** I have not been paid to draft it, I am not paid to hold it, and if it worked for nine years and the bank stood through nine waters, the payment for nine years of that would be a name against a list of works, and that is the same instrument again, and I have been round it four times and it is the same instrument."

He stopped.

"That is the price. I have said it out loud in a room three times and it has been shorter every time and I do not think it can be shorter, and I have never once been asked why it costs that, and I have never once said what it is for, and I have noticed that I do not know."

Oswin Marrow, who had been standing at the end of his own table for about two minutes and a half with his hands behind his back, said: "That is not the question I was going to ask you. I am going to ask you a different one and I am going to ask it in the ordinary way, because that is how it is asked, and you are a person in a room and there is a copyist in it and I am sixty and I would rather you answered it than that I put it off.

"**What is it for.**"

And Marek Kest stood at the end of a table that came with the room and did not answer for about eleven seconds.

"I cannot tell you that," he said, "and I want to be exact about why, because *I do not know* is a thing a man says when he has not looked, and I have looked.

"**It is for a bank.** Four hundred and forty foot of outer face at the Slade, and about nine acres and thirty-one households behind it, and a district works that cannot compel the work, and a water office that gave its flood authority away in writing to two people on purpose.

"That is the thing it is for. That is what I have got in four lines. And if you are asking me what the bank is for, then I have been in this district for about a month and I have not worked that out either, and I have not asked, and the reason I have not asked is that I have been in about nine rooms in this matter in fourteen months and I have asked about four documents and I have not once asked anybody what a piece of water is for."

Oswin Marrow looked at him for a while.

"No," he said. "That is not what I asked and you have answered a different question correctly, and I have watched men do that in this room for twenty-two years, and I want to tell you why I am not going to accept it.

"**You have a price. A thing with a price is a thing with a purpose and you know what the purpose is, and you have written the price down twice, and the reason you cannot say the purpose is not that you have not looked.** It is that the purpose and the price came out of the same room, and about four times a year a person in this corridor hands me a document and tells me what it will cost and cannot tell me what it is for, and in about two years I have found that those two men had both been told by somebody else what the cost was.

"So it is for a bank. And the bank is for something. And you have four lines and a price and no sentence, and that is not a fault in you, and it is the shape of everything anybody has handed me in fourteen months that I have been able to hand on to anybody."

Tamsin Rook said one sentence. She had been standing by the door for about eleven minutes and had not said anything, and it is the only thing she said in the room.

"**A cost nobody can be shown the bill for is not a smaller cost. It is an unpriced one, and an unpriced one is a cost somebody else finds out about later, and there is nobody in this room who will be standing there later.**"

"That is the best sentence anybody has said in this room in five years," said Oswin Marrow, "and I am not going to say it is a finding, because it is not one, and if it were one it would be a rule, and I have not got a fourth power."

---

He walked them to the door, which he does, and stopped, which he does, and said the last of it, which is the thing he says at a door and which is about himself and which he has now said twice.

"The question is up the corridor. It will be read out or it will not be read out. In about nine days there will be a page that says it was examined and not answered, and that page is going in a file on that shelf next to three others, and the four of them are the only record of this and there are four hundred people on it and one of them is you and one of them is her and **neither of you will be able to do one single thing with it, and that is the correct result and I have got to keep telling myself that for about nine years.**

"And I am going to say the one thing I have not said to you and then you can go down the corridor.

"**A register keeps persons and not records. That is why it is safe. And it is safe because it cannot say what a reading did to a file, and that is the whole of what I have for twenty-two years, and it is also the reason it cannot say what a form does to a person, and you have just spent two days at a counter finding that out, and you have come up that corridor with a blank form and a blank fourth line, and there is nobody in this empire — not in this room, not in that building, not in any court — who can tell you what happens to a person the day they write that line, except a person who has already been harmed and a person who has not.**

"And you are the second of those and it is not a small thing to be, and I want it written down by somebody who is not me that I said it, and I have said it twice now to two different people and it is still the truest thing I know about a register of persons, and I have never once been examined over it, and I have been waiting to be."

---

He was in the Received Room that night with the stove in and the bar on the inside of the frame and eleven feet of shelf.

He wrote out the afternoon in his own book and read it back and crossed nothing out, and near the end of it, between the entry about the four lines and the entry about the page on the shelf, he wrote a line he had written once before in a different month in a different room and had then crossed out, and he did not cross it out this time.

> *There is a document in a room I work in that I have not opened. The reason I have not opened it is the reason I have not started a case. I have now written that sentence twice, and both times it was because somebody in a room said something to me that was better than what I had, and neither of them knew there was a document and neither of them would have used it.*

> *I have read no instrument tonight. I have read no instrument on any day in this matter. There is no mark on me and there is no certification and there is nothing in this room tonight that an auditor has done. In about nine days there will be a page upstairs with four questions examined and not answered on it, and my name will be on it, and about four hundred people's names will be on it, and none of them will be able to do a thing with it.*

> *What I have not been able to say in a room and could not answer when a man of sixty asked me in the ordinary way: what the four lines are for. I know the price. I do not know the purpose. I have had that for two months and I have not looked at it, and the reason I have not looked at it is that the purpose is in the only direction I have been forbidden to go for nine months, and I would rather be asked a second time than answer it wrong in a room with a copyist in it.*

> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the twenty-first day of the third month, at about the ninth hour.*
