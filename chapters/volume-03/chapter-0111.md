# Chapter 111: What a Bay Costs

The Company hires a stone building off the Slade with the floor a foot off the ground, and it has hired it for eleven years at a shilling a quarter, and the shilling comes out of the book four times a year and the four shillings a year is the whole of the money this body has had for sixty years. The key is in Rennick Adley's mother's keeping and she is sixty-eight and has never once been asked for it by anybody but him.

The building has a south end and a north end. The south end is where the book is and it is where the book has been for sixty years, and it is dry, and it is eleven feet by nine, and about eleven people can stand in it without touching one another. The north end is a bay that is not hired. It is on the same level, it is the same stone, it is thirty-one feet by nineteen, and it has a bench against the north wall that came with the building, and it has a door in the west end that has never shut properly because the lintel has dropped about half an inch in about forty years and nobody has had it packed.

It is the biggest room within two miles of the Slade end that anybody in ninety-one households can stand in.

On the sixteenth of the first month, in the mill yard, in the middle of an afternoon, a man of about forty-six said that the north bay was empty from about the middle of the fourth month to about the middle of the ninth, and that a body could hold a meeting in it, and that he was only saying it.

Rennick Adley said no.

Then he said yes, about a minute later, and then he said that he would have to ask somebody, and then he said nothing at all for about eleven minutes, and four people waited for him to finish and he did not finish, and afterwards nobody asked him again.

That was seven weeks before the sixth day of the third month, and in those seven weeks the bay had been empty the whole time, and nobody had knocked on the door of the man who owned it.

---

The miller's own door is in a range older than the store by about a hundred years, and the fanlight over it was painted shut at some point in the last forty years and never opened. Aven Tace is about fifty and does not use his mill for four months in the year. He is one of the thirty-one households behind the bank at the third-of-the-way place, which has gone over nine inches twice in nine years and that the crew faced on the twenty-first of the second month and that has not gone over this year.

He stood in the door with a hand on the frame on the sixth day of the third month, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, the same way he had stood there for a whole hour in the twentieth of the first month while about forty people stood in his yard and nobody could tell him to go away.

Rennick Adley said the thing he had come about.

"Mr Tace. I want to ask you what the north bay costs."

"That is a strange thing to walk four miles for," said the miller.

"It is a strange thing to answer," said the book-keeper. "I have come four miles and I have said one question, so you have got a fair exchange either way."

The miller took his hand off the frame and went and got a stick, which is what a man does when he is going to measure something out of politeness and not because of any need.

"The north bay is thirty-one foot by nineteen and the floor is a foot off the ground the same as the store, and it holds about thirty and I have counted it twice with a crowd in it. It has a bench. The door has a lintel on it that has dropped and it has never been packed and it will not shut, and I am telling you that so that you do not find it out in the fourth month with thirty people in it.

"You have been in the south end eleven years at a shilling a quarter. **The north bay is not in that.** It is mine, and it is empty from the middle of the fourth month to the middle of the ninth, and the two ends of it have been unused for about nine years and I have used the one for sacks and the other for nothing."

"How much for the year."

"Nothing," said the miller.

Rennick Adley stood in the yard in the daylight and did not say anything for a moment.

"That is not a price," he said.

"It is a price," said the miller. "It is a price of nothing, for a year, and I am going to tell you why I have said it in about four minutes and then you are going to want to give me something for it, because everybody does, and I am telling you now that if you give me something for it then I have made it a transaction and I will have to think about whether I want the transaction."

"Mr Tace—"

"**Four minutes.** I have had this in an envelope in my own kitchen for six years and I have sent it four times and I have not been in a room with anybody about it, and you are the first person who has come to this door in a year, and the reason you have come is not that you have come to see me."

---

Rennick Adley did not know what was in the envelope. He had known since the twentieth of the first month, in the yard, in an hour when the miller had said nothing at all and had afterwards given one reason, which was that there is no form for a miller to say *you may not stand in my yard*, and about forty of his customers were standing in it.

The miller went into the range and came back with a sheet of paper folded in four and soft at the creases, and he did not give it to him. He held it.

"That is mine," he said. "I wrote it. The sixth year. It is about four hundred and forty foot of the outer face at the third-of-the-way place and I have written down the day nine times that I saw the face move, and I have written down how far it went each time, and there are two of those nine that I can prove and seven that I cannot, and I have written down which are which because a man who cannot tell you which of his own figures are soft is not a man you can use.

"**It has my name at the top of it.** Aven Tace, of the mill yard, and the day, and what I am, and the four hundred and forty foot.

"And at the foot of it I have written that I sent it to the district works in the sixth year and the second year after that and the third and the fourth, and that nobody has ever come for it. That is the whole of it. Four times, four roads, and four returns, and not one person has ever come to the mill and said *give it here*."

He turned it over and put his thumb on the back of it, and there was nothing on the back.

"Now I am going to tell you why I have not handed that paper to anybody in six years, and I have never said it out loud to anybody, and I have had it every day for six years, and I am saying it to you in a yard because you asked me a question in a yard and I have been waiting since the first month for somebody to ask me one."

"Mr Tace—"

"**The moment my name is at the top of a paper about that bank, then that bank is a thing that can be asked about my name.**"

The yard was quiet for a moment and out past the end of it the channel went by.

"And I have thought about the whole of it for six years, by myself, in this kitchen, and I have got to the bottom of it and I am not proud of the bottom of it. It is this. A bank with a miller who cares about it is a bank. A bank with a miller's name at the top of a paper about it is a **case**, and a case can be carried somewhere. And once it can be carried somewhere, then there is a day somebody comes to this door with a thing in his hand and he is very polite and he says *I only want to ask you about the ninth*, and I am fifty-one next spring and I will say what I saw, because I did see it, and I will be a man who was asked about a bank.

"**And that is not the part I mind.** I mind the seven. I have written down seven times that I saw that face move and I cannot prove one of the seven, and the day my name goes on the top of that paper, the seven is what somebody will want. Not to do me harm. To have a look. Because that is what a thing in a hand is for, whatever hand it is in, and I have found that out by myself about six years too late and about nine hundred times, and there has never been anybody in this district I could ask, and I have wanted to ask somebody about nine times a month for six years."

Nobody had ever told him that he was right.

That is the thing Rennick Adley carried out of the mill yard on the sixth day of the third month, and it was not the bay and it was not the paper, and it was the third thing, and it took him the whole of the walk back to be sure of it. In nineteen years of meetings, in a hundred and forty files four miles down the river, in fourteen months of a man from a city writing words at the top of documents, **not one person had ever gone to a householder and said: you have worked that out on your own and you are right, and here is what it costs to be right.**

---

He took the paper. That was the fourth minute and it was the whole of the negotiation and neither of them called it a negotiation.

Then the miller said the rest of it, from the door, and it was about the price.

"Now. About nothing. **You are going to go away from this yard with a bay that costs nothing and you are going to think you have had a good morning, and I want to tell you what you have had.** You have had a third thing you cannot pay for.

"Your body has four shillings a year in it. That is the rent of a room you keep a book in, it comes out of the book four times a year, and it has been the whole of your money since before your mother was born. And now you have a room you have not paid for and you have a paper in a book you cannot pay for either, and both of them came out of my door this afternoon, and I have not written either of them down anywhere, because a thing you write down is a thing somebody can ask you for.

"So that is my half, and I would like it said out loud in your yard, and it is not modesty, it is this: **a thing that is free is a thing nobody can refuse, and I have found that out the hard way, because I have given things away for nothing four times in this yard and three of them were not free.**"

"Then what was the third one," said Rennick Adley.

"The third one was the last four months of the fourth month, the year my wife died," said the miller. "Do not write that down."

Rennick Adley did not write it down.

"And now your half, because you have been standing in my yard for four minutes not saying the thing you came to say, and I have watched you not say it. **You did not come about the bay.**"

"No," said the book-keeper. "I came to ask the price and I did not know the price and now I do. I came because the man who told me about the bay has been telling people about the bay for seven weeks and I have said nothing in a store for seven weeks and I would like it written down somewhere that I said nothing for seven weeks after I was asked a question I could have answered in four minutes."

The miller looked at him for a moment.

"Say it where," he said. "In a book?"

"In a book in a store," said Rennick Adley, "with the day on it, and in my own hand, and I will read it back to you tomorrow if you want it read back."

---

He was in the store from about the fifth hour. He had the lamp going by the sixth and the paper on the bench and the ninety-one-year page open on his knee, and the two of them together on about four feet of bench, which is the whole of the working surface of the Slade Cut Company and has been since his grandfather's time.

The paper is a good deal better than he had expected. It is written on both sides. It is the hand of a man who kept a mill's day book for thirty years, and the figures in it are the figures of a man who has weighed grain since he was thirteen. There is a line at the top with his name and the day and what he is. Then four hundred and forty foot of outer face, described in eleven lines, from the third-of-the-way mark to the eleven feet, and the eleven lines are good. Then the nine dates. Then the two he can prove and the seven he cannot, listed, and he has listed which is which, and he has listed the reason he cannot prove them, which is that he was looking at it and there was nobody else looking at it.

At the foot, in the same hand:

> *Sent to the district works of the Reed Cut, the sixth year of this district's numbering, and the second year after, and the third, and the fourth. Four times by hand to the works gate. Four papers returned. Nobody has ever come for it. A. Tace, of the mill yard, and I am one of the thirty-one households behind this bank and not a clerk and not a holder of anything and I do not know what I am.*

Rennick Adley read that last sentence about four times and then put his hand flat on the bench, which is a thing he does when he is about to write and has decided to.

He wrote a heading at the top of a fresh leaf, which the book has not had at the top of anything since his grandfather ruled one in about fifty-nine years ago and let it go out of use, and he wrote it in a hand that is not as good as his grandfather's:

> *The Slade Cut Company. What this book is, and what it holds. Written the sixth of the third month, R. Adley, keeper, and nobody else asked me to write it and nobody told me to.*

And under that, four lines.

> *What the Company holds, in the whole of its money, is four shillings a year and the shilling comes out of this book four times a year, and it is the rent of the south end of a stone building off the Slade at a shilling a quarter, and it has been that rent for eleven years and it was that rent before me.*
>
> *The Company holds, from the sixth of the third month and for a year, the north bay of the same building, which was not hired and is not rented and costs nothing, given by Aven Tace of the mill yard in his own doorway and without a word of writing, and it is to be entered as given and not as bought, and I am not going to enter the price as a figure because there is no figure and I am not going to enter it as a kindness because a kindness is a thing you cannot refuse.*
>
> *The Company holds a paper about the outer face at the third-of-the-way place, four hundred and forty foot, nine dates, the sixth year, written and sent four times and returned four times, given by Aven Tace of the mill yard, and the hand at the top of it is his and not ours and stays his, and the paper is kept here and the name at the top of it does not become ours by being in this book.*
>
> *The Company is not a person and cannot be compelled and cannot be instructed and cannot say no, and it has three things it cannot pay for and has had two of them for eleven years.*

Then he stopped, and read it back, and then he did the thing that was the fourth thing of the afternoon and the reason he had walked four miles.

He wrote a line in the margin, against the entry, the way a man writes a thing in a margin so that it is not part of the column.

> *Against this entry, and as a person and not as a crew and not as a debtor: **Aven Tace**, of the mill yard, of this Company and of this cut, who gave two things to it this day and is owed nothing and has asked for nothing and has been asked for nothing in six years.*

He looked at that for a while.

There is one other name in this book that is not the name of somebody who worked. It went in on the second of the third month, on the ninety-one-year page, because a man off the lower Cut with four children wanted to give it and Rennick Adley let him and wrote it where the man pointed. **That name is on a page. This one is against an entry, with a day on it, and it is a different act, and I have thought about the difference for a day and a half and here it is.** A name on a page is a thing somebody said. A name against an entry is a thing somebody can be asked about the entry.

He said that out loud, to the store, at about the seventh hour, because the store is the only place he has for saying things out loud.

"I am not going to start a column called *why not*, and I said that on the second of the third month and I am saying it again, and it is not a rule, it is that there is no room for it and nobody to read it. And I am not going to start a column called *who* either. I have said that twice now and I have meant it twice, and I am not going to be careful about what I mean.

"**A name in a margin is not a debt and it is not a claim and it is not a vote and it is not a party.** It is a name. And I want to be very plain about the rest of it, because I have been sitting here since the fifth hour and I am not going to write one word of what I am about to say down, and I would like somebody to be able to tell me afterwards that I did not.

"**It is findable.** That is all it is. A name in a book is the only thing anybody has ever wanted out of a book, and there is not one person alive who has ever come to a book to be told something and walked away again. So Tace's name is in this book, and he is findable from this day and about that bank, and the price of it is not what he thinks it is. The price of it is that **a man with a tray will be able to come to this store and ask about the bay, and about the paper, and about the north end of a stone building, and about a foreman's list, and about anything else in this book for the next eleven years, and there is no way in this empire to give a name back.**

"And I am not going to pretend I have decided that is a bad thing. **The honest thing to do about a body that cannot be compelled is to be easy to compel yourself.** If we were a person they could come and ask us this afternoon and I could say no. We are not a person, so nobody can ask us anything at all, and the only reason anybody in ninety-one households has ever heard a sentence out of this book is that I put one there.

"So it goes in. And if anybody ever comes with a tray I will read it out to them myself, out of this book, standing up, in daylight, the same way I would read out a list of works — because a name in a book should be worth exactly as much as a list of works and not one penny more, and if it is worth more than a list of works then somebody is going to work that out in about four years and use it."

---

He wrote the letter last, and it is in a hand of thirty-eight on a leaf cut off the back of a spoiled sheet, and it went down the road on the seventh morning with a man who was going to the district works gate anyway for his own reasons.

> *To the foreman of the Reed Cut district works, from the keeper of the book of the Slade Cut Company at the Slade end, the sixth of the third month.*
>
> *The Company has been in a court through the wrong form and I am not writing to you about that. I am writing to ask one question, and I would like it in writing if it can be in writing, and if it cannot I will take an answer in a room from anybody who is there and will not go to a magistrate about it.*
>
> *Can the district works put a person's name against the Slade? Not the Company's. A person's. And if they can, what is done to that person afterwards, and who asks about it, and how often.*
>
> *There is no money in this and I am not asking you for any. R. Adley.*

He sealed it with a stone off the bottom of the yard wall, because the Company has no seal, and the seal on the letter is the miller's, on a gatepost, and it is not the Company's and is not entered as the Company's.

---

The bay is thirty-one foot by nineteen and the door does not shut and there is a bench against the north wall, and the bay is not in this book as a thing anybody is entitled to after the ninth month of next year, and nothing has been resolved, and no instrument of any kind has been written by anybody in this matter, and no reading of any instrument has been performed by anybody in this matter.

What a body now has is a room, and a piece of paper four hundred and forty foot long with a miller's name at the top of it, and a name in a margin in a book that has had no name against an entry in sixty years.

And about four miles down-channel a foreman of the district works has a letter in his hand at about the seventh hour of the seventh day of the third month, and has read it, and has not answered it, and the answer is on a page in a book in his own office that nobody has ever asked to see, because it has got no heading over it.
