# Chapter 101: A Name at the Foot of It

The Slade Cut is four miles above the Reed Cut gate and it is a different cut, and the difference is not a matter of degree. The Reed Cut is a gate, which is a frame the water goes through, and it has a rail on it and a crew and a foreman and a platform. The Slade is a ditch with water in it, and the ditch runs about two miles along the top of a bank, and the bank is what goes.

The bank at the Slade is eleven feet high in the second month and about five feet by the third week of the first month, and there is a place about a third of the way along it where the outer face has come away twice in nine years and gone over nine inches both times, and a place near the far end where the inner face is fine and always has been. Nothing about that is a mystery. It is a bank and it is made of the district's own material and the outer face is the wet side.

What is a mystery, and it is a small one and it has been a small one for sixty years, is that nobody has ever written down what the bank is and who owes it.

There is a shed at the Slade end with a door that has a hasp on it and a nail in the wall inside, and there has been a nail in that wall since before Halla Wray was born, and on the nail there is a sheet of paper with a list on it. The list is about eleven lines long. It is the spring works. It is the same list every year, in the same order, in the same hand, because the same person has copied it off the same list for as long as anybody can remember, and the person who copied it last year is the person who copied it this year and the person who copied it in the year her mother did it.

The list has the crew's names on it. It has never had a name at the foot of it.

---

Halla Wray is fifty-one and she has been on that bank since she was nine, and she can tell you which nine feet of it will go and which will not by looking at it in the wrong light, and four people in this district would tell you the same and two of them would be right. She is the foreman of the Slade Cut, which is an office with no office in it: it is a thing nine families agreed to about eleven years ago because a man from the works came up with a crew and could not get anybody to stop working, and the agreement was not written down and has never been produced by anybody and could not be produced, and she holds it the way a person holds a job that nobody is going to ask her about.

Her brother is **Piotr Wray**, twenty-nine, and he works the far end of the cut and is the only person on that bank who has ever been in a position to say no to her about anything, and on the seventh day of the first month he asked her, at the shed, whether she was going to put the Company's name on the spring list, and she said that was not a question he had any standing to ask her, and he said he had asked it anyway.

The crew is five men and a boy. The day is eight pence for a man and five for a boy, and five men and a boy together come to forty-five pence a day. The work as it stands on the list is a fortnight. Forty-five pence for fourteen days is six hundred and thirty pence, which is two pounds twelve and sixpence, and that is the whole of it, and Halla Wray has never seen any of it, because the money is not hers: it comes out of a works fund under a head that says bank works, when there is anything in it, and the works clerk sends it up with the list and takes it back on the list when the work is done, and there has not been anything in that head since the third week of the twelfth month of last year.

---

What brought her to the shed on the seventh was the fourth week coming, and the way the fourth week comes is that the water comes up out of the flats about eleven inches a day for nine days and then goes down about four inches a day, and if the outer face at the third-of-the-way place is going to go it goes in the third week and not before, and if it is not done in the second week and the third week then it is done in the fourth week of the third month, and the fourth week of the third month is when the seed goes in.

The eleven inches of face that came away last time is about four hundred and forty feet of bank. Behind it there are nine acres and thirty-one households that have been on that ground for a very long time, and about sixty children under sixteen, and a good deal of it is early and a good deal of it is not, and the difference is what is at risk.

Sixty children is a figure and the figure is off the Company's book, and Halla Wray came at it by adding on the back of her own hand with a stick because she cannot read a paragraph and has never been able to, and she knows what her own arithmetic is worth and she told the man who asked her for it that the number was the number of a book and not the number of a district.

---

She got to the shed at about the sixth hour, and she took the list off the nail and read it, which she can do, and then she sat down on the upturned bucket that has been in that shed since her father's time and put the list on her knee.

The list says:

> *Slade. Spring bank. Second week to the third week.*
> *Cut, outer face, from the third-of-the-way mark to the eleven feet above it. Spoil on the low side and not on the towpath.*
> *Two days the digging and two days the facing and the rest of it the barrow.*
> *Five men and a boy.*
> *The kerb stones on the bed and not on the mud.*

That is the whole of it. There is no price on it. There is no memorandum number on it. There is no device on it. There is a man's name at the top of it from nineteen years back, in a hand that is not hers, because somebody in about her age once wrote a heading on it, and there is a man's name at the foot of that list, and there is nothing between the two but the work.

She read it twice and then she got up and turned the sheet over and looked at the back, which is blank, and she said out loud, to an empty shed, the thing that she had been carrying since the seventh of the ninth month, when the water office put a second sheet on a nail in another building and nobody went and read it for fourteen months.

Then she copied the list out again, on the front of a new sheet, in her own hand.

And at the foot of it she wrote her own name.

---

Piotr came in at about the seventh hour with the barrow handle under his arm, and he read the list, and he read it twice, and then he read the name, and then he put the barrow handle down against the wall.

"That is your name."

"That is my name."

"That is not the Company's name."

"That is exactly why it is there," said Halla Wray.

She said it standing up, with the list in her hand, and she had not planned to say anything, and she said the rest of it in four minutes, standing, in a shed, to one man, and eleven years of not having said it.

"Listen to this and then say back to me what I have said, because I have not said it before and I want to know whether I said it.

"A list of works is a list of jobs with days against them, and it is the only kind of paper in this empire that a person can read and understand and act on, and I know that is not my discovery, because there is a sheet nailed to a rail at the Reed Cut gate with a list of jobs on it and a foreman's name at the foot and nobody's name at the top, and that sheet has been on that rail for a year and a bit and nine families cross that gate every day of their lives and not one of them has stopped, and I have been on that bank since I was nine and I never went and read it, and neither did my mother, and neither did any of us, and that is not anybody's fault, that is how anything works.

"And here is the thing I have got in my hand. This sheet has got your name on it and Dyke's and Corrin's and old Searle's and Hask's and the boy's, and the boy's is Raff Ordway and he is sixteen and you can say that back to me if you want to check I have not lost it. It has got a man's name at the top of it in my aunt's hand, and it has never once had a name at the foot of it. Not once, in sixty years. And a list with nobody's name at the foot of it is a list that nobody has to answer for, and a list with a body at the foot of it is a list the body never read, and a list with a person's name at the foot of it is the only kind anybody in ninety-one households can point at and say *that was us and we were told*.

"That is the whole of it and I have been trying to say it to four men and a boy for ten years with a nail in a wall."

Piotr Wray picked the barrow handle up again and looked at the name at the foot of the list for a while.

"You have put your own name under it," he said. "Not the Company's. Yours."

"Mine."

"Then if you go down with the face, it is on you and not on us."

"Yes," said Halla Wray. "That is what a name at the foot of it is. **It is the only thing in this whole business that says anybody is in it.** And I have not been asked to do that and I have not been paid for doing that and nine families in this cut will now be able to say *Wray did it and Wray asked for it* instead of *it was done*, and I have known all morning that it is going to cost me and I am going to do it, and I would like it noted that nobody asked me."

Then she said the thing that the volume is about, and she said it to a shed and a nail and a man with a barrow handle, and she has said it since to nine people and it has not changed and she does not expect it to.

"And the other thing, and this is the other thing, and it is worse.

"The water office can send nine men and a boy for a fortnight in the autumn. They come up the river with a memorandum and they do the bank and they go back down and for the other eleven months of the year that office is nobody. So the spring bank has to be done by somebody, and it is done by us, and it has been done by us for sixty years, and nobody has ever made us do it and nobody has ever paid us for all of it and nobody has ever written down that it happened.

"And now a sheet has come up the river that wants something in a box, and I do not know yet what is in the box, and I am telling you now, this morning, before I know, that **I am not going to put a household in a box.** Not a house of Wray, not a house of anybody, not a house of nineteen families who are not even in this cut. And I am not going to put the Company in one either, and I want that said as loudly as the other, because everybody thinks the danger is the family and the danger is the company.

"**A company is worse. A company is worse because a company cannot be asked and a company cannot say no and a company lasts longer than me.** A household lasts until the last of you is dead, and after that there is nobody holding anything and the work stops, and that is bad and it is survivable. A company goes on after every single one of us and it does not know it is doing it and there is nobody to go and say *stop*, and everything that is put on a company ends up on the children, because the children are the only people in it who did not agree to anything and cannot get out.

"So the two things I will do are these. I will keep the book. And I will put a person's name at the foot of a list of works. And if the water office wants somebody to hold this work, then that somebody has to be a person who can be asked questions, and there are ninety-one of us, and I am one, and **this Company is not going to choose which one.**"

Piotr Wray said the thing back to her. He got the part about the family right and he got the part about the company right and he got the ninety-one right, and he got the bit about the children out of order, which she let him, because he is twenty-nine and it is a hard sentence.

Then he said: "You have not answered whether I can have the work in my own name."

"You can have the work in your own name," said Halla Wray, "and you will not have the bank in your name, and there is a difference and I have been trying to explain the difference for four minutes and I am going to stop now and go and put a man's name at the foot of a list."

---

She took the list to the weigh-house at the Slade end that evening because that is where the money is counted when there is money, and the weigh-master is a woman of about fifty-eight who has weighed about nine hundred loads of wet earth and does not care what a list says as long as it has the right number of men on it, and the right number of men on it is five and one.

"Two names."

"Two names," said the weigh-master. "I have been putting *the Company* on that sheet for nine years and you are the first person in nine years to have handed me anything else, and I have got to be honest with you, I am going to have to write down which of the two it is, because if I write *the Company* then in nine years somebody is going to come and ask me what that is and I will not know, and if I write two names then in nine years somebody is going to come and ask me why, and that is a better question to be asked."

"Write the two names," said Halla Wray, "and write the day, and write at the bottom of it that the money is not to be paid to the Company and is to be paid to the men on the list, and write that anybody can come and read that line."

"There is a person coming up the river."

"There is a man coming up the river from the city four hundred and thirty miles away and he has been in a file for fourteen months and he does not know what a list of works is and I would not take his advice on a shed," said Halla Wray. "Write the line."

The weigh-master wrote the line, and the line is the only line in the whole of this matter that anybody has ever written about a public work at a weigh-house on the Slade, and it reads:

> *This is a list of works. The work is the spring bank at the Slade. The two names above are the two persons the district's flood sheet names. The money is to be paid to the men on this list and not to any body, and the Company is not to be paid. Any person may come and read this line. — H. Wray, foreman, the seventh of the first month.*

And the boy whose name went into the box at the water office is the boy on that list, and he is sixteen, and his mother has four children and a house with a stone floor, and nobody has asked him anything and he is not on this page as anything except a name on a list twice.

---

There are about nine families on that bank and forty-odd people in a mile of it, and the bank is eleven feet high and four hundred and forty feet of the outer face is the part that matters, and it went nine inches in nine years twice, and a list of works in a shed on a bank is now the fourth document in ninety-one years that somebody on that bank could take hold of, and there are no other three within nine miles.

The girl of eleven who came to the rail at the Reed Cut gate in the last month of last year asked a question that nobody answered, and the question was *and who is it that reads it to the ones who cannot read it*, and about four hundred and thirty miles downstream a sheet went up the river on the ninth of this month with two names in a box and four lines on its face and no body on it anywhere, and **the box was a thing a person could be asked about, and the reading of it had not been settled, and the whole of what came down the river next was the argument about the box.**

A shed has a door with a hasp on it and a nail in the wall and a list on the nail, and the list has a foreman's name at the foot of it now, and the nail is the same nail.
