# Chapter 103: Nine Hands

The grain store at the Slade end is a stone building with a wooden floor that has been raised off the ground by about a foot, which is the only thing that makes a grain store worth having, and it is hired by the year from a miller who does not use it, because his mill draws from up-channel and this store is on the wrong side of a ford.

The rent is a shilling a quarter. The key has been in the same family for eleven years and it is Rennick Adley's mother who holds it, not him, and she is sixty-eight and she cannot get down the two steps with a sack and has never been asked to and has not offered.

There are about nine hundred bushels in there in a good year and a great deal less in a bad one, and there is a bench along one wall that is not a bench and is not for sitting on, and on the bench along that wall there is a book.

The book is about two inches thick and it is not bound in anything anybody would pay for, and it has been in this store for about sixty years, and it has no heading at the top of it.

That is the first thing to say about it and it is the thing that a person notices last.

---

Rennick Adley is thirty-eight. He has kept the book for six years. Before him his aunt kept it for nineteen, and before her it was kept by a man on the far end of the cut who could not write at all, and his wife wrote it for him, and there is a page in the middle of the book where her hand changes twice in the same year because she ran out of room, and before those there were four other people and the book does not say who they were, and it does not say because there was nowhere on a page to say.

He is not a clerk. He is a man who rents a cart when a cart is wanted and who can count and who has a fair hand and who is the only person on that bank, apart from four others, who can read a paragraph.

On the thirteenth day of the first month a sheet came up the river from the Reed Cut and the weigh-master brought it to the store in a cart with eleven bushels in it, because the weigh-master brings things to the store and takes things from it and it is the shortest way to do anything, and she put it on the bench and she said, "You are the one who keeps it," and went.

He read it. It took him four minutes, including the four lines, and then he read it again, and then he went and got the book off the bench and put it on the cart and read the whole of it, which takes about two hours because there is a page to a year and there are sixty pages and about half of them are lists and half of them are nothing much.

---

Here is a page. It is the fourth month of the eleventh year of the district's reckoning and it is a list of works and it has the crew's names against the days and it has a name at the top of it in a hand of about fifty and it has nothing at the foot of it.

> *Slade. Spring bank, the fourth to the sixteenth.*
> *Dyke, four days, at eight pence.*
> *Corrin, four days, at eight pence.*
> *Searle, four days, at eight pence, and two days at the barrow.*
> *Hask, nine days, at eight pence.*
> *Raff Ordway, nine days, at five pence.*
> *Spit and barrow, the second week.*

That is a page. There are about four hundred like it in that book and every one of them ends at a name and none of them begins at a person who is responsible.

The page is not signed. The six years he has kept it are the six years he has put his own name at the top of a page as *kept* and not as *answerable*, and before him his aunt had done the same for nineteen, and before her the man who could not write had put nothing at all, and before him the four other people had put nothing at all.

The point of the book, and he worked it out on the fourteenth, is that it is the only record anybody has.

And it is more than that, and he spent the afternoon of the fourteenth finding out how much more, and it is the reason a woman of sixty-eight handed a key to a man of thirty-eight instead of a chest to a solicitor.

He took a page at random out of the middle — the eleventh year, the autumn, the reed cutting — and read four of the names off it, and then went down the lane and asked about those four, and came back in about an hour with three answers out of four.

Dyke is alive and is at the far end of the cut and is sixty-eight and was the man who could not write and whose wife wrote the page. Corrin is dead, four years, and there is a son on the bank. Searle is dead, nine years, and there is a daughter in the fourth house past the mill. And the fourth name — a man called Quillon, on the page as a barrow man in the ninth year, who is not there now and has not been for about six years, and whose people are in a village nine miles up-channel that has not changed its name in that time, and the clerk there would know.

**Four names, one hour, and three answers, and the fourth was findable with an address.** That is what a book is. It is not a truth and it is not a claim and it is not a court record. It is a list of people, and a list of people is the only kind of list anybody in this empire can act on, and the district's own book has a body in the place where that should be, and the Company's book has ninety-one households in it, and neither of those can be turned into a man at a door in about an hour with a shilling spent.

He came back to the store and wrote that down in the book, in the margin, in pencil, and then read it and did not leave it, and the reason he did not leave it is that a book of names that has been checked against the world and found to be four names out of four is a book anybody can rely on, and a book that has not is a book of claims.

He also worked out the other half of it in the same hour, standing by the door, and the half is this: **that every one of those four people was findable because somebody wrote the name down while the person was still alive to be written about, and that if the district's book had been kept the way his book is kept, the district would have known in four minutes who it ought to write to, and instead it writes to a parish that was dissolved, and that has been the whole of the method of this district's harm for about two hundred years and nobody ever called it harm because nobody ever kept the other book to compare it with.**

Not a court record. He has gone and looked. The district keeps a book of works at the works office, four miles down-channel, and it has entries for the Slade in nine years out of the last nine, and they are entries of *crews sent* and *works done*, and the crew is a body, and the works done is a list, and there is no person in the district's book for the Slade Cut and there has not been one since the district was last mapped, and the district has been mapped twice since he was born.

The Company's book has no person in it either, because the Company is ninety-one households and a household is a body, and a body is the shape a box wants.

His book has people in it. It has had people in it for sixty years, and about nine hundred and forty different people have been written in it, most of them more than once, and it has never once had a name at the foot of anything.

And the people in it are findable, because a name is a name and a man who writes a name down can be asked about it, and that is the entire difference between the Slade Cut Company's book and every other record of a public work in that district, and nobody has ever said it out loud because nobody has ever been asked.

---

The ninety-one-year page is the twelfth page and he had read it before and read it again on the fourteenth, in the store, by a window that does not shut, and it is a different kind of entry from the other four hundred and it is the reason the book exists.

> *The Slade. The eleventh day of the second month, ninety-one years.*
>
> *The water of the Slade, and the eleven householders named below, and the works below, and the works are to be kept by the water on the terms in the instrument, and the terms are in the instrument and not here, and a copy of the instrument is with the clerk of the district and not here either.*
>
> *One. The outer face, from the mark to the eleven feet.*
> *Two. The inner face, the same.*
> *Three. The kerb, from the third stone to the ninth stone.*
> *Four. The gate at the lower end, and its linkage, set to a maker's gauge and not to the hand.*
> *Five. The ditch on the north side, cut to level, spoil on the low side.*
> *Six. The plank at the mill end, and the chain, and the lock.*
> *Seven. The ford, and the stones, and the two posts.*
> *Eight. The by-road side, the ditch, and the culvert, and the grating on it.*
> *Nine. The upper catch, and the grating, and the two bars.*
> *Ten. The scour at the third of the way place, and the stones out of it.*
> *Eleven. The bank at the mill end, and the coping, and the coping is to be set on the bed and not on the mud.*
>
> *Given at the Slade, in the eleventh day of the second month, ninety-one years.*
>
> *— Ander Fellis, of the Slade, reeve.*
> *— Iven Marsk, of the Slade.*
> *— Osa Trill, of the Slade.*
> *— Houl Adder, of the Slade.*
> *— Wyn Pether, of the Slade.*
> *— Cat Bellow, of the Slade.*
> *— Merrow Slee, of the Slade.*
> *— Jent Lask, of the Slade.*
> *— Dace Olliver, of the Slade.*
> *— Rill Cauld, of the Slade.*
> *— Tarn Skell, of the Slade.*

Eleven names. Eleven works. And a sentence in the middle of it that Rennick Adley read four times on the fourteenth of the first month and then had to go and stand outside for a while.

*And the terms are in the instrument and not here, and a copy of the instrument is with the clerk of the district and not here either.*

Ninety-one years. The book has been in this store for sixty. The district was mapped twice since he was born and the parish of the Slade was dissolved in the sixth year of this century, or the century before, and the notices went to the parish, and **a notice addressed to a parish that has been dissolved is a notice about nobody, and a notice about nobody cannot be produced against a person and cannot bind a person and cannot be asked anything by anybody**, and there is not one person alive on that bank who remembers a household of ninety-one years back, and there never will be again after this winter, because the oldest person on that bank is eighty-one and she was not born.

He wrote the eleven names out on a slip of paper and took it to the door and held it up to the light and could read ten of the eleven and could not be sure of the sixth, which is either *Cat Bellow* or *Cat Bellou*, and a name that cannot be read is a name that cannot be checked, and a name that cannot be checked is a name that cannot be found.

---

Doreth Ferrow came down to the store at about the fourth hour on the fifteenth to look at the book before she called the meeting, which she had decided to do on the twentieth of the first month at about the seventh hour, in the mill yard, because the kitchen would not hold what was going to come to it.

She is sixty-four and has kept the meeting for nineteen years and has never once been asked to keep it, and she came down the two steps with a stick and looked at the book on the bench for a while without touching it, and then she said:

"Will you bring it on the twentieth."

"No," said Rennick Adley.

"Then I will ask you again on the twentieth in front of forty people, and you will say no again, and that will be the end of it."

"You can do that."

She sat down on the grain sacks, which she should not have done, and looked at the ninety-one-year page, and did not read it, because she cannot read a paragraph either and has not pretended to for fifty years, and she said:

"Ninety-one years."

"Ninety-one years," said Rennick Adley. "And there is not one of those eleven names that anybody can be asked about, and that is not because they were unlucky. That is because a name in a document is not a door unless somebody alive can be asked about it, and there is nobody."

"Read me the line in the middle."

He read it to her, twice, and she made him read it a third time, and then she was quiet for about a minute and a half.

"Then we have never known what we owe," she said.

"We have never known what we owe," said Rennick Adley, "and we have been doing it anyway, and that is not a defence of anything, it is just what happened."

---

Then he said the thing that he had been carrying since the fourteenth, and he said it to a woman of sixty-four sitting on grain sacks in a store that is not a room.

"You know what I have just told you. That I have been keeping this book for six years and my aunt kept it for nineteen and a man who could not write kept it before her, and not one of us was asked. Nobody asked me. My aunt told me in a kitchen, in a sentence, in the first week of the sixth year, and she did not say *will you* and she did not say *you may stop*, and I have not thought about it for six years, and I have thought about it every day since the fourteenth of this month.

"That is the whole of it and I want it said in a room while there is a person in the room. **I have been holding a duty by inheritance without being asked, and that is the exact thing this business is about, and I am a person, and my aunt is a person, and the man who could not write was a person, and we have all done it, and the only difference between us and the sixty households on this bank is that we kept a book about it.**"

"And is it the same thing," said Doreth Ferrow.

"It is the same thing with a book," said Rennick Adley. "That is what a book is. A book does not make it different. It makes it *findable*, and that is all, and I would rather it was findable than not, and I am not going to pretend that being findable is a kind of innocence, because a person who is findable is a person who can be asked, and I have never in six years been asked about this book and I have thought about it every day."

---

He wrote it out that evening in the book, on the first leaf at the front, which was blank, in his own hand, with the day on it, and he read it back twice and he did not cross anything out.

> *The fifteenth of the first month. This book. Written by me, R. Adley, who has kept it six years and was not asked to keep it and was told about it in a sentence by my aunt in the first week of the sixth year and was not given any way of stopping.*
>
> *What this book is. A list of who did what on the bank at the Slade, and when, kept by a person, since about sixty years. The page to a year and the year to a hand. There are about nine hundred and forty names in it and most of them are written more than once.*
>
> *What this book is not. It is not a court record. It is not an instrument. It is not a claim on anybody. It is not office property and it does not belong to the Company and the Company cannot have it and does not know that it exists. It cannot be produced against a person. It cannot be given to a person. There is no office in this district that could order me to produce it and there is one in a city four hundred and thirty miles away that would not know to ask.*
>
> *And there is one page in it from ninety-one years ago with eleven names at the foot of it, and it is the only page in ninety-one years with names at the foot of it, and there is nobody alive that any of the eleven can be asked about, and the sixth name cannot be read with certainty, and a name that cannot be read is a name that cannot be found.*
>
> *And the terms are in the instrument and not in this book, and the instrument is with a notary's table in a city four hundred and thirty miles down the river from this store, and I do not know that for a fact, I know it because the page says a copy is with the clerk of the district and the clerk of the district was dissolved in the sixth year and the notice went to the parish.*
>
> *I have ruled a line under the six years I have kept it, because I was not asked to keep it and my aunt was not asked to keep it and the man before her could not write and nobody wrote that down either.*
>
> *— R. Adley, the fifteenth of the first month.*

And then he ruled a line under it, in ink, right across the page, from the left margin to the right, and the line went through the words *six years I have kept it* and through the date, and he did not put his name under the line, and that was on purpose, and the reason it was on purpose is that in nine years a person will turn to that line to find out whether anybody has taken this on, and if there is a name under the line then the question is answered and the question is the only thing worth keeping.

A book with a line in it and no name under the line is a book that says *nobody has taken this on yet*, and that is a different sentence from *nobody will ever*, and it is the best sentence anybody in that store has got.

The miller came in on the sixteenth about the rent and asked whether the store wanted the north bay as well, and Rennick Adley said no, and then said yes, and then said he would have to ask somebody, and then did not say anything else about it for about eleven minutes, which is the longest he has ever thought about a bay in his life.
