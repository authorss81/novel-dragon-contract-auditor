# Chapter 132: The Free Door

The fourth counter of the Notaries' Table in Auremar is at the end of a passage, past the estates counter and past a door that has been propped open with a quill since somebody's time, and the counter itself is a slab of oak with a rail on it and a fee board on the wall behind, and the fee board has four things on it and none of them is time.

**Marn Ottery is thirty-four.** She came to the fourth counter from the second, where estates are, eleven years ago, and she has never once been a lawyer and has never wanted to be one, and she knows more about scale instruments than anybody who has ever been a lawyer at that counter, and none of that is on the board.

The fee board says: *Question, one shilling. Copy, fourpence. Certificate, four shillings. Attendance, by arrangement.*

A blank form is a shilling whether it is filled in or not. A copy is four pence. An answer is about eleven weeks.

The courier came in on the afternoon of the twenty-first of the fifth month and put a sheet on the counter, and it had come four hundred and thirty miles up a river and it had been to a water office in between, and it was warm.

"This is not a question," said Marn Ottery. "This is a refusal with a question on the back of it."

"It came up the road this morning."

"You have read it."

"I cannot read it."

She turned it round. There was one line on it in a dragon's hand and four lines under it in the same hand and a stamp.

> *To the Notaries' Table. From a notary's table of a House, being a water-notary practice.*
>
> *A water guarantee sealed under the fire-and-water device has been offered to an unincorporated company working a public cut in the district named at the foot, and the Company has not answered the fourth line within the time the instrument allows.*
>
> *We are asked whether we will certify the instrument, and we are asked first to be satisfied of one fact, namely who is of the Company.*
>
> *The Company is not on the register of bodies. There is no head of it. Our enquiry has established that no court may make it a party.*

Marn Ottery read that four times and then went and got the register of concessions out from behind the counter, which is a fat book on a high shelf that she has to be given a chair for, and she carried it across and put it on the slab and opened it at a page about ninety years back, which is the oldest water instrument on that river that anybody alive has read.

"Do you want a receipt for this?"

"It came to be answered."

"A receipt is fourpence and it is for a copy and there is nothing to copy until somebody has said something. **A question is a shilling and you have brought me neither, and I am going to answer it anyway, and I want that written down somewhere, because in eleven years nobody has ever walked up to that counter without a shilling in the hand and it has made me the only door in this building and I have never once minded until about a fortnight ago.**"

---

The answer took her about nine minutes and she had it ready in about four.

She wrote it on the back of a blank form, which she was not supposed to do, and then she did it properly, which was to write it out longhand on the back of a blank form, in her own hand, with the day at the top, and sign it, and say out loud that it was a clerk's own note and not a certificate and that nobody might rely on it.

> *What a notary's table may certify. One: what an instrument is, and the day and year it was made, and what it is called. Two: what a book is, and what is written in it. Three: what a party is. Four: what a document does.*
>
> *What a notary's table may not certify. That a person ought to do a thing. Who ought to answer for a thing. What a body is made of.*
>
> *The fourth line of the form at the foot of a scale instrument asks in what way the instrument touches you. It asks about one person. It has always asked about one person. **It is not a form about a body and it is not a form about a household and it was never intended to be either, and it has never had to be, because in the hundred and forty years of this table no body has ever been able to put a person in front of it.***

Then she stopped, and read that last sentence again, and put her pen down, and picked it up again.

"**That is the finding and it is mine and it took me about four seconds and I have been standing at this counter for eleven years.**"

"Will a table answer it?"

"About eleven weeks, and there is a shilling, and that is if a person asks it. **A body cannot ask it. I am aware that I have just written a sentence that says a body cannot ask it, and I have been one of the people who put that in a book, in a different building, four hundred miles up a river, in the second month of this year, with a name and a date on it, and it took a woman of sixty-four nine minutes to say it to a room of about thirty people, and it is on a bench in a hired bay with mud on it.**"

"You want me to tell them that?"

"I want you to take that paper back up the road and say, in your own words, that this table cannot be asked by a body, and that I do not know who to send the answer to, and that I am not going to find out for a shilling I have not been given."

That was the end of the courier. He went down the passage past the estates counter and out into the afternoon, and she watched him go as far as the propped door, and then she went and got the register of concessions off the high shelf.

---

There is a box under the counter.

It is about two feet by one and it is full of blanks, and it holds about four hundred of them, and it has held blanks in that box for longer than she has been at the counter, and the clerk who was here before her used to keep it in date order and the clerk before him kept it in no order at all and the one before that tied them in bundles of fifty.

Nobody keeps it. It is a box of paper that people bought at a shilling and either filled in or did not, and a person who filled one in took it away, and a person who did not put it in the box, and about four hundred years of that building's small commerce is in there.

She got the box up onto the counter at about the fourth hour and started on it, and it took her until the sixth hour of the next day, and she did it on her own time and told nobody and was not paid for any of it.

There are about four hundred blanks in that box and the fourth line is ruled on every one of them, and she looked at every one of them.

**Two hundred and sixty-one of the fourth lines are empty.**

That is not the finding. That is a thing about people who buy a form and do not fill it in, and about four of those are in this box because a person bought a form once to be angry with, which she has done herself, in the second counter, in about the ninth year.

The finding is in the rest.

**A hundred and forty of the four hundred have a fourth line filled in.** She wrote the number out twice and looked at it for a while and then she went through all a hundred and forty and read the name, or the description, or whatever was in the box at the top.

And in a hundred and twenty-six of them there is a person's name, and it is a person, and the box is filled in, and the form went away to be answered.

**In fourteen of them, the box at the top does not say a person's name.**

She wrote the fourteen out on a sheet of her own and she wrote beside each of them what was in the box instead, and this is what was in them.

A guild. A combination. A trade. A chapel. A poor-box. A company of watermen. **An unincorporated company, twice.** A body described as the body. A body described as all persons having the use of a thing. A body that had put the word *all* in.

And in eleven of the fourteen, at the foot of the form, underneath the fourth line, in a different hand, there is a person's name and a date.

**Not in the box. Underneath it, where a person signs.**

"So the form was not filled in by a body," said Marn Ottery, to an empty counter, at about the sixth hour of the next day, with a sheet of paper on it with fourteen lines on it. "**It was filled in by a person who was careful and wrote the body in the box, and then a person signed underneath and answered for it, in a different hand, and nobody at this counter ever asked whether that person had been asked.**"

She laid it out on the slab with the two shilling pieces beside it so it would not blow.

"**That is the whole of it and it has been in a box under this counter for about a hundred and forty years and it is the only finding I have ever made at work that nobody wanted.**"

And then she sat there for about nine minutes and did not go and do anything else, which is not how she works.

"Because it was asked the wrong way," she said, at last, to the empty counter. "**They asked me for the third thing. Who is of a body. And I said no, and I was right, and I was proud of myself for about nine seconds.**

"The list on that board has four things on it. What an instrument is. **What a book is, and what is written in it.** What a party is. What a document does.

"The first, third and fourth are the same question asked three ways and all three of them are about documents. **The second one is the one that is not.** And a House does not need me to certify who is of ninety-one households. A House needs a thing in front of a court that says the people in ninety-one households are the people in ninety-one households, and it does not need me to say it. **It needs a seal on a true sentence, and a true sentence about a book is on my board at item two, and I would be saying it in my own hand and it would be true.**"

She wrote a sentence out on the back of a blank and looked at it for a while.

> *The book produced is a true record of the members of the said Company, being the persons entered in it.*

"**That is a lie about a cut and it is not a lie about a book and I could put a seal on it tomorrow.** Nine hundred and forty names in about sixty years, nine hands, no heading at the top of it, no list of persons in it, about four hundred of the nine hundred and forty people dead, and the count of about ninety-one households and about sixty children under sixteen added on the back of a hand by a person who cannot read a paragraph. That book is a true record of the members of that Company. **Every word of that sentence is correct and I would have to swear to it and I would be able to.**

"And a seal on it does not say *these are the members.* A seal says the paper and the ground are the same thing. **So the seal says the ground is the book, and after that there is no difference left to argue about, and the difference was the only thing anybody had.**"

She put the pen down and then picked it up and added one more line, and it is the line that is at the foot of the sheet that went up the road.

"**A table may refuse a question and be entirely right, and a party may put the same question differently and be served inside four days, and the only defence anybody in this Empire has against that is a person who is in the room when the second version is written. There is no form for that. There is a person for that.**"

---

Here is what she did not write on the back of the blank form and would not have written on the front of one either, and she thought about it for the rest of the afternoon and then about four days more.

The fee board behind her had four things on it and none of them was time, and every one of the four was a price a person could see before they committed themselves, and she had been proud of that board for eleven years the way you are proud of a thing you have never had tested.

A shilling is a shilling and everybody objects to a shilling. The four pence for a copy is the same. **A person who can pay can find out what she is buying, and a person who cannot pay can find out nothing, and the whole of the design of that counter is that the price is on the wall and the price is the same for everybody and anybody may see the price before deciding.**

And the fourth line was free.

**Not fourpence. Not a shilling. Free. It is the only line on that form with no charge against it anywhere in this building, and it is the one that changes what a person is, and it is free because nobody has ever had to charge for it, because nobody has ever been in a position to be charged.**

She got out a sheet of foolscap and wrote it all out again on its own, the nine lines and the fourteen and the two hundred and sixty-one, and dated it, and signed it, and then she sat looking at it for about four minutes and decided she was not going to put it in the file.

**Not because it was wrong. Because a file is a place a thing goes to be looked up, and if the fourth line is free and nobody is charged for it, then the record of that is a thing that ought to be in a place where about nine people can find it and not in a press behind a counter that one clerk happens to be sitting at.**

So she did the thing she has done eleven years for, which was to write a thing out neatly and give it to a person, and she gave it to a carrier on the sixth of the sixth month with a note in her hand on the front of it, and the note said four words, which were: **About the fourth line.**

She paid the carrier herself, which was four pence, and she did not put it in the fee book, because there is no head on that board for it and the only honest place for it was the column of a clerk's own spending that she kept on the back of the counter and never showed anybody.

And at about the fourth hour of that afternoon a woman of twenty-four came down the passage from the third gallery with a bundle of proofs under her arm and stopped at the counter, and said that she was told the fourth counter had written something out about the fourth line.

"Yes," said Marn Ottery. "It is two sentences and it is the only thing I have written in eleven years that I have not put in a file."

"May I read it?"

"You may. **And you will tell me, in this room, standing up, whether it is true, because you are the only person I have met in this building who has read a fourth line in a case where a person could not fill it in, and if it is not true I want to know that in about nine seconds and not in four years.**"

It was true. It took her about eleven seconds to say so, and then she said the other thing, which is not in the note and was said out loud.

"**A person who turns up is never paid and cannot be, and you know that better than anybody in this building and you have written it on a wall in a different district in the second month. And I have just found out the same fact about a form. It is the same fact. A thing nobody is charged for is a thing nobody thinks about, and a thing nobody thinks about is the thing that takes a child into a guarantee in a bay four hundred and thirty miles from here.**"

"And the second thing you have found out."

"That a clerk can be right about a question and be the reason the question gets asked again in a form that works." She squared the two shilling pieces on the slab. "**I have been the door for eleven years and I have never once asked what the door was for, and it took a boy of fourteen who cannot read and a woman of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph and a book-keeper who could read perfectly well to work out what it was for, and none of the three of them can read what I have written here, and all three of them are now on the paper.**"

And the copy went up a towpath in a hand and not in an envelope, at four pence, to a book-keeper of thirty-eight in a hired stone store, and a woman of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph had it read to her, and the woman of sixty-four said that the word *free* had been in that bay twice in a month in two entirely different mouths and that she did not like either of them, and could not say why, and would like somebody to find out.
