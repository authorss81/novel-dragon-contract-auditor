# Chapter 143: The Day a Chair Fixed

The north bay of a miller's grain store off the Slade is thirty-one foot by nineteen with the floor a foot off the ground, and about thirty people fit in it if a good many of them are on the benches against the wall, and about thirty people were in it at about the seventh hour of the day a chair fixed, and the door in the west end was doing what it does.

**Doreth Ferrow has kept this meeting for nineteen years and she is sixty-four and she cannot read a paragraph, and she has not pretended to for fifty years, and she has about sixty children under sixteen in that cut and has met the two youngest of them, and she can add and she can count a room and she does not need a slate to do either and everybody in that bay has known that for nineteen years.**

She fixed this day on the twenty-ninth of the sixth month, in that bay, standing at the west end with her hands behind her back, which is where she puts them, and she said it in nine words and about eleven people heard it and one of them wrote it down.

She said: **We are going to ask this Company whether it will take a duty that stops.**

And then she said the part that matters and that she had not said on the twenty-ninth and has thought about every day since, which is that a chair fixes a day and does not consult the room, and that in nineteen years that has been a virtue and that on this occasion it is going to cost about thirty people a morning.

---

Here is what she did, and it is why about nine people in that bay were not disappointed at about the ninth hour, which is the only review of this meeting anybody has ever got.

**She said the finding first. Out loud. Before anybody had spoken. Before the bench had been cleared and before the slate was out.**

"You have all come here on a morning because I said a thing on the last day of the last month and I did not say why I said it, and I am going to say why first and then I am going to ask the question, and the reason I am going to say the why first is that if I do it the other way about nine of you will sit there for an hour waiting to be told that a company cannot be asked, and you have all known it since the ninth of the second month and I have said it out loud in this room twice."

Nobody said anything. The door did what it does.

"**A company cannot say no.**

"That is the whole of it. Not cannot be asked — it can be written to, it is written to, there is a paper in this room with a seal on it about four hundred and thirty miles away that came to a yard in the fifth month and is still standing offered and unanswered in that yard. It can be told what to do. It is being told what to do. It is on a schedule in a city at this moment and every future flood on that cut is guaranteed by nobody asking twice.

"**What it cannot do is say no.** And a body that is asked a thing and cannot say no has accepted it, and that is not my opinion and it is not a lawyer's, it is what a notary of a House wrote down in his own hand and sealed on the twenty-sixth of the last month and it is in that book in a room about two hundred miles from here and I have had it read out to me twice.

"So I fixed a day to ask this Company whether it will take a duty that stops, **and a Company cannot be asked, and I have been a chair for nineteen years and it took me until about the eleventh of this month to work out which of two things I was doing on the twenty-ninth.**"

---

Somebody at the back said, "Then what was the point of the day."

"**There was no point of the day.**"

"Then why—"

"**Because I wanted to hear the word no out of a mouth in my own room.**" She had the back of her own hand. "That is the reason and it is not a good one and I am sixty-four and I have said in this room that I do not want to learn things at sixty-four, and I fixed a day anyway, and if you want to be angry with somebody be angry with me and not with the miller, who has been standing in a doorway for a month about a letter of his own."

Aven Tace, who was about fifty and who had said on the eleventh of the fifth month in that bay that he was the door to this thing and had been since the ninth, said, "I do not mind."

"Then be quiet, you have been a door for a month and it has not made you interesting."

Somebody laughed, and it stopped short.

---

Then she read the question, off the back of her own hand, and she had it in about nine words and she read it out twice and the second time was slower, and about nine people in that room could not read a word of anything and were spoken to by a person sitting next to them, which is the rule of that bay and has been since the twenty-eighth of the third month.

> *One person. Not a household. Not a company.*
> *Forty-five pence a day.*
> *One season — about ninety days.*
> *A day to give it up, one day's notice, in your own hand, in a book that has no heading.*
> *Your name at the foot of a list, and a day against the name.*

She read it twice, and then she said, "**And I am going to add a sixth line and I am the chair and nobody can stop me and it is on the back of my hand and Rennick is writing it down.**

> *And a person who says no is not asked again by this meeting.*

"That is not in the question anybody handed me. **That is mine, and I have put it in, and if somebody in this room wants to take the sixth line off I will take it off and I will not argue, and I will not ask for the question again either.**"

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds.

"Do not take it off," said a woman of about forty-four at the back. "**You have never once said that in nineteen years and I have wanted to hear it said and I did not know that is what I wanted.**"

Then somebody said, "**The chair of this meeting in a bay cannot give anybody anything. What is she just given us.**"

"**I have been a chair for nineteen years and I have given this room a bay with a stove in one end, and about nine words, and a rule that everything is read out twice.** I have not given anybody anything and I am not starting in the seventh month of my life." She put her hand down. "**The sixth line is not a gift either. It is the only thing I have got and I am telling you what it is: it is a chair saying out loud that she will not come back. That is not power. It is the opposite of power and it is all I have.**"

---

Then she asked it, and she asked it the way the bay asks things, which is one question at a time and about thirty people and no hands.

"**Will you take the bank for a season. Yes or no or nothing.**"

And then, which is the part that is on the page and that nobody in that bay had thought of and that about nine of them are still thinking about in the ninth month:

"**And it is the same question at every door. That is the whole of it. Rennick walked up the lane on the seventh of this month and asked three people the identical question in the identical words and I want everybody to know that, because the last four months I have said in this room that a name in a book is a chain, and this week a man went and asked three people a question in a doorway and each of them answered for herself and nobody answered for ninety-one households, and that is the first time anybody in this matter has done the thing I have been asking for since the second month.**"

And Rennick Adley, who was thirty-eight and who had gone up that lane and come back with three answers and two noes, stood at the far end with his arms folded and did not say anything, and she told him afterwards that she had looked at him and he had known she was looking at him and had let her.

---

It took an hour and about forty minutes and the count was done on the back of a hand in chalk and then read back out loud and then wiped off and put in a book, which is what they do with everything.

**Eleven said yes.**

Nine of them had to be told the second time what a season was, because a season is not a word anybody in that bay had been using, and a man of about thirty-eight explained it with a day rate and a figure and nothing else, and a woman of about thirty-one at the first house at the north of the ford was not in the room and her yes is in a book with a day on it and the chair read it out twice in her absence and said so.

**Four said no.** Two of them were in the room. One of those was the woman of about forty-four at the third house, who had said no in a doorway on the seventh of the month and said no again in the bay and gave the same reason and said she was not going to give a third reason to anybody in this matter.

The other two were not in the room and the chair said why, and read their entries out twice, and the second reading was slower.

> *The fourth house at the north of the ford, a person of about seventy-one who cannot read a paragraph, who cannot stand in a room with about thirty people in it for nine minutes, who walked four miles of towpath in about two hours each way in the fourth month on the advice of nobody, and who has asked in writing to be asked at her own door four times a year: no. The same reason as in the sixth month, said twice, and she says she is not going to say it a third time, and asks that the chair reads this to the room twice.*

"I am reading it twice," said Doreth Ferrow, and read it twice.

**Nine people asked what happens after** — after the season, after the day, after you give it up — and were answered, and the answer was about four sentences long and it was: **that is what this meeting has not got.**

And the rest said nothing. About thirty in the room, eleven yes, four no, nine asking, and the rest not saying anything, and the chair counted the nothing separately, which nobody in that bay had ever thought to do, and which is the only new arithmetic of the day.

---

Then a man of about thirty-one in a bay coat at the back, who had come to that bay four times since the fourth month and had said nine words in it in the last week of the sixth month that had frightened a chair of sixty-four more than a dragon losing a hundred and forty years, asked the question nobody had thought of.

"**What is in it for the next one.**"

"I have not got an answer to that," said Doreth Ferrow.

"**Then that is the question.**" He had not moved. "**You have been asking everybody in this room whether they will hold a thing. Not one person in this room has been asked who holds it after them, and that is not a small thing you have left out, because the whole of the finding of this morning is that a person who holds a thing stops being asked.**"

Somebody said, "**Who are you.**"

"I am the man and the boy," he said.

---

Then the chair did the last of it, and it took about four minutes, and it is in the book with the day on it in Rennick Adley's hand and it was read out twice.

> *The fifteenth day of the seventh month. This meeting asked the Slade Cut Company, in this room, whether it would accept a duty that stops. **A body cannot be asked and cannot say no, and the chair said so at the start of the meeting before anybody spoke, and the chair has said out loud that the day was fixed for nineteen years of habit and not for any reason that survives being said.**
>
> *The question was put to persons and not to a body, in about nine words, and it was read out twice and the second reading was slower. Eleven persons said yes. Four said no, of whom two were not in the room and were read in. Nine asked what happens after and were told there is no answer to that yet. The rest said nothing and the chair counted the nothing.*
>
> ***And I record here, because it is my job and because it is the only thing I am certain of: no household is bound by anything said in this room today, and nothing said in this room has undone anything. The first exception is in force. The guarantee is standing offered and unanswered and costs nothing. About sixty of us under sixteen are inside both of them exactly as they were on the eighth of the fifth month, and nobody in this room has made that better and nobody has made it worse.***
>
> *There are about forty-nine persons wanted. There are eleven answers. I am sixty-four and I have been a chair for nineteen years and I have said in this room that the best thing that ever came out of forty meetings was a no, and I was right then, and I am going to say the other half of it now and I do not like it. **A count is a thing you can act on. A yes is not.** We have spent nineteen years collecting yeses and I have never once counted anything, and I am handing the count on today, and I am handing it to a man of thirty-eight who cannot read a question either and a foreman of fifty-one who has never once in nine years volunteered for anything, and neither of them asked me for it.*

Then she sat down, which she does not do, and about nine people looked at it.

---

Rennick Adley came to the west end afterwards and asked her one question, and it was the only question he asked her, and she answered it.

"You said a count is a thing you can act on."

"I did."

"**Then we have got forty-nine and about nine weeks, and you have just told about thirty people that this meeting has failed.**"

"**I have told about thirty people what this meeting produced.** That is not the same as failing and if you put *failed* in that book I will read it out and take it out and I have got the only vote in this room and it is mine and it is a chair's and I have never once used it and I am not starting at sixty-four." She stood up again. "**And I will tell you what I have found out, and you may write it down and you may think it is a woman's nonsense.**

"**In nineteen years I asked this room forty times for a resolution and I got a no out of it about nine times, and the noes are the only things in that book I would put my name to.** And the yeses we have got are about eleven this morning out of about sixty people, and eleven yeses is not a company and eleven yeses is not a resolution and eleven yeses is not a list of works, and it is a count, **and I would rather have handed on a number than a promise, and I did not know that about myself until this morning and I am sixty-four.**"

---

Two things closed the morning, and the bay found out later that they were the two things it was for.

A book-keeper of thirty-eight wrote the count at the top of a fresh page in a book with no heading, and under it he wrote the number of doors, and under that he wrote a line that Rennick Adley read out twice and the second reading was slower:

> ***A rotation is not a list. It is one question, asked sixty times, in sixty doorways, and the question has to be the same question every time, and it has to be one a person can say no to, and the sixth line is what makes it one.***

And then the man of about thirty-one in the bay coat came back to the west end on his way out, and stopped, and said nine words to a chair of sixty-four without being asked anything.

"**I will take a season. And I am not the first one you should ask, and I know that, and I am asking to be asked later and not first, and put me down as the last.**"

And Doreth Ferrow said, "**That is not a thing anybody has ever said in this bay and I do not know what to do with it, and I am not going to find out this morning, and Rennick, write down exactly what he said and the day and do not tidy it.**"

It went in with the day on it, untidied.

**Forty-nine.**
