# Chapter 147: The Question That Is Not Hers

The tool shed at the Slade end is about nine feet by seven and it has a bench on one side with a vice on it and about eleven tools hanging on nails that all used to have names painted under them. The doors are off their top hinges and it is not a room anybody sits in, it is a place where things are kept that are needed at once.

**Halla Wray is fifty-one and has been foreman of the Slade Cut for nine years.** She has been asked about a bank five times this year and said no five times and every one of the five was right. She has said yes twice. She has never once been asked to take anything, and in nine years there has not been a form anywhere that wanted her to, and she found that out in the third week of the ninth at about the sixth hour of the morning and it took her four days to do anything about it.

The second week of the ninth is cold at that end of the cut. The bay is about four hundred yards from the shed and the shed is four hundred and forty foot of bank above it on one side and about nine acres and about thirty-one households on the other, and there is about one hundred and forty feet of ditch on the south side and a scour at the third-of-the-way place, and both of them are on nobody's list.

---

Here is what she worked out in four days, in the ordinary way, in a shed, on the back of a return, and she wrote it out and then she did not enter it in the returns because a return is not a book that has no heading in it.

> *A duty that stops is not the same as a duty that survives stopping.*

> *A season's duty with a day at the end of it and nobody at the day stops. That is a duty that ends. The water does not end. About nine acres and about thirty-one households are behind this bank and the only arrangement anybody has found in four months for what happens after a duty ends is that the water does not end and somebody finds out.*

> *So the arrangement has to have a **name entered before the term ends**, and a name cannot be entered before a person has been asked, and asking is about nine weeks of doorways.*

> *And about nineteen of about sixty will say no.*

> *And the nineteen are still behind this bank.*

She read that back four times and then she folded the return and did not enter it and has not entered it and does not intend to.

---

Then she worked out the second half, which is the half that undid four months of her own work, and it took about a day and a half and she has said since that it is the only thing anybody has taught her in nine years and it was taught to her by a piece of arithmetic.

"I have spent four months saying **no lists**. That is what I have been saying. I have said it in this shed, I have said it in a bay, I have said it to a man of fifty about a letter with a seal on it, and I have been right every time and I want it on the record that I was right every time.

"**And a rotation is a list.** A list of works, with about forty-one names on it and a day against every name, and it is a list, and I have not got an argument against it, and I have got an argument against the other one.

"And here is the argument. **The other one has no end in it. A person on it is on it until a man with a tray changes his mind in a city and tells nobody.** And this one — every name on this one is on it for ninety days and then comes off, in front of about thirty people, on a day anybody can point at.

"**That is the whole of it. That is what a term is. A list with a term in it is not a chain, it is a chain with a door in it, and the door is the day it comes off, and nobody can close that door and nobody has claimed to.**"

---

Then she went to the gate, which is about two hundred yards from the mill, and there is a man at most gates within a mile of that cut and there is only one who has come to the bay four times since the fourth month and has not once been asked anything by anybody.

**Kem Ostry is thirty-one. He is a carter and he works the Slade to the mill wall and back with a cart of no particular description and he has no employer of record, and he has been paid at a guild rate of one shilling and sixpence a day for a barrow of stone, a man and a boy, since the fourth month, and he has not been on a list of any kind in his life and he has been *the man and a boy* in a guild's phrase for about six weeks, and his name has been in nobody's column anywhere.**

He was sitting on the end post at the landing with his sleeves up, and Hob Bray's slate was above him on a nail in a wooden frame with Bray's name and his age on it, and he had read it about nine times and had not asked Bray what it was for.

She asked him at the gate, standing, in about four minutes, and she did not have a slate.

"**Will you take the bank for a season.** One person, forty-five pence a day, about ninety days, a day to give it up with a day's notice, and your name at the foot of a list with a day against it, in a book that has no heading. And the chair put in a line on the fifteenth of the seventh that nobody asks a person twice, and that applies to you as well as to anybody, and I am not going to come back."

He did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"No," he said.

"That is that. Thank you."

But he said, "**You have not asked me why.**"

"**No. I have not, and I am not going to, and I have said in a shed in the sixth month that a person can be asked and can say no and can put it down, and I meant it, and you have just proved it and I would rather a person proved it than a rule.**"

And he said, "I will tell you why, because nobody has ever asked me, and then you can do what you like with it."

---

It took him about four minutes and he did it well, and about nine people heard all of it because about nine people were in the mill yard and none of them moved.

"I came up that road in the fourth month with a cart of seed behind a man and I stopped at the gate and watched a flood come down a bank that a clerk in a city paid for out of his own wage.

"And I stayed. That is the whole of it and it took me a fortnight to understand that I was staying. I stayed because I did not want to go and find out whether a thing I had seen work was going to keep working, and I have been in about four of those bay meetings since and I have not said one word in any of them except one.

"And in the last week of the sixth month that woman of sixty-four asked a room who came in the fourth month and why, and about four people said they wanted to see, and I said, in nine words, **I wanted to see if it worked.**

"That is the truest thing I have said in nine years and it has been in a book in this bay with the day on it, and I would like somebody to have put it there, and I would also like it not to have been frightening.

"**And that is the why. If my name is at the foot of that bank, then every fortnight for a season somebody in this bay is going to come and find out whether it is working. And that is what a list of works is. That is what it is for. And I have been the one watching and I have got about four months of it and I would like it stopped.**

"**I would rather be the man and the boy in a rate with no column in it. That is the truth and I have never said it out loud to anybody because nobody has asked.**"

---

There was a gap of about nine seconds in it, and then a woman of about forty-four at the third house, who had said no twice and was not in the bay that morning because she was in her own kitchen, said nothing at all because she was not there, and about nine people who were there looked at each other.

Then a foreman of fifty-one said, "**Then I am not going to put you down, and I am not going to ask you again, and I want that written down with the day on it and I want the no read back to you twice.**"

And Rennick Adley wrote it, and it is in a book with no heading, and it is the fourth entry in a month that is not work, and it says:

> *The seventeenth day of the ninth. Kem Ostry, thirty-one, carter, asked at the gate in the same words as sixty other people and said no, and gave a reason, and the reason is that a name at the foot of a list of works means somebody comes and finds out whether it is working every fortnight for a season, and that he has been the one watching since the fourth month and would like it stopped. He was not argued with. He was not asked a second time. **The reason is entered because a person is entitled to have the reason they gave written down in the place where the no is, and not in a letter.***

---

Then she asked him the second question, in the ordinary way, on her way to the gate and not making a thing of it, and it is the question that built the arrangement and she has said since that she did not think of it and that it was handed to her.

"Kem. If you were putting a thing on about forty-one people and you were the one putting it on — what would you have asked the next person."

He thought about it for about eleven seconds.

"**I would have asked them who was going to be standing there when they wanted to know what the paper said.**"

---

Halla Wray stopped walking.

"Say that again."

"I said it once."

"Say it slower and I will get a book."

And she got a book, and a slate, and a pencil, and he said it slower, and she wrote it down as he said it, and then she read it back to him and he made her change one word.

It is in a book with no heading, in his hand and then in hers underneath, with the day on it.

> ***Who is going to be standing there when I want to know what this paper says.***

And the seventeen-year-old girl who reads things out in that bay twice was sitting on the bench against the north wall and had not said anything for about an hour, and she said, in a voice about four people in that yard could hear:

"**That is a question a girl of eleven asked a person of seventeen on a bank at the Reed Cut gate on the twentieth of the last month of last year.**"

---

And Tavi Venn told Halla Wray about it afterwards, on the bank above the mill, in about four minutes, and she did not want to be thanked and said so before she started.

"I have been carrying it since the twentieth of the last month of the last year. She asked me four questions off a list a man put on a stone in a lane and then she asked a fifth that is not on the stone, and she said it to me on a bank in the cold at about the seventh hour, and I did not answer it, and I have not answered it since and it is the only question anybody has ever asked me that I could not answer.

"And I have watched four men read four papers out in this bay since the fifth month and not one of them has put that question at the top of anything. And a carter of thirty-one has said it in about eleven words at a gate this morning and he does not know where he got it, and he got it from standing in a yard in the cold, and so did she, and I think that is the same place."

"He has not read a schedule in his life."

"He read one he could not read," said Tavi Venn, "and I read about nine of them to about nine people who could not read them, and neither of us is paid, and that is a thing you are about to do something about and I would like to know what it is before you do it and I am not asking for anything."

---

So Halla Wray did the arithmetic, out loud, in a yard, to about nine people, because that is the rule of that place and it has been since the twenty-eighth of the third month.

"**About nine of the sixty cannot read a paragraph.** Two men in that shed are both about fifty-five and one of them told Halla Wray in the sixth month that his father had a thing like that on him once and could not read it either and never found out what it was.

"**So a rotation of about forty-one people, of whom nine cannot read a paragraph, is not a duty. It is a notice.** A notice with forty-one names on it that about nine people on this cut cannot find out what it says, and one reader, unpaid, standing in a bay at the seventh hour, saying *I read it, I do not say* and being asked by four people what it means and giving all four of them the same four words.

"And that is what has happened since the fifth of the fifth and nobody has changed it because it is free."

"And the answer," said Rennick Adley, who had come down to the yard without being asked, "is that the reader goes in the rotation."

---

"In the rotation," said Halla Wray. "Not paid extra. Not paid by the bay — the bay has four shillings a year and that is a rent. In the rotation. Forty-five pence a day like everybody else, for a season, with a day against the name, on the list of works.

"And the reason, and it is the only reason and it is the whole of the finding of this volume: **a person who reads and is not in the rotation is a person who cannot be told to stop.**"

That went round the yard, and about four people at the mill wall did not move.

"And that is what a foreman of the district works found about a column with no heading nine months ago, and what a clerk of a flood terrace found about a sixth space with no heading on it last week, and what a woman of twenty in a counting room four hundred and thirty miles away found in a fourth column of a dyers' book six years after a woman of sixty-eight refused it, **and the four of us have never met and I would put money on there being no way to get us in one room.**"

---

Then she did the thing nobody asked her to do, and it is the reason this chapter is the chapter before the last one.

She took the slate and she wrote her own name at the bottom of it, and a date against it, and she read it out twice, the second time slower, to about nine people.

"**H. Wray. From the seventeenth of the ninth to a day stated, to be stated by whoever is after me, and not by me.**"

Somebody said, "Put the day on it."

"**I have just said that the day is not to be stated by me, and that is the first clause I have ever written for myself, and I am not changing it for a man in a bay coat.**

"Forty-five pence a day. About ninety days. One day's notice, in my own hand, in Rennick's book. And my name at the foot of it with a day against it, which means that about forty people in ninety-one households now know where I am on any given day without having to send to a house, and they have known it since about the ninth of the first month anyway, and the difference is that this is on a page and that was on a habit."

She put the slate down.

"**And I have been a foreman for nine years and I have never once been on a piece of paper, and I have spent four months telling this bay that a name at the foot of a page is a road and that the road goes to the man with the tray, and I am not going to stand in a yard and say that and then not be on one.**"

"You will be on one," said Rennick Adley, "and about four hundred yards below this a foreman of the district works is on a column with no heading over it with four hundred names in it who do not know they are on it, and you are going to be on one with a day on it and about sixty people who do know."

"**Then I am going to be a more expensive mistake than him,**" said Halla Wray, "**and I have had that one about nine years and I know exactly what it costs.**"

---

The count in the book with no heading that night, read out twice in a bay and the second reading slower, was **seventeen persons who had said yes, with days against them, and twelve who had said no, with reasons, and the rest of the sixty not asked at all** — and a foreman of fifty-one refused to have that last sum written down, and said so out loud, and the reason she gave is the reason this volume is worth reading.

"**Write the two numbers. Do not write the third one. The third one is going to change every week for nine weeks and in nine weeks it is going to be a fact, and I want there to be one figure in that book that means the same thing on the day it is written as it did when it was written, and those two do.**"

So the book has seventeen and twelve and a line under them that says, in a foreman's hand:

> ***Seventeen yes. Twelve no, with reasons. The rest not asked, and I am not going to add it up in here, because the sum is a moving thing and a moving thing in a book stops being a record and becomes an opinion.***
