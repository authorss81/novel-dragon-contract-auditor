# Chapter 135: A Form With a Reason and No Why

The tool shed at the Slade end is about nine feet by seven and it has a bench on one side with a vice on it and about eleven tools hanging on nails that all used to have names painted under them. The doors are off their top hinges and it is not a room anybody sits in, it is a place where things are kept that are needed at once.

**Halla Wray is fifty-one and has been foreman of the Slade Cut for nine years.** She has been asked about a bank five times this year. She has said no five times and every one of the five was right, and she has said in a shed that she would rather be asked than not, and has been asked twice and said yes twice, and has refused to be asked a third time without being asked.

Her condition on the spring work was that a crew be paid out of something with a figure on it and not out of a man's wage in a city. There was no such thing, and the ford work lapsed on the last day of the second week of the fourth month with two figures on a lapsed list, and she put her own name under both.

And on the sixth of the fifth month a foreman of fifty-one read nine pages in a bay, and in the first week of the same month a clerk from a city four hundred and thirty miles away wrote a thing on the back of that lapsed list and held a ford for a year, and a man of about fifty opened a letter that was addressed to a body and became the person it was about.

Then on the ninth of the sixth month a paper came up the road with a seal on it and her name at the top of it.

---

The man from the district works brought it. He brought it to the shed and put it on the bench by the vice and stood there, and it is the wrong side of sixty and he has a name and it is Wendt Sayer and he is a foreman of the district works and not a lawyer.

"You are not to answer it."

"I did not say I was going to answer it."

"I know. I am telling you what the outside of it says, and I have read the outside of it twice, and I am going to read it to you and then I am going to leave and shut the door behind me because I have a return to write."

He read the outside of it.

> *Notice of a seat, given in a matter touching an instrument sealed with the fire-and-water device, being a water guarantee offered to an unincorporated company working a public cut in the district named at the foot, and touching a proceeding of no court, there being no proceeding.*
>
> *One. The seat gives notice of the instrument and of the offer of it.*
> *Two. The seat states that the instrument requires the answer of a person and not of a body, and that the seat is aware that the body has not answered and that the instrument stands offered and unanswered.*
> *Three. The seat asks the person named below to acknowledge receipt.*
> *Four. The seat states that the answer of the person is not a consent and does not bind any person who has not answered, and that the instrument is not affected by an acknowledgement either way.*
> *Five. The seat will pay the carriage of any person whom the district court thinks should be heard about the instrument.*
> *Six. The person is this one. — with the device, and a seal, and a name at the foot.*

Wendt Sayer read all six lines off a piece of paper about two feet long that is not printed, and then he said, "That is five lines and a question and it has a seat's name on the bottom of it and not a person's, and I do not know what to do with it, and I am not going to pretend I do."

"The fifth line," said Halla Wray.

"I do not know what the fifth line is for."

"You have read it three times since the ninth and you have not asked me once, and I have been waiting nine days for you to ask me."

"I was not going to ask you because I have got four men standing at a gate."

"You have got four men at a gate because a letter is standing in a mill yard. Ask me."

"It pays carriage," said Wendt Sayer. "**It offers to pay the carriage of anybody the district court thinks should be heard. And I have been in this matter for about eleven weeks and I have read four hundred and thirty miles of paper about people not being heard, and I have never once seen a court asked which people it thinks should be heard.** That is what the fifth line is for. It is the only one of the six that is not about the guarantee."

---

She read it out herself, in the shed, standing up, with the door off its top hinges, and the second time was slower, and there were about nine men in and out of that shed in the course of the afternoon and she read it to every one of them who had not heard it and made the second reading for the men who cannot read a paragraph, of whom there are two and both of them are about fifty-five.

**Halla Wray can read. That is not in dispute and has never been in dispute and is in nine years of a shed nobody's business. It is the other thing about her that is not in dispute: she has never once been asked to read anything by anybody at the district works, in nine years, and on the ninth of the sixth month a seat's paper arrived with her name at the top of it and the ordinary consequence of that is that she has to.**

And there is no why on it.

---

Here is the form, and it is a House form about a seat, and it is a single side of good paper and it is not printed.

> *I acknowledge receipt of the notice given on the ninth day of this month, and I am: the person named. My answer is ( ) I do not acknowledge ( ) I acknowledge and wish to say —*
>
> *Reason, if any: —*

Two boxes and a line for a reason. The line for a reason is about two inches long and takes about nine words.

There is nothing on it at all about why.

She looked at that for a long time. The clerk of the district works stood in the doorway and then went and sat on the mill wall and let her get on with it, which was the single most useful thing anybody did for her that fortnight and he has not been thanked and did not expect to be and is not a villain and is a foreman of the district works who has wanted to be asked to write a heading over a column in his own return book for nine years.

"Noted," she said. "**A box for the answer and a line for the reason and nothing whatever for the why.** So the most a person can get into the record of this is nine words about a fact, and everything anybody actually thinks about it has to stay in their own head, and I am not going to complain about that, because I have wanted a reason on a piece of paper since the first month of this year and I have not had one either."

She wrote it out herself, in her own hand, on the form, in about nine words.

> *I am not the head of a body and cannot be and there is none.*

Then she read it back twice, and then she made Wendt Sayer read it back, and then she made him write it into the book at the Slade end in his own hand because hers would have been thrown away by whoever receives it.

"**Do not put my name at the foot of that. Put the reason in and put nothing else. I do not want to be the person whose name is at the foot of a seat's paper and I am not going to be findable twice in one month.**"

"**That is the first sensible thing anybody has said in this matter for about four months,"** said Wendt Sayer, "**and it is also the exact thing you said in a bay in the first week of this month about a man from a city, and you told him you would rather be able to be found. You have changed your mind in six weeks.**"

"I have not changed my mind. He did not have a piece of paper with my name on it, and I have."

---

Then she did the thing the chapter is actually for, and she did it because she was standing in a shed at about the fourth hour with a form in her hand and a form is a thing that makes you look at the shape of the next one.

She asked the shed the question, in the ordinary way, and she asked it of about nine men over the course of an hour and a half, and nobody was in a hurry because it was the sixth of the month and there was nothing on any list.

"**Has anybody here ever had a thing they did not want and could not put down?**"

Four people said yes. Two of them could not say what it was. One of them was about forty and said, "**I wanted to go to the coast.**" And Wendt Sayer, who was still sitting on the mill wall, said, "I signed a return once in the ninth year I did not agree with and I have been carrying it for about nine years and there is nobody in that office I could hand it to and there is no form."

Halla Wray wrote that down on the back of the form she had just filled in, and then she did not give it to him, and put it in her own pocket instead.

"You are not going to enter that anywhere."

"No."

"**Why not.**"

"Because you have not asked me to and because if I enter it in a book in that office it is a return about a person and I do not write returns about people, and I have not written one in nine years and I am not starting in the sixth month with a piece of paper in my hand." She said it as a fact and not as a rebuke and he took it as one.

Then she said the rest of it, out loud, in the shed, to about nine men, and it is the sentence the chapter ends on and it is not hers, it is nine words she took off a form and gave back.

"**A person can be asked and can say no and can put it down. A body cannot be asked, and cannot say no, and cannot put it down, and if a thing is put on it, it is put on it.** That is the whole of what I have learned this year and I have learned it in a shed and not in a room with a table in it, and I have said no five times and every one of the five was about a bank, and I would like it on the record that I have never once been asked about a guarantee and I have been put in one."

The two men who cannot read a paragraph in that shed are both about fifty-five and they have been at this cut for about thirty years and neither of them has ever asked her anything, and she made the second reading of the notice for them anyway, twice, and one of them said, at the end of it, "That is six lines."

"That is six lines."

"My father had a thing like that on him once and he could not read it either and he never found out what it was."

"**Then he is the only person on this cut who came out of a thing like that all right and it was not because anybody did anything for him.**"

Nobody laughed at that either.

---

She sent the form back down the road in a hand and not in an envelope, on the eleventh of the sixth month, and the man from the district works carried it himself, and it went four hundred miles, and nobody in ninety-one households was told any of it by anybody who was paid to tell them.

Here is the thing she said to him at the gate of the district works yard, standing, at about the ninth hour, and it is the last thing she says in this chapter and it took her about nine seconds and Wendt Sayer wrote it down afterwards in his own return book in a column with no heading over it, which he has done three times this year, and did not put a date on it because a column with no heading is not a return.

"**Take it yourself. Not a boy. If a boy takes it then a boy is what a House sees at the end of it, and in about four years there will be a record of this somewhere that says a boy was involved in a thing a seat did, and he will be twenty-two and he will not be able to say no.** And when you get to the top of that road I want you to read the sixth line out again to yourself before you go in, and it is your own name, and it is the first time in nine years that your name has been at the top of anything a building will keep.

"And then I want you to come back up the hill and tell me what you saw, and whatever it is, I am not going to argue with it.

"**A gate a person can shut is the only thing worth having and there are about sixty of them shut on purpose at the foot of that bank at this moment, and the price of shutting one is that the person who shut it can be found. That is the arrangement. That is the only one on offer. I have been the price for about nine weeks now and I would rather be the price than be a room with nobody in it and four hundred feet of bank in front of it.**"

He took it himself. It went four hundred miles, and nobody in ninety-one households was told any of it by anybody who was paid to tell them.

The carriage offer was still live on the day it went out. It had been live for about fourteen months. It was live, and inoperable, and it said that a seat would pay the carriage of any person whom **the district court** thought should be heard, and its own terms required the district court to be asked, and nobody had asked it, and there is no form in this empire for one court to ask another court what it has found.

On the thirteenth of the sixth month a clerk of thirty-eight at a window on a flood terrace four miles away was handed nine lines of a notice of a seat by a boy who was told to give it to the magistrate, and the magistrate was in her chamber, and the clerk brought it up, and the magistrate read all six lines standing up and then asked one question.

"Who sent it."

"A seat," said Ivet Sarn. "**It is a House paper and it is a good one, and it is the second-best piece of drafting anybody has ever handed me at this window, and the first one was the other half of the same office in the sixth month of last year.**"

"Has it been answered."

"Four days ago. Declined, with a reason, and there is no why on the form and there is not going to be one." She put the slip down. "**And the fifth line pays carriage.**"

Leda Rell read the fifth line twice and then went and stood at her own window and looked out at the embankment, where the water mark from the fourth month is still about four inches above the paving, and where about a hundred and forty files are in a press in a corridor behind her, in her own hand, and which she has never read as a set.
