# Chapter 139: What a Seal Is For

There is a fire in the grate because there is a fire in the grate, and the window looks at a roof, and there are nine things in the room and one of them is a book about ninety years old with a crack in the spine.

**Vaunt Oris is fifty-one or two. He is a notary of House Oris in his own right, in a chamber of this House, and he has been in that chair for nineteen years, and he reads human inheritance records as slowly and as carefully as that city reads a river return.** He has certified about four hundred instruments a year in that time. He has never certified one he had not seen. That is not in any rule of the house. It is in him, and it is the whole of what he is, and it is the thing that is going to cost this House something on the twenty-sixth day of this month.

There are two people in the room with him. The practice's clerk, who is about thirty-four and drafts, and the trade assessor, who stands between a house and a guild and is not anybody's servant and has been writing things down for twenty-six years.

**The request came up the stair on the second of the sixth month and it has been on that table for thirty-two days.**

> *The practice is asked to be satisfied that the persons named in the book of the Company working the Slade Cut are the persons of that Company, so that the guarantee may be certified. The practice is also asked to be satisfied that the Company has accepted the duty, the acceptance being on the face of an instrument drawn in a district four hundred and thirty miles away and placed there on the twelfth of the fourth month.*
>
> *A certificate is desired.*

He answered it in two parts on the twenty-sixth day of the sixth month and it took about nine minutes and there was a witness at each end of the room, and the witness at this end is the trade assessor and the witness at the other end is a person in another building four hundred and thirty miles away who was told that a notary's table and a House notary do not sign the same sheet and that somebody has to be at both ends of it, and the somebody was a foreman of fifty-one who has been on a bank since the second month of this year and who came down the road in the fourth week of the sixth month and stood in a passage in a House about two hundred miles from a notary's table and did not sign anything and said afterwards that she was glad of it.

---

He read the second part out first, and certified it, and it took about four minutes and it is this:

> *Certified. That the said Company is an unincorporated body upon the register of bodies in this district; that it has no head; that no person is its head; that a body may not be made a party by any court in this empire; and that a body which has been given a duty and cannot refuse it has accepted it.*
>
> *The last of those is not an opinion. It is a consequence. **A body cannot say no. Nine words on a piece of paper in a hired bay four hundred and thirty miles from this room are the whole of the acceptance, and nobody in ninety-one households said them, and the paper says on its face that the acceptance does not evidence a consent. That sentence is why this certificate can be given and it is the reason the certificate is worth nothing.***

"I have just sealed a true statement that a body agreed to something it was not asked about," he said, "and I want it noticed that I have done it deliberately and that I would do it again, and that the reason is the last sentence, and that if that paper had not said it, this certificate would not exist."

---

Then he read the first part out, and refused it, and this is the whole of the refusal and it is four sentences and it is copied into the book of concessions in his own hand.

> *Refused. The practice is not satisfied that the persons named in the said book are the persons of the said Company, and is not able to be satisfied of it, and is not asked to be satisfied of anything else in place of it.*
>
> *A book with names in it is not a list of persons. The said book has nine hands in it and about nine hundred and forty names in it over about sixty years, and no heading at the top of it, and no list of persons in it, and about four hundred of those names are of persons who are dead, and the figure of about ninety-one households is not in the book at all and is a count taken off it and written on the back of a person's hand by a person who cannot read a paragraph.*
>
> *The practice is not able to certify that the ground and the paper are the same thing, because they are not the same thing, and the difference is about four hundred dead names and sixty years of writing down work instead of making a list.*
>
> *A seal is a promise that the paper and the ground are the same thing. **I will not put one on a statement that is true of a book and false of a cut.***

Nobody in the room said anything for about nine seconds, which is about how long Oswin Marrow waited for a different man in a different room in a different city, and Vaunt Oris has never been told that and would not have minded.

Then the practice's clerk asked the question that a clerk asks, because a clerk has to ask it.

"What goes in the book, then."

"That is the whole of what is left of the job," said Vaunt Oris, "**and it took me thirty-two days and about nine of them were spent being asked nicely by people I work for.** What goes in the book is the refusal and the reason, in my own hand, with the day, and the name at the foot, because the practice's rule is that a concession is only ever entered with the name of the person who gave it up, and the reason the rule exists is that a House which can lose one quietly will lose the next one quietly and the next."

"That is a hundred and forty years."

"That is a hundred and forty years on that reach, and it goes in the entry for the reach, and it does not go to a court and does not go to a wing and does not go anywhere." He put the pen down. "**It goes in a book in a room, and in about four years, a person who wants to know whether this House can be held to anything will come and read it, and there will be a name at the foot of it, and the name will be mine, and I will not be able to say that I had no choice in the matter, because I did have a choice and I took the other one.**"

---

Here is what he said once, in that room, to two people, and he said it before he signed and not after, and he has not said it since and would not say it again if it were asked for.

He was asked, by the trade assessor, in the ordinary way, whether he wanted anything put in that he was not being asked for, which is a thing that assessor asks of everybody and which nobody has ever answered.

"I want one sentence on the page in my own hand," said Vaunt Oris, "**and it is not a reason and it is not a defence, and I am aware of that, and I am going to write it anyway because I have been in this chair for nineteen years and I have never once put a sentence of my own into a document and I am about to lose a hundred and forty years of somebody else's work and there is no reason on this earth why a man should do that without leaving a mark of his own.**

"I would rather it held. The rotation. The list of works with a name at the foot of it and a date and a review. A foreman who says no before she says yes. A book-keeper who will not start a column called *why not*. **I would rather ninety-one households were on a schedule than under a guarantee that costs them nothing and takes their children, and I have wanted that since the twelfth of the fourth month and I have not said it to one living soul until this afternoon, and I am saying it in a room with two people in it because I am about to do the other thing and I would like there to be one line on this page that is mine and is not a reason.**

"And I am aware that a dragon saying *I would rather* is worth about what a person saying *I would rather* is worth, and I am aware of why the first is worth more, and I am aware that in about four years somebody is going to read this book and see that a House said *I would rather* and use it as a thing about Houses."

He wrote it at the foot, in his own hand, and dated it, and it is the only sentence in the book of concessions in a hundred and forty years that is in the first person, and it is the reason that the rest of the entry is believed, and he knew that when he wrote it and wrote it anyway.

---

He used his own House's practice, and it is worth saying how.

**No instrument takes effect in a room before the persons it touches have had the day.** That is about four hundred years of house practice and not a law, and he told a human court about it in another city in the sixth month of last year before he was asked, and said that he had never once been able to persuade a court to adopt it and had stopped trying, and said *you are about to see me use it*.

He used it on the certificate. He wrote the second part and sealed it and then he left the sheet on the table in the open for a day, unsealed, and the day was for the persons, and the persons were about ninety-one households and four hundred and thirty miles away, and the day did them no good whatever and that was not the point.

"This is the first time in nineteen years I have used my own House's rule against my own House's request," he said, to the assessor, on the twenty-sixth day of the sixth month, at about the fourth hour, after the thing was signed and before the thing was sent. "**I am aware of what that is. It is not defection and it is not a conversion and it is not a man discovering that human law is better. It is a notary refusing to put a seal on a sentence that is true of a book.** And I am not going to leave the chamber and I am not going to stop certifying and I am not going to be a bridge to anything, and I would like it entered that I have said the word myself and I have not been asked for it by anybody."

The assessor wrote it down, and it is the only sentence in about four hundred and thirty miles of this matter that is in the record of a House and not in a court, and it went down the road to a bay on a towpath on the twenty-ninth day of the sixth month in a hand and not in an envelope, at four pence, and the woman of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph had it read to her twice, and the second reading was the slower one.

---

**A split instrument.** The column in the register of concessions had never been used in this house in nineteen years and it now has one line in it, and one half of the sheet carries a seal and the other half carries a name, and the name is at the foot of a page in a book about a hundred and forty years old with a crack in the spine.

The entry says, in the hand of the practice's clerk, that the concession with which the practice certifies on that reach is **lapsed**, and that the lapse is in respect of the certification of the Slade Cut guarantee, and that the person who gave it up is named at the foot, and that his reason is copied in above in his own hand.

**What that means, in a room, in about nine words:** this House can no longer certify a scale instrument on that reach. Not this practice. This House. From the day the entry is made, and there is no appeal, and there is no form in this empire for anybody to ask whether it is right.

**It means the guarantee stands, and stands offered, and stands unanswered, and can be taken up at any time afterwards by a person answering in those terms, and nobody is going to take it up, because a person answering in those terms is a person who is the head of a body, and there is no head, and a magistrate has entered that one cannot be had.**

So it stands anyway, and nothing on that cut is affected by anything that happened in a chamber two hundred miles away, and about sixty children under sixteen are in it exactly as they were on the eighth of the fifth month, and no household on that cut has been told a single word of it by anybody who was paid to tell them.

He wrote a third thing at the foot of the register, after the entry, in his own hand, and this is the finding and it is the one that will be read:

> *An uncertified guarantee may be offered again, by a person, with a different question. **I am not able to say that nobody will.** I am the person who refused this one and I have written my name at the foot of the refusal, and in about four years the person who reads this will have both, and the person who comes then will not be a boy of fourteen and will not be a man from a district works and will not be a woman of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph, and will be somebody who has been looking for this for a long time.*
>
> *I would like it recorded that I thought about that for about nine days and I did it anyway, and that the reason is that the practice's rule is a name at the foot of a page and a name at the foot of a page is worth exactly what it is worth against the person who will be looked up next, and I have been the person who will be looked up, and I would rather be looked up by somebody with a reason.*

---

He did not go down the road. That is the thing about him and it is the last thing in this chapter.

**A notary of a House does not go and tell a district what his House has done.** There is no form for it and there is no expectation of it and the whole of the practice's arrangement is that a House does not go to a place. A copy goes, at four pence, in a hand, carried by a person who is not paid for carrying it, and the person who carries it is a clerk of the district works who was the wrong side of sixty and had four men at a gate for four days with a list that said nothing on it, and he carried it up a towpath in three days and did not open it.

It went into a bay. There were about thirty people in it, and about nine of them could not read a word of it, and it was read out twice, and the second reading was the slower one, and both readings were by a person who was not paid and neither of them would say what it meant.

Nobody on that bank knows what a concession is. That was said out loud in that room on the twenty-ninth of the sixth month by a book-keeper of thirty-eight, and it is the first sentence in about four months that anybody in ninety-one households has said which is worse than not knowing.

**The guarantee did not go away. That is the thing that has to be said at the end of this chapter and it is the reason the refusal was worth what it cost and it is also the reason it was not enough.** The guarantee stands, offered and unanswered, under a device that no longer has a notary behind it on that reach, and it will stand there in exactly the words it stood in on the eighth of the fifth month, and about sixty children under sixteen are inside it, and the instrument that would take them out of it is on a bench in a hired bay with a foreman's name at the foot of it and a date and no figure on it.

So the cost of a seal that was not put on a sentence about a book is a hundred and forty years of a House's licence on one reach of one river, paid by a man who is not a villain and is not a friend and has not been thanked and did not ask to be, **and what it bought is about four hundred and thirty miles of road and one sentence in a book in the first person, and neither of those is a wall.**

**A body cannot refuse. A person can. And the whole of the difference is a name at the foot of a page, and it took a dragon nineteen years and a hundred and forty years of his House's work to be able to put one there, and it took a woman of sixty-four who cannot read a paragraph to say out loud, in a bay, in nine seconds, what it is worth.**
