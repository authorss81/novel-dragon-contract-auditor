# Chapter 120: The Line at the Top of It

The Mosswake Roll Court is a building on a flood terrace with a court room on the ground floor, a clerk's window at the back of it, a board above the sill, and an embankment corridor behind that with four presses in it.

The clerk's window is the same window a man of about fifty came up the road to in the fourth week of the first month of last year with a shilling in his hand, and the same window a woman of twenty-four put a shilling on before the hour was over, because a person who has paid can answer without owing anybody.

Rennick Adley got there at about the ninth hour on the last day of the third month, having walked four miles up a towpath the night before and four miles back down it in the dark, and he was carrying a form and a sheet of foolscap and a list of four doors.

**Two of the four doors gave a name up in under a minute.** The third took about eleven minutes because the woman in it wanted to know what it was for, and he told her, four times, in four different ways, and she gave it.

The fourth door was the fourth house at the north of the ford and it belongs to a woman of about seventy-one who cannot read a paragraph, and she gave her name before he had finished asking for it, and then said, in about nine words, that if the paper was going into a building that recognised the room then it was a better paper than most things that have ever come up that lane, and that she would be wanting to see it in about four years, and not before.

**And at the first door nobody would give him anything at all.** Not a name and not a refusal. A woman of about thirty-one with two children stood in her own doorway with a boy on her hip and let him finish, and then said that her name was on the back of a thing in a shop in the district below the mill and that if it was on this as well then there would be two, and that she did not know what two of anything was for, and that he could write down *the woman at the first house who was in her own door on the last day of the third month* and that it would serve her fine because nobody was going to come.

So: **two names, and two descriptions of persons, and a form that will carry both**, and the reason it can carry both is that a described person is still a person and this court is going to say so in words on the face of the paper.

He asked, at all four doors, the one question he had not had time for anywhere else: *whose grandfather was Tarn Skell.*

Nobody at any of the four doors could tell him. One of them said she had been given the name in a kitchen and had never asked. One of them said there had always been Skells at the north of the ford. One of them said the name was not on any of the stones in the lane and he should go and look at the stones, which are a headstone and two kerbs, and that a man who wanted a family should try a churchyard instead of a lane.

---

The form is the ordinary form for hearing a body of persons, and it is about a third of a sheet, and it has been the ordinary form for about a hundred and forty years, and it is the form this court uses when a body of persons applies to be heard.

**There is a line at the top of it for the head of the body.**

The Slade Cut Company has never elected one. It could not elect one: a company is a body, a body cannot choose a head, and a magistrate of this court entered on the ninth of the second month, on her own motion, that a body of persons is not a person and cannot be made one by her court or any other, and there is no form in the district or the empire that does it.

So the line is blank. It was blank on the sixth of the second month when the application was made, and it was blank on the ninth when a woman of sixty-four told a clerk of two years to admit it anyway, and a magistrate admitted it on her own motion before anybody had spoken, and the clerk entered that it was the wrong form.

"I am not going to ask you to fill in that line," said Ivet Sarn, from behind the window, before he had got the paper out. "**I want you to hear me say that first, because in about nine seconds you are going to hand me a form with a blank line at the top of it and a clerk is supposed to say no to that, and I am not going to.**

"Sit down. No — stand, if you would rather. Everybody stands."

She was thirty-eight and nine years at that window and she has a book with no office on it in the second drawer, and everything she reads she writes down in her own hand first, in the first person, before she copies anything, and says who is not in the room.

"**I cannot admit an application.** A clerk cannot. On the ninth of the second month a magistrate of this court admitted one through the wrong form, on her own motion, before anybody had spoken, and it is in the minute, and if I had done that on my own authority she would have sacked me and been right to.

"And there is a magistrate in this building and she is not in this room and she is not on the floor and she is not expecting anybody this morning, and **she is not here, and she does not have to be, and that is the whole of the thing I am about to do and I am going to do it in front of you so that you can hear it.**

"There is a written undertaking in this court's own minute book of the ninth of the second month, entered on her own motion, and it says that this court will hear every application a body of persons brings through the wrong form until she is told otherwise. **It is a written undertaking. It is not a form. It does not require her to be in the room, it does not require anybody to be in the room, and it does not require me to agree with it.** I am using it. I am going to write at the top of this form, in my own hand, on this day, that it is admitted in the wrong form under the undertaking of the ninth of the second month, and that the magistrate was not in the room and was not asked and does not have to be.

"And then you are going to tell me, in about four minutes, what a form at this window costs a person, because you have not worked it out yet and I have read your face and you have not, and I would rather you worked it out here than in the fourth year."

---

He worked it out in about four minutes and it is written at the top of the form in a clerk's hand, and the clerk read it out to him before he signed anything, and it says:

> *These four persons may be heard, and are parties to this application from the filing of it, and cannot be un-named by them or by this court, and are findable from this day. The filing of this form is the making of them parties. It is entered here on the face of the form because a decision with nobody's name at the foot of it is worth about a fifth of one with a name.*

"A fifth," said Rennick Adley.

"A fifth. I have got a figure and it is out of my own experience and not out of anybody else's, and I have never written it on a form before, and I have never been asked to, and I am writing it because you are about to hand me a piece of paper about four people and I am not going to let you do it in a cold way.

"**A name in a box is worth about a fifth of one with a name at the foot of it.** I have had nine years of this window and that is my figure and it is the only figure on this form. If I am wrong then I am wrong out loud and in writing and somebody can find it in about four years."

Then she asked the question he had come to ask and he answered it in the third person, which is a clerk's own trick and which he had learned off her four miles up the river in a month.

"The line at the top is for the head of the body," she said. "What is at the top of yours."

"**Nothing,**" said Rennick Adley. "There is no head of the Slade Cut Company and there is not going to be one, and a magistrate has entered that one cannot be had, and I have come four miles to hand you a form that says so on its own face, and I would like the form to keep saying it after I have gone."

"Then I am going to write in the box, in my own hand: *no head of the body; the body is not a person and has not elected one and cannot.* And the minute will say it, and the minute is the thing that will be read in about nine years and the form is the thing that will be thrown away. **I have watched people care about the paper and it is never the paper.**"

---

He wrote it out then, on the counter, on the sill, with the shilling on the sill, and it took him about forty minutes, and this is the whole of it and Ivet Sarn copied it into the minute in her own hand afterward.

> *In the wrong form. The head of the body: none. There is none and there cannot be one.*
>
> *The applicant is the keeper of the book of a body of households of about ninety-one, with about sixty children under sixteen, which works a public cut. He is not a party and is not a solicitor and has no office and there is no other person in this matter who can be here instead of him. The body is not a person and this court has so entered, and the body is not asking to be heard. Four persons are asking to be heard and they are named below and they do not know they are named and they are not in this room.*
>
> *What they are to be heard about. A bank of about four hundred and forty foot of outer face on a public cut about four miles from this building, of which about nine acres and about thirty-one households stand behind, and a ford, and two posts and nine stones, and a water expected in the third week of the fourth month. A copy of the undertaking of ninety-one years back is in a notary's table in a city four hundred and thirty miles from here by a run which is a number of a book, and this court is asked for no copy of it and knows nothing of its terms.*
>
> *The persons to be heard. Four houses at the north of the ford and a person at each. This court is entered that the four are the four because there are four and not because anybody chose them.*
>
> *One. The person at the second house, aged about fifty-eight, named on this form and able to read a paragraph, who has never once been asked to read anything in sixty years.*
> *Two. The person at the third house, aged about forty-four, named on this form, who took eleven minutes to give the name and asked four times what the paper was for.*
> *Three. The person at the fourth house, aged about seventy-one, named on this form, who cannot read a paragraph and cannot be given this form, and who has been entered on it and told so out loud in her own door and did not ask to see it and did not refuse it.*
> *Four. The woman at the first house, aged about thirty-one, with two children, who gave no name and is described, and this court enters that a described person is a person, and is harder to serve and easier to lose than a named one, and is worth a good deal less than a fifth of a named party in every use this court knows of, and is entered here because the alternative is that she is not here at all.*
>
> *The court is asked for a date in the first week of the fourth month and is given to understand that the water is expected in the third week of the fourth month and that there is no form in this empire for asking any court to fix a date at which a water is expected, and that the applicant does not know that the water is coming and has taken nine days to find out and has taken the date from a keeper who does not give it out.*
>
> *The applicant says, and this court enters, that this application binds nobody who has not been heard; that about thirty persons of about ninety-one households came to a room on the twenty-eighth day of the third month and about sixty did not, and are not parties to this; that a body cannot be summoned, cannot be be a party, and cannot ask this court for anything; and that the four persons named are the four houses at the north of the ford because there are four houses there and not because anybody chose them.*

"Stop," said Ivet Sarn, about four lines from the end, in the middle of a word.

"I have said *can be be a party* and it is a thing I have learned off you, and I have been copying your hand since the fourth of the second month and I have got your *be be* and I am sorry for it."

"Keep it," said the clerk. "**I have had that *be be* in nine years of my own day-books and there is no form anywhere that puts it right, and if a man of thirty-eight from a body of ninety-one households copies it into an application in a court of the jurisdiction then it has travelled, and that is the first time that anything I have ever written down has travelled into a document with anybody's name at the top of it.** I am going to enter that I noticed it and that I let it stand, and the day I do that I am going to stop being able to say I did not know what I was doing."

---

There are three dates in this matter and two of them are on that form and the third is not.

There is a sitting of this court in the first week of the fourth month, and it is the first sitting, and the clerk can put four persons on it, and it is a morning, and it will be a morning of about two hours and a quarter.

The water is expected in the third week of the fourth month.

And there is a third date and Rennick Adley does not know it and cannot find it out and has stopped trying, and it is in a drawer in a building two hundred yards above a gate, and three people in this matter know the day of it and one of them is a man of twenty-nine who is the only person in the district who has said out loud that he is not going to be asked twice.

"I asked," said Rennick Adley. "I have been up four roads and one towpath in three days and I asked a foreman, a book-keeper, a miller, a chairwoman, a clerk of a water office who would not answer me and a doctor, and I have asked about the water, and I have not asked about that date because I do not know it exists, and I have not asked about it because **if there is a thing in this matter that a book-keeper of a company is not to know about, then that thing is probably the only thing holding that company together.**

"So there are three dates and I can move none of them. **A water in the third week of the fourth month is not a thing a court can be asked about. A sitting in the first week of the fourth month is four weeks early and there is no form for the gap. And a third thing on the last day of this month that I do not know about and will not try to.**

"Nobody in this matter can move any of the three. That is the sentence and I have come four miles to have it written down by a clerk in a form with a blank line at the top of it."

---

He paid the shilling and took the copy himself, and did not ask for it to go anywhere.

"**You are not sending that to the Company.**"

"No."

"That is a decision and it is a strange one and I want to know the reason and I am not asking you in the window." She wrote the copy number on the stub and pushed it through the sill. "**A copy that goes to the Company is a copy about ninety-one households. A copy in the hands of one man is a copy about one man, and the man is the one who is standing in front of me, and he is the only person in ninety-one households who can be asked about it.**

"And you are about to be findable. You have been findable since the second day of the third month and you did it on purpose, and now you are findable in a building that recognises the room, on a piece of paper with a court's name on it, and you will be asked about that paper in about four years by somebody with a tray. **You bought that and not a thing about the Company, and I have never seen anybody do that, and I have been at this window nine years.**"

---

And then, because the copy had to be stamped and the stamp is in the embankment corridor, she stood up and went through, and he went with her because there was nowhere else in that building for a man of thirty-eight to stand.

The corridor is about eleven feet wide, it runs the length of the back of the building, and it is colder than the window. There are four presses in it. The fourth is the press-marked one and it holds the returns of this court's quarterly correspondence cycle, and there are about a hundred and forty files in it and there have been for years.

He put his hand on one of them. It is a file of the sixth year, at about knee height, a hand's width thick, with a routing slip on the spine in her hand of nine years ago, and he put his hand flat on the spine and left it there for about four seconds.

He did not open it. He did not pull it out. He did not ask what was in it.

"You know what is in that one," he said.

"Yes," said Ivet Sarn.

"Then I am not going to ask you, and you are not going to tell me, and I am not going to write it down, and I want both of those on the record and I am aware that I cannot put them on a record of this court, so I am putting them here, in a corridor, at about the second hour of the afternoon, to a clerk and a book-keeper.

"And I want you to write one thing down and it is the only thing I am going to ask you for, and it is not about the file."

She had the book out. The book with no office on it, out of the second drawer, and it is the same book, and it is open at a page with about two hundred lines in it in her own hand in the first person.

"Write this," he said. "**A person came and put a hand on the file of the sixth year on the last day of the third month and did not open it, and he does not know what is in it, and I have not asked, and nobody has asked me, and neither of us is going to.**"

She wrote it. She read it back. She made him read it back, which she does with everybody, and then wrote a line of her own under it and read that back too, and shut the book, and it is the only line in that book in nine years about a thing nobody asked her about.

"I have known which press it was in and which shelf it was on and which year it was for, since the year I was clerk at that window," she said, "and I have not opened it in nine years, and I have never had a reason, and about a hundred and forty people have walked up and down this corridor since then and I have not wanted anybody to ask me about it, and I have just found out what that is.

"It is not that it is a secret. **It is that nobody has ever asked me, and a person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and I have been a clerk of this court for nine years and that is the fourth time in nine years that sentence has been true about me and the fourth time has been the easiest to write down.**"

---

Rennick Adley went back up the towpath at about the fourth hour with a copy in his coat and four doors in a list and a form's worth of about forty minutes' work in a minute book in a building four miles behind him, and the sun was going over the flats about two inches an hour and the bank at the Slade was eleven feet high and had been eleven feet high since the twenty-first of the second month and will be eleven feet high until the third week of the fourth month.

The third week of the fourth month.

A court clerk of the Mosswake Roll Court admitted an application on the last day of the third month in a form that is the wrong form, under a written undertaking of the ninth of the second month that was entered on a magistrate's own motion, with the magistrate not in the building, and put at the top of it, in her own hand, that **these four persons are parties from the filing and cannot be un-named**, and that a decision with nobody's name at the foot of it is worth about a fifth of one with a name.

The line at the top of the form is for the head of the body and it is blank and it stays blank.

And nobody in this matter can move any of the three dates: **a man of twenty-nine at the far end of the cut has until the last day of the third month to say one word that would void the only sheet in that district which gave a flood authority to a person instead of a body, and the court will not sit about a bank until the first week of the fourth month, and in a bay in a mill yard a rule binds about thirty people out of about ninety-one households and says nothing whatever about the other about sixty, and the water is in the third week of the fourth month.**

And about four hundred and thirty miles away, at the fourth counter of a notary's table, a shilling's worth of good paper with four lines on it is lying in a book with no office on it, and the fourth of the four lines is blank.
