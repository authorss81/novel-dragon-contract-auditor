# Chapter 124: What a Wagon of Kerb Is Worth

The mill yard off the Slade has a grain store in it, and the grain store has two ends, and the south end is the Company's and the north end is a bay that a miller gave to the Company on the sixth day of the third month for a year, in about four minutes, in his own doorway, and which costs nothing and is not recorded as a gift.

The yard itself is about a hundred and forty yards from the ford and it is beaten earth and it belongs to **Aven Tace**, who is about fifty and does not use his mill for four months in the year and is one of the thirty-one households behind the bank at the third-of-the-way place, four hundred and forty foot of which is about a quarter of a mile up the cut from where he can see it from his own door.

He has a wagon's worth of kerb and coping in his yard. It came on a cart out of the lower district in the last week of the third month for a job at a landing office and it did not go on the job, and it has been standing against the south wall of the store since, under a tar, and nobody has asked him about it and nobody has wanted it.

He got up at about the fifth hour on the ninth of the fourth month, which he does, and he did not go to the mill, and he took a pole and a line out of a shed and walked four hundred and forty foot of bank.

---

He had looked at the face of that bank for nine years and had never once written a number about it.

He wrote one on the back of his own paper. The paper is a sheet of about a third of a foolscap with a man's name at the top of it and about four hundred and forty foot of outer face in it and **nine dates in his own hand on which he saw the face move, two of which he can prove and seven of which he cannot, and he has written down which is which and why.** It is the paper he wrote in the sixth year and sent to the district works four times and that came back four times, and it has been on a bench in a hired bay in his own yard since the twenty-eighth of the third month, and about thirty people have seen it and nine of them can read it.

He put the pole in at the third-of-the-way mark on the twenty-first of the second month — no, he could not put the pole in at the third-of-the-way mark on the twenty-first of the second month, because he was not here on the twenty-first of the second month and nobody had been.

What he had was a mark he had cut in the coping himself, in the second month of the year before last, with a cold chisel, because a miller who has been looking at a bank for nine years cuts his own mark on it, and he had cut two, and the one at the third-of-the-way mark was about four foot in from the coping and he had cut a small cross into the stone.

He put the pole against the cross and read the coping.

Then he walked nine foot along and did it again, and again at about two foot out, and he did eleven of them from the third-of-the-way mark for about thirty foot each way, and he wrote a figure against each one on the back of his own paper, and at the end he stood in the middle of nine feet of beaten earth with a pole in his hand and did not go to the mill for another two hours.

**At one place, the toe of the bank has gone about nine inches in seven weeks.**

Not the face. The face is where the face is and the face is eleven feet high and it is eleven feet high where a crew of five men and a boy left it on the twenty-one of the second month and it has not moved a hand's breadth at the coping anywhere he walked, and the coping and the kerb are on the bed and the bed is sound.

The toe is the bottom nine inches of a bank, where the water goes underneath, and a man in a good coat will tell you a toe is the only part of a bank that matters, and this district has about four hundred and forty foot of toe that nobody has measured since about the year the district was last mapped.

He wrote it on the back of his own paper, with the day on it, in the middle of the space under the fourth of the nine dates, and it says:

> *Ninth of the fourth month. At about two foot back from the third-of-the-way mark, the toe is nine inches lower than the cross I cut in the second month of the year before last. I put a pole to it eleven times between the cross and thirty foot either way and I got between eight inches and ten inches and nine is what eleven of them say. A. Tace. The ninth of the fourth month.*

He looked at it for a while, and then he did the thing he has done about nine hundred times, which is to go and look at the price of something, and he went and looked at the tar against the south wall of the store.

---

**There are about nine tons of kerb and coping in that yard and it is his and it cost him nothing to bring here and it is worth nothing to him where it is.**

That is the whole of the arithmetic and he did it in about nine minutes, standing by a tar, on the ninth of the fourth month, with the water in the third week.

The ford is four hundred and forty foot up the cut from the third-of-the-way mark and about a hundred and forty yards from his own gate. It is the seventh work of eleven on a page from ninety-one years back. It has not been on anybody's list for about thirty years. Its two depth posts were taken out of their sockets in the fourth week of the second month and nine stones were taken out of the throat, and both were stacked neatly on the low side, and a man with a cart and a boy did it, and nobody has asked him to stop and he has not put them back.

The ford fills. That is what a ford does.

He had nine tons of the stone that would put it right, and his own carts go up past it twice a week with everything the mill buys and everything the mill sells, and he could have it at the third-of-the-way mark by the second week of this month with no money changing hands anywhere at all.

And that was the thing he stood in his own yard thinking about for nine minutes, and it is the thing this whole matter is about, and he got to the bottom of it at about the seventh hour with his hand on a tar, alone, exactly the way he got to the bottom of the other thing in about six years with nobody to ask.

**A gift is money with no figure on it.**

Not a discount. Not a favour. A gift. Money with no figure on it, handed over, and the person who hands it over has put their name on a thing they cannot ask anything about later, and the person who takes it has taken something they cannot say what it cost.

He worked it out like this, and he is not a clever man and he is about fifty and he has been a miller for twenty-two years.

"If I give them the stone then in about four years there will be nine tons of kerb in a ford on the Slade Cut and there will be no price on it anywhere, and the only thing anybody will be able to say about who put it there is that a miller did, and I will be the only person on this river who can be asked about that ford at all, and I will not be able to answer a word of it, because I gave it. **A thing with a figure on it can be argued with. A thing with no figure on it can only be found.**"

So he went and found a figure, and it took him about four minutes, and it is this:

Nine tons of kerb and coping, his own, delivered, is **seven shillings and sixpence.**

Not the market price. The market price for nine tons of kerb and coping off a landing in the lower district in the ninth year is about one pound four and eight, and he knows that to a penny and has known it for twenty-two years. Seven shillings and sixpence is not what the stone is worth. **Seven shillings and sixpence is what it costs to put the stone in the throat of that ford with five men and a boy for two days, at the rate the Company pays, which is eight pence a man and five for the boy, which is forty-five pence a day on the five and one.**

He worked that out twice on the back of his own paper and got it twice, and he wrote it down, and then he sat down on a millstone and had about a quarter of an hour in which he did not get up.

Seven shillings and sixpence is the whole of the ford. Two days of a crew. That is all the thing has ever cost anybody, and he has known that for thirty years, and there are about nine hundred and forty names in a book four hundred yards away that pay a shilling a quarter to keep the names dry.

And the Slade Cut Company has four shillings a year.

**Four shillings a year, and the bill is seven shillings and sixpence, and the Company is three shillings and sixpence short of the cheapest thing anybody has ever tried to do on that cut, and three shillings and sixpence is the rent of that store for about a year and a quarter.**

---

He went to the shed at the Slade end at about the ninth hour because he had been holding a figure since the seventh hour and it had been sitting on him.

There were nine men in it. Halla Wray was there with a list and her brother was there with a barrow, and Rennick Adley was there because he is always there, and the book-keeper had a sheet of foolscap out and no pen in his hand.

"Miller," said Rennick Adley. "You have had a piece of paper in your yard for six years and I have had it on a bench in your yard for ten days, and I have read it four times, and there is a figure on the back of it now that I did not see on the front, and I would like to know when you wrote it."

"This morning."

"Then I am not going to touch it, and I am going to say why, and I am going to say it before I have had any of it, because that is a thing I learned in the third month of this year from a woman of sixty-four and I am not going to be the one who breaks it.

"**A name at the top of a paper about a bank is a name anybody can ask about the bank.** I have your name against an entry in that book since the sixth of the third month and I have said out loud that it was findable and it was honest, and findable is the only thing anybody has ever wanted out of a book, and a figure on the back of your own paper is a thing you can be asked about for the rest of your life and you wrote it yourself, and I am not going to hand it to a man with a tray."

"I wrote it for myself," said Aven Tace. "I wrote it because I have been looking at that toe for nine years and nobody has ever put a number on it, and I did not write it for you and I am not going to read it out, and you may have the front of the paper and not the back of it and that is not a clever thing and it is the only thing I have got."

---

Then he said the thing he had come for, and it was four sentences and it was to a foreman and not to a book-keeper.

"**Seven shillings and sixpence.** Nine tons of kerb and coping out of my own yard, delivered, and five men and a boy for two days at your rate, which is forty-five pence a day on the five and one, and I have done that sum twice this morning on the back of my own paper and it is the sum of the whole of that ford and has been for thirty years.

"And I am not giving it to you. **I have had it in my yard under a tar for a fortnight and I could have it in the throat of that ford by the second week of this month for nothing at all, and I worked out this morning why I am not going to do it, and it took me nine minutes, and I am going to say it because I worked out the same kind of thing once before on my own in about six years and nobody ever told me I was right, and I am not doing it twice.**

"A gift is money with no figure on it. If I put nine tons of stone in that ford for nothing then there is no price on it anywhere, and the only person who can be asked about that ford is me, and I will not be able to answer a word of it, because a thing with a figure on it can be argued with and a thing with no figure on it can only be found."

Halla Wray said: "**Say the price out loud again and say it in a form, because I have a condition and I have had it since the thirtieth of the last month and you are the first person in about a year who has come at me with a figure instead of a favour.**"

"Seven shillings and sixpence, delivered, and my own carts, and I do not want it out of the book and I do not want it out of a man in a city, and I do not want it credited in that store against the rent."

"Nobody in ninety-one households has got seven shillings and sixpence."

"**I know that. I have four hundred yards of your store and a shilling a quarter and I know it better than you do, because that four shillings a year is the whole of the money this Company has ever had in sixty years and it goes on keeping about nine hundred and forty names dry, and I am not going to have you take three shillings and sixpence out of a store to put a ford right, because the day that book is wet is the day names go off it and there is nobody to write them out again.**"

---

It went on for another half hour and nothing was agreed to, and nine men in a shed are not a decision.

At the end of it Halla Wray said the thing that made the half hour worth having.

"I cannot take it off you and I am not going to try. **Write it on my list.** Nine tons, seven shillings and sixpence, a miller at the mill yard, and a date — the second week of this month, and not one day past it, because that is the last week before the water. And I am going to write under it that I have not got the money, so that the list does not lie, and if the money comes then the work is done and if it does not then the list says in my own hand that it was not done and why."

"You will write that anybody can read."

"**Anybody that can read can read a line with a figure on it, and about a third of the people who come to the bay cannot read a word of it, and Rennick will read it out, and he reads it out twice.** And I want it on the list in that state because a list of works with nothing wrong on it is the only paper this Company has ever had and it is the reason nobody has ever been able to be frightened of it."

---

The notice came up the road on the tenth of the fourth month, in the afternoon, and it came to the mill yard because the mill yard is where the bay is.

It is a sheet in a clerk's hand, a third of a foolscap, with a day on it, and it says that four persons at the north of the ford were heard at a court on a flood terrace on the fifth of the fourth month, and that they are parties to an application and cannot be un-named, and that one of them is the person at the second house, one the person at the third, one the person at the fourth, **and one the woman at the first house who was in her own door on the last day of the third month.**

It has four names or descriptions on it and no price and no office at the top of it beyond a court's, and there is no mark on it of any kind, and it is a piece of paper about four people and it is the first piece of paper in sixty years that has ever been about anybody in that lane.

Tavi Venn read it out.

She is seventeen and she can read and she is not paid and cannot be, and she came up the towpath in the morning with the sheet in her bag because somebody at the Slade end said there was a notice and she said she would read it, which is the whole of what she does and the whole of what she is paid, which is nothing.

She read it out once at the ordinary speed and then she read it out again slower, because about a third of the bay cannot read a paragraph and a notice about four people who cannot read it is worse than a notice about four people who can.

**She did not say what it meant.** She said that when somebody asked her, and she said it in the ordinary way, in about four words, and nine people in that room had been spoken to by a person sitting next to them without being asked whether they wanted it, and the number of people who asked her what it meant was four, and the number of people who did not ask was about twenty-six.

And then the man whose name is at the top of the paper about the bank asked her the fourth question, which is *who read this to you, and do they get paid if you sign*, and she has heard that question asked twice in her life and she has never once been asked it by anybody it was written for.

"Nobody read it to me," she said. "I read it. **I am not paid and I am not going to be, and I am not going to tell anybody what it means, and if somebody wants a name in a box about that ford I will say no and I will not care whose name it is, including mine, and that is a condition and it is mine and I have had it since the first month.**"

And Aven Tace, who is about fifty and is a miller and who wrote a paper with his own name at the top of it in the sixth year and worked out alone for six years that a name at the top of a paper about a bank is a name anybody can ask about the bank, looked at a piece of paper about four people that had come four miles up a road to a mill yard and had been read out twice in a cold room by a girl of seventeen for nothing.

"You are the person who comes," he said.

"I am the person who comes. **And it is the whole of the cost and I am not going to be thanked for it in a room, because the moment I am thanked in a room I am a person who reads for thanks, and then in about four years somebody will ask me to do it for nothing because I have been thanked.**"

So the tar came off about nine tons of kerb and coping in a mill yard on the tenth of the fourth month, and it went back on, because there was nowhere for it to go, and the list in a tool shed four hundred yards up the road said seven shillings and sixpence and a date and, in a foreman's own hand underneath, that the money had not come.
