# Chapter 134: What Is on the Ground

The chamber is two rooms and a passage, about two hundred miles from the table where the fourth counter is, and it is up a stair that does not turn, and there is a window in the outer room that looks at a roof.

**Vaunt Oris is a notary of House Oris in his own right. He is fifty-one or two and he has been in that chair for nineteen years and he reads human inheritance records as slowly and as carefully as that city reads a river return.** There are nine things in the outer room and none of them is a machine: a table, two chairs, a lamp, a rack of about four hundred blanks in a wooden case, a press, a shelf, a tray, a bowl, and a book about ninety years old with a crack in the spine.

There is a fire in the grate in the last week of the fifth month because there is a fire in the grate in the last week of the fifth month.

He has not repeated one sentence in thirteen months and nobody in this matter has asked him to and he is aware of how lucky that is.

---

The request came up the stair on the second of the sixth month and it was brought by a trade assessor, who is not his clerk and is nobody's clerk, and who stands between a house and a guild as a matter of trade and has said so himself.

It is four lines long and it is from the practice and not from a person.

> *The practice is asked to be satisfied that the persons named in the book of the Company working the Slade Cut are the persons of that Company, so that the guarantee may be certified. The practice is also asked to be satisfied that the Company has accepted the duty, the acceptance being on the face of an instrument drawn in a district four hundred and thirty miles away and placed there on the twelfth of the fourth month.*
>
> *A certificate is desired. The practice is the oldest water-notary practice on this reach and holds the concession with which it certifies.*

He read it twice and then read the second paragraph again, because the second paragraph is the one he could have sealed in about four seconds and the first is the one that matters.

"Two things," he said. "Say them again and I will tell you which one I have."

The trade assessor said them again. He listened with his hand flat on the table.

"The second one is true," he said. "I can certify that a body which has been given a duty and cannot refuse has accepted the duty. That is not an opinion, it is arithmetic. **A body cannot say no. A body that is given a duty and cannot say no has accepted it. Nine words on a piece of paper in a hired bay are the whole of the acceptance, and nobody in ninety-one households said them, and the paper itself says that the acceptance does not evidence a consent, which is the only reason the nine words are honest.** So the second one is true, and it is worth nothing, and I can put a seal on it tomorrow."

"And the first one."

"The first one I have to go and look at," said Vaunt Oris, "**and I am not going to look at it from a piece of paper.**"

---

Here is what the trade assessor is, because he is the hinge of the whole business and nobody has ever written him down.

He stands between a house and a guild. When a guild wants a house's water, he takes the guild's paper to the house. When a house wants a guild's road, he takes the house's paper to the guild. He is paid by neither and is retained by both and is the only person in the arrangement who is paid by both without being anybody's servant, and he has been doing it for twenty-six years, and he is about fifty-one.

"You have asked for one thing and it is not a document," he said. "Ask it properly, in the words you would use in front of a witness, because I am going to have to write it down and I am going to write it down as you say it and not as you mean it."

"I am asking what is on the ground."

"That is a sentence a man says when he is about to make a nuisance of himself."

"Then I will say it longer." He did not raise his voice and he has never once had to. "**I am asking what is on the ground, meaning: I will not put a seal on a statement about a book that I have not been shown, and I have seen about four hundred instruments a year for nineteen years and I have not sealed one I had not seen. So I am asking what is on the ground, and I would be obliged if it came in an answerable form, and if it does not come in an answerable form then I will say no and it will be my no and not my House's.**"

The trade assessor wrote that down. It took him a while.

"You do not know whether it will be your no or your House's."

"No," said Vaunt Oris. "**That is the honest position and I have been in it for about nine days already and I have not said one word about it to anybody, and I would be glad if it were a matter of a week.**"

---

The answer came up the road in nine days by three different routes at once, which is what happens to a question nobody owns, and it took about eleven minutes to hear and about nine seconds to write down, and it is this.

There is a company of about ninety-one households working a public cut about four hundred and forty foot long. It has no head, is not on the register of bodies, and may not be made a party.

It keeps a book.

The book is in a hired stone store at a shilling a quarter and it has **nine hands in it** and **about nine hundred and forty names** in it and it goes back **about sixty years**. There is **no heading at the top of it.** It has **no list of persons in it** and it says on its first leaf, in a hand about six years old, with a day on it, what the book is and what it is not.

About four hundred of the nine hundred and forty people in it are dead.

There is no figure of households in it. The figure of about ninety-one households and about sixty children under sixteen is a count taken off the book and written on the back of a person's hand, and the person cannot read a paragraph.

No person has ever been chosen from the book. It is not a rota. It is a record of work and it has never once been a list of anybody.

And there is a second thing, which the trade assessor put in a different voice, because it came in a letter from a different person and it is the only part of the answer a notary in this room is actually interested in.

**On the sixth day of the third month, for the first time in sixty years, a name was written against an entry in that book, and the name is of a person and not of a crew and not of a debtor, and the man who wrote it wrote it on purpose so that the Company would be easy to compel itself, and he has said so out loud in a room of about thirty people, and he put it in a book with no heading so that nobody could tell what it was for.**

Vaunt Oris read that twice and then said the thing that is the whole of this chapter, and it took him about nine sentences and there were three people in the room.

"**A book with names in it is not a list of persons.** I have read human inheritance records for nineteen years. In nineteen years I have never once been asked what a company's book was, and I have certified about four hundred instruments a year, and not one of them has ever been about a cooperative, because a cooperative cannot produce a person to be certified about and so nobody has ever needed to know.

"That is the whole of my profession. I am a person who is asked what a thing is and who goes and looks at it before he says. **And the request on that sheet is not asking me what a thing is. It is asking me to say that a book is a room, and that a room is the Company, and that everyone who ever walked into that room is still in it.** About four hundred of them are not. And about nine hundred and forty names went in over sixty years by nine different hands who were writing down work, and not one of them was writing down a list, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a record and a register, and it is about eleven seconds wide, and it is the width of a person standing up deciding which one they are doing."

"And if you certify it," said the trade assessor, "the certificate is true."

"**The certificate is true and that is the whole of the danger and I am going to have to think about it for longer than I would like.**"

He thought about it for about eleven days, and in that time he did three things, and only two of them were about the case.

The first was to write the answer down in his own hand, because he is going to need it, in a book of his own with no office on it, with the day at the top, and he read it back and did not improve it.

The second was to send for the concession book.

The third was to go down the stair, into the passage, and stand in the passage on purpose, and say out loud, to nobody, the thing he had said in a chamber in another city fourteen months ago and had then told a human court he was not asking it to do anything about.

"**Dragons hold the same set of obligations for six or seven lifetimes and a human has one.** That is held in my House as a working opinion and not as a doctrine, and I have said it in that room twice, and I have never once had to say what follows it, and what follows it is this: **we can keep a promise for six lifetimes and a cooperative can keep one for a term and be wound up in a quarter, and the reason the human arrangement is the right one and the reason I am frightened of it are the same sentence.**"

And then he went back up the stair and asked the practice whether it would accept an answer in two parts, and was told that an answer in two parts is an answer in one part with a refusal in the middle of it, and that a notary of a house who answers in parts is a notary of a house who has refused and is describing the refusal, and that this is known as a **split instrument** and that there is a column for it.

There is a column for it. Nobody has ever used it in this house in nineteen years, and Vaunt Oris had read about it and had never expected to, and he stood in the passage with the practice's clerk in front of him and asked what happens to a concession when the half that is refused is the half the concession is for.

The clerk said that it did not go to a court and did not go to a House and did not go anywhere, and that it went in the book of concessions, in the entry for the reach, with the name of the person who gave it up, and that this was the practice's rule and not the law and had been since before the clerk was born.

"And the reason for the rule is."

"That a concession is held and not owned," said the clerk, "**and that a House which can lose one without saying who lost it will lose it quietly and will lose the next one quietly and the next, and a House that is going to lose one anyhow might as well have it written down.**"

"That is a very good rule and I have never once in nineteen years had cause to think about it."

"You have not had cause because you have not refused anything."

He said that last part without any weight on it at all, and then went away, and did not know for about nine days that it was the sentence that was going to cost his House a hundred and forty years, and would not have cared if he had known, which is also true and which he never found out either way.

---

The practice tried to help him and it took three days and it was done by two people who were being kind.

The first was the practice's own clerk, who is about thirty-four and has been in it nine years and is not a notary and does the drafting, and who came up the stair on the fourth of the sixth month with a rewritten request in a fair hand and put it on the table face down.

"I have redrafted it. **The certificate now reads: that the book produced is a true record of the members of the said Company, being the persons entered in it.** It is the second thing on that board at the counter four hundred miles away, I have looked it up, and it is a thing tables certify every day of the week. It does not ask you about the ground. It does not ask you about ninety-one households or nine hundred and forty names or four hundred dead men. It asks you about a book."

Vaunt Oris turned it round and read it and put it back face down.

"That is exactly the sentence I was going to write myself," he said, "**and I had it in my own book on the fourth day, and I want you to understand that I would have arrived at it, and that the fact that your office arrived at it first does not make it somebody else's.**"

"Will you seal it?"

"No."

"Then I do not know what I did wrong."

"You did nothing wrong and that is the difficulty," said Vaunt Oris. "**Nobody in this house did anything wrong. The request came in a form I cannot seal and then, four days later, in a form I can, and both forms were drawn by careful people, and the second one is shorter, and it is shorter because it has left out the only question there is.**"

---

The second was the House.

It did not come up the stair. It came down about a week later as a sheet of about nine lines in a hand that is not his and is not anybody's in that room, and it was not signed, because it was a seat's paper and a seat's paper does not have a person at the foot of it, and it was put on his table and left there and taken away again four days later and left there again, twice, which is what a House does to a piece of paper it cannot get answered.

It said, in substance, and he read it about four times and has never quoted any of it:

that the guarantee is a good instrument and is offered without charge and would stand the water of that cut for the length of the works.

that the practice is the oldest on that reach and the concession with which it certifies is not a formality and is the only reason anybody in that district has ever been able to have a scale instrument put in a hand at all.

that the request as drawn is a reasonable request and a clerk of a notary's table four hundred miles away would seal the redraft without reading it twice.

that a person in this chamber who cannot seal a request his own House has put forward is not being asked to be difficult, and a person who finds a difficulty in a House's own paper is doing his duty and will be told so in the practice's own book.

and the last line, which is the one he wrote out in his own book with the day on it, in full, in his own hand, because he was told to and he has a practice of writing down the things he is told:

> *Nothing in this paper requires the practice to answer. The paper is not a question. An institution that sends a thing to be answered and does not require an answer has sent a thing to be complied with.*

He read that four times.

**He had said, in a room in another city fourteen months before this, that an institution is not told things by its notaries in the middle of an afternoon, and that it is told in about four years, by somebody else, at more expense, and that his question had cost his House nothing.**

He had been right about the timing. It took about nine days.

He had been wrong about the price, and he had been wrong about the price for about thirteen months before that without ever once thinking about it, and the price was not a house and was not a fund and was not a wing.

**The price was a person, and it was going to be himself, and it was going to be his own name at the foot of a page in a book of about a hundred and forty years, and the House was not going to know it had happened for about four years either.**

He did not answer the sheet. He left it on the table for nine days, which is longer than a notary of nineteen years would normally leave a seat's paper lying about, and then he wrote on the bottom of it, in his own hand, four words, and sent it down the stair.

The four words were: **Noted, and not done.**
