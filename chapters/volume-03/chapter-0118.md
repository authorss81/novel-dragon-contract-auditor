# Chapter 118: A House for Sixty Years

Bel Skell is about seventy-one and cannot read a paragraph and has not pretended to for fifty-five years. She is in the fourth house at the north of the ford in a lane of channel-side houses, and there are four houses of Skells at the north of the ford and has been as long as anybody can remember, and there are about nine houses in that lane altogether, and the water is on one side and about nine feet of beaten earth between the water and the doors.

She has been in that lane sixty years. Her mother was in it before her. There is a man in the second house called Roan Skell who is her brother's son and is about fifty-eight, and he is the only one of the four of them who can read a paragraph, and he has never once been asked to read anything in sixty years because there has never been anything to read.

Nothing has ever been written about any of those four houses. Not by a court, not by a district, not by a company, not by a woman with a barrow. There is no name of a Skell anywhere in a record of this matter. There is a Skell in a map of nineteen years back as *Skell, T., household of nine, north of the ford*, and there is a Skell on a page from ninety-one years back, and the two have never once been put in the same sentence by anybody including her.

---

It happened on the thirtieth day of the third month, in the morning, in flat light.

There was a two-wheel and a cart on it and it came into the ford at the shallow place, which is where it has always gone, and **the shallow place is not there any more** because the nine stones that made the throat of it were taken out in the fourth week of the second month and put on a stack on the low side, and nobody in this lane knew that, and nobody in this lane knew there were nine stones in the throat of the ford at all.

The cart went in up to the axle and the boy went in to about his waist, which is what happens to a boy of fifteen in about two foot of moving water on a stone bottom, and he got out.

He is out. He is on the low side with his leg out straight in front of him and his hands flat on the earth, and he cannot stand on that leg, and he has been lying in about nine feet of beaten earth since about the seventh hour, and it is now about the ninth.

---

Bel Skell went out at about the seventh hour, and what she did about it in the first half hour is the ordinary thing, and the ordinary thing took about forty minutes.

She got him out of the wet. She got her coat off and put it under him, which is a thing her mother did, twice, and which has never once turned out to be a bad idea. She got nine people out of nine doors by standing in the lane and saying one sentence, which was that a cart is in the ford and a boy is out of it and I want nine of you and I do not want a fuss, and she got nine of them.

Then she had the rest of it, which is the part that is about a boy and not about a leg.

"He is my sister's boy," she said, to the lane, in the first four minutes. "**Not by the book.** His mother was Sef Skell and she died in the fifth year and she married a Bray at the fourth house below the mill and the boy has gone by Bray ever since because that is where his father is, and he has slept at my house since he was about four and he is at my house now and he is fifteen and he is a Bray and he is a Skell and I am the only person in this lane who could tell you which and I have told you inside four minutes because I am not going to be asked for it later.

"I want that said now and I want it said out loud, and then I am going to say the rest of it, and I have not had a fuss and I am not going to get one."

Somebody said, "Bel, there is a doctor at the second house below the mill."

"Yes there is. And I cannot get him without saying a name."

That stopped the lane for a second.

"Now listen to me, because I have thought about this for about two hours lying on my own bed with my coat on and this is the first time in sixty years I have had to think about anything at all and I am going to say it straight and I am not going to be talked out of it by anybody.

"**I have never given a name to a piece of paper.** Not for a court, not for a district, not for a company, not for anybody. And I have got a reason for that and the reason is that I cannot read a paragraph, and if my name is on a paper then there is a paper about my leg and I will never know what is on it and somebody will.

"And the girl of seventeen came up this lane yesterday and read a paper out twice and would not tell anybody what it meant and told me in about four minutes that she was not going to ask a man's name and not going to put one anywhere, and I stood in my own door and heard her say it and I have thought about that woman for a day and a half.

"So that is my position and it is a good position and it has kept me out of a box for sixty years, and I am giving it up this morning, in the open, in about nine of you listening, and I am giving it up for a leg.

"**Say Bel Skell.** Not my house. Not the fourth house. *Bel Skell*, of the fourth house at the north of the ford, and the boy is my sister's boy, and he is fifteen, and the name of his father is on the ninth house down and I am not going to say his father's name to fetch a doctor because his father is not here and I am not going to put a dead woman's name on a paper to fetch a doctor.

"And I want it noticed that I have just said a name out loud, on purpose, with nine witnesses, without anybody asking me, and I am about to be a person. **That is what is happening to me this morning and I am not going to pretend it is a small thing, because I have been a house for sixty years and being a house has been the safest thing I have ever been.**"

---

The doctor came at about the tenth hour and he was the man from the second house below the mill and he is about fifty and he has been the doctor for this reach for about twenty-six years, and he looked at the leg for about a quarter of an hour in front of nine people and he did not say anything about the future, which Bel Skell has since described as the best thing anybody has done in that lane in her lifetime.

"I have seen this leg before," he said. "You will sit up on that wall tonight. Somebody has to sit with him and it will not be me."

"Then I will sit with him," said Bel Skell, "and I will sit with him for as long as it takes and I am seventy-one and I am not going to be asked twice about it, and that is the last thing anybody is going to get out of me today."

---

The rest of the morning was the ordinary part and it went on longer than the extraordinary part, which it does.

There is no count of that ford and there never has been. **A count of a ford is a number of crossings and not a number of people**, and a ford that is crossed on a plank and not on the stones has no count at all, and there is nobody in this district to ask for one, and nobody has asked for one in sixty years, and the only person who has ever come near it is a clerk four miles away who told a magistrate in a room in the second month that a headcount cannot be produced from a count of a bar by anybody.

So the lane does its own counting, which is a woman of about thirty-one with two children standing in the middle of the beaten earth with her arms folded and saying *how many* out loud, twice, in about two hours, and getting the same number both times, which is thirty-one people in nine houses, and about eleven of them not in the lane that day because of where they were, and the number of people in the lane is not a number anybody has to answer for because the lane is a lane and the lane is nobody.

Bel Skell listened to the whole of it lying on the wall with her bad leg up on a sack, and she counted, because she counts.

---

At about the fourth hour in the afternoon somebody said, *that woman is walking up from the Slade end.*

Halla Wray came up the lane at about the fourth hour, and she had walked it, and it is two hours each way and she is fifty-one and she has a bad knee in cold weather and she did not tell anybody she had a bad knee in cold weather and has not since.

She had a list. She wrote it in the lane on the back of a sheet of a foreman's list, sitting on the wall opposite Bel Skell's, with about nine people standing in front of her, and it took her about nine minutes.

> *Slade Cut, north of the ford. The thirtieth day of the third month.*
> *One. The two posts of the ford, to be put back in the bed, in the holes, to the depth as they stood.*
> *Two. The nine stones of the throat, to be put back and bedded.*
> *Three. The ford, to be looked at as a work, and not as a place where a cart went in.*
>
> *H. Wray, foreman, Slade. The thirtieth day of the third month.*

She put her own name at the foot of it and a date against her name, and then she read it out, once, to the lane, in the plainest words she had.

"I have held that work twice this year. **I am not asking this lane to do it and I am not asking the district to do it, and there is no money in it and there has been no money in it since the third week of the twelfth month, and I am not going to stand here and tell you it costs nothing, because it costs three pounds and something for every day of it and I have watched where that came from the last time and I am not going to do it again.**

"And I am a person at the foot of it and my term ran out in the third week of this month and nobody asked me to hold it again, and they have not asked me this afternoon either, and I am going to say that out loud because if I do not say it somebody will take it that I was asked, and I was not.

"And I am not going to put a household of yours on it and I am not going to put a name at the top of it, and I will tell you why: **because a name at the top of a paper about a bank is a name anybody can ask about the bank, and I have been doing that to Aven Tace for nine years and I did not know that was a thing you could be caught doing until three weeks ago when a man told me in his own doorway, and there is not one of you going to get that done to you by me.**"

Then she said the thing that put her on the list, and she said it before anybody asked her.

"There is a boy of fifteen in this lane with a leg and I am putting the ford on that list and I am not putting the boy on it, and if anybody in this lane has been putting the boy on it since the seventh hour this morning then they have been doing the same thing to him that a district does to a parish, and I would like to know who and I would like them to stop."

---

The lane said the second question at about the fifth hour, and it was said by the woman of about thirty-one with the two children, who had done the counting twice in the morning, and she said it in the ordinary way, the way anybody asks a question in a lane.

"Whose name is on it."

It is the first question a lane asks about a piece of paper and it is the right one and it is the one nobody has ever been able to answer in a lane in this matter before tonight.

"Foreman Wray's," said Bel Skell. "And the doctor's name is not on it, and mine is not on it, and the boy's is not on it, and the man who took the stones out has not got a paper on this lane and none of us knows his name was ever asked for, and the woman of the fourth door who told the girl of seventeen where the stones went has not got her name on anything either.

"And that is not a good accident and I want somebody in this lane to stop treating it as a good accident, and I am the one who is going to say it, because it was me and not anybody else who worked it out, and I have been working it out since about the tenth hour this morning lying on a wall.

"**Nothing has ever been written about these four houses in sixty years. By anybody. And I have spent two days thinking that was a protection and it is not a protection, it is a luck.** It is a luck that nobody in this district has ever needed a name out of this lane. Sixty years, and it has not needed one, and that is not because anybody protected us. **It is because nobody has ever wanted anything from here**, and a lane nobody wants anything from is the safest place on this river, and we have been standing in the safest place on this river for sixty years and we did not know it and we did not do it.

"And this morning a cart went into the ford and this afternoon a foreman came up the lane with a list of works, and now there are about nine people on this lane who have heard their own lane said out loud as a place where a thing happened, and about four of them have heard somebody ask whose name is on it.

"And a house that is known to be a house where a thing happened is findable. **Not for that. For ever, and for everything, and not only for that.** And in about four years there will be a form and a clerk will send it to this lane, and it will say *the incident of*, and the number of the houses will be in it, and the boy will be in it, and he is a boy and he is fifteen, and there is not one form in this empire that a boy can be kept out of if a person is named somewhere else in it."

Nobody said anything for a bit and about four of the nine went inside, which is the ordinary way of ending a thing in a lane.

"**And there is no way to have the name and not the luck.** That is what I have got and I am seventy-one and I am going to say it and then I am going to stop for the day. You can have a name and be findable, or you can have no name and be a lane that nothing has ever happened in. There is no third shape and nobody has found one and I have looked for about a day and a half from a wall with my leg on a sack."

Then she said the last of it, to the foreman, and it is the sentence that the fourth household at the north of the ford is going to be asked about in four days' time by a clerk who does not know that it was said in a lane.

"And I am going to say the thing that nobody has said, and then that is me for the day.

"**There is a name on the page from ninety-one years back.** I have known it since the girl of seventeen read it out in the store and I did not understand a word of the reading and I understood all of the reading. *Tarn Skell, of the Slade.* And there has been a Skell at the north of the ford as long as there has been a lane, and there are four houses of us, and I am seventy-one and I was not born and my mother was not born.

"**So a name is findable and a family is not.** That is the whole of what I have got out of two days on a wall. You can find that name in a map of nineteen years back and in a book in a store and you can find four doors in this lane, and you cannot find out whose grandfather Tarn Skell was, and there is nobody alive in this lane who could tell you, and including me, and I am the one who is most likely to be able to and cannot, because I was not born.

"**Skell is a list of four doors. Tarn Skell's son is not a thing anybody can look up.** And that is the good half of it and the bad half at the same time, and I have not been able to get them apart in two days, and I am going to sit on this wall for another hour and then I am going to go in and look after a boy."
