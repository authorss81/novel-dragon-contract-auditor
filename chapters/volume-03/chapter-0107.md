# Chapter 107: A Copy of a Copy

The Mosswake Roll Court is a low building on a flood terrace with a public bench outside it and a clerk's window with a sill, and the window is about four feet off the ground and about two feet wide, and Ivet Sarn has worked at it for nine years.

Above the sill there is a wall, and the wall is about nine feet of plaster, and the plaster is the only wall in that district that a person can stand in front of and read without a light. It has the order of the court sitting in room four of the eighteenth of the seventh month on it, transcribed, in a copyist's hand, five words on the front and no office. It has a certification of the fourth week of the eighth month on it, transcribed, five words on the front and no office. It has the answer of the eighth of the third month on it in one side in five points. It has seven numbered paragraphs of what the district has to do about it, which is almost nothing, and it says so. It has four lines written in the first person about a clerk who did not ask a question in the sixth year, and the four lines are dated. And at the foot of the whole wall, in a hand about half the size of the rest, there is one line that is the only instruction on it:

> *A wall is a thing that a person looks at. I have been putting things on walls for nine years and this is the first time I have written on one what it cannot do, and it is the first time I have written a sentence for anybody who is not in this room, and there is nobody in this room, and I have noticed that while writing it.*

And beside the window, on a board, in chalk, in letters about four inches high, there are the figures, and the figures have not moved in about a year and did not move in the first week of the second month of this year and are not going to be restated in this chapter, because they are on the board and the board is nine feet away and anybody can walk up to it.

---

He came up the road on the fourth day of the second month, at about the eleventh hour, and he was not alone, and that is the first thing she wrote down.

He came up the road with a copyist. The copyist is a woman of about twenty-four with a satchel and a hand that is kept better than his and who stood about four feet behind him the whole time and did not come forward once, and when Ivet Sarn asked a question she was answered by the man, and when he said anything that mattered he turned round and said it to her first, in about four words, before he said it to the sill.

Ivet Sarn has run this window for nine years and she has never asked a person why they have brought somebody, and she did not ask him, and she has written down that she did not ask him, and the reason she wrote it down is that about four months ago a woman at this sill asked her a question and she could not answer it and has had six months about it since, and the question was not this one and the six months are the same six months.

"Two things before you say anything," she said. "You are not carrying carriage money, I can see that from here, and if you want a thing done here you will want to pay for it, and the price of a copy of anything in this building is a shilling and the price of my time is not on any sheet and is not going to be on any sheet, and I am telling you that now so that you do not thank me later, because I have had four people thank me this year and two of them were being kind and one of them was being cruel and I could not tell which until the second month."

"I am not here for a copy," said the man. "I am here to ask you a question and I would like to buy an hour of your time and I have about a shilling and sixpence and I would like to put the shilling on the sill now and I would like to do it now and not at the end."

He put a shilling on the sill.

Ivet Sarn took it before she answered, because she has taken one three times now and has written down why she takes them, and the reason is on that wall in the first person and it is nine words long: *a person who has paid can answer without owing anybody.* A person who has not paid has to be grateful, and gratitude is a debt with no figure on it, and a debt with no figure on it is the kind that gets paid in land.

"Now you cannot thank me," she said, "and you cannot come back and say I was helpful, and if you tell anybody I was helpful I shall deny it and I shall be able to, because you have paid and I have been paid and neither of us is in the other's debt and that is the only good that is in this transaction."

Then she said: "Ask it."

---

He gave her a sheet. It is the district's own flood sheet of the ninth day of the first month, the one with two people's names in the box, and it has a list of works stapled to it with five men's names and a boy's on it and a foreman's name at the foot of it in a hand of about fifty.

She read it. She read it twice. And then she did a thing, and it is the thing that the whole afternoon was for, and she has done it nine thousand times and nobody has ever noticed her doing it except about four people in nine years, one of whom is a woman in a lane in a furnace town four hundred and forty miles away who put four questions on a stone and told the lane to take them.

She turned the sheet over, and took a sheet of foolscap out of a drawer, and wrote on it before she copied anything.

> *What I am reading. Written before I copy anything, because in nine years somebody will read a transcription and not this page, and in nine years somebody in this room will have a reason for having read the transcription first.*
>
> *I am reading a district flood sheet of the ninth of the first month with two human persons' names in a box and no body in it, and four lines on the face of it in which a keeper gives away the district's flood authority to those two persons and disclaims any power to compel the work. I am reading a list of works with a foreman's name at the foot of it. I am reading a note that says a keeper entered the second of the two names without asking him and has written on the face of her own sheet that she did.*
>
> *I am not reading any person's mind. I do not know whether the foreman wants this. I do not know whether the man has said no and been ignored, and the sheet says he has not been asked and does not say he has not said no. I am reading a piece of paper.*

Then she said, out loud, to a man at a sill and to a woman four feet behind him:

"**I am going to tell you how to do that, and I am not going to be kind about it, and I am not doing you a favour. You are going to do that for the rest of your life and you are going to do it in about nine rooms that do not know about each other and nobody will ever give you a form for it, and if you do not do it in a room where somebody else is standing you will do it in your own head and you will get it wrong about twice a year for the rest of your life, and you have been getting it wrong for fourteen months and I do not know that and it is not important.**

"Write down what you are reading. **Before you copy anything.** In your own words. In the first person if it is about a person. And then say who is not in the room.

"That is the whole of it. It takes about a minute and a half and it is the difference between a record and a copy, and I have been doing it for nine years and I have never once been asked where I got it, and I got it from a woman called Ansel who kept a roll in a temple in a town I have not been to since I was twenty-six, and she made me do it in the first month and she made me do it every day for four years and she never once explained it to me and I did not understand it for about two of those years."

---

Then she answered the question, and it took her about four minutes, and it is the answer and it is the reason the chapter is called what it is called.

"You want to know whether this district can get at the certified copy of its own instrument."

"Yes."

"No," said Ivet Sarn.

And then she said it properly, standing at a window on a flood terrace, in about nine minutes, and about four people on the terrace heard it and about two of them are in this matter.

"A scale instrument is certified by a notary's table. Not by this office and not by the district and not by me. **That is what makes it worth anything against a dragon party, and it is the only thing about it, and it is the reason a copy made by a clerk of a district is a piece of paper.** A copy made by a district clerk proves that a district clerk copied something. It does not prove what is in the thing, and it certainly does not prove anything about a water, because a water is not a party's instrument until a notary's table has said so in a form, and about eleven people in this district have never had that form and do not know it exists.

"And the certified copies are in Auremar. In a table, in a building, in a run. And the run is a number of a book, and the book is a book of a hundred and forty years of a table, and it is about four hundred miles from here, and I have not been in that building and neither have you, and neither, I am willing to bet, has the copyist.

"Now. **The thing you are actually asking me is whether a person may go and ask for it, and I am going to tell you that nobody may, and I am going to tell you the whole of the reason, and then I am going to tell you the part that I have not said to anybody and I am going to say it to two people at a window because you have both paid.**

"A person affected by an instrument may ask a notary's table a question about it. A person. Somebody, named, with a door they can be found at. **That is the whole of the door in this empire, and it is the only door, and it has been the same door for a hundred and forty years, and it is a door a person walks through, and not a body, and not a household, and not a letter, and not a claim, and a table that took a body's question would not be a notary's table, it would be a funnel.**

"Your company cannot ask. **A company is not a person.** I am not saying that as a technicality and I am not saying it because it is convenient to me this morning. I am saying it because a person is a thing that can be named and can be served and can be put in a room and can say no, and a company is none of those four, and if you let a company ask a question then what you have built is not a door, it is a funnel, and everything anybody in this empire has ever been afraid of has gone down a funnel and come out as a sentence with nobody's name at the top of it.

"So the Slade Cut Company cannot ask for its own instrument. Halla Wray cannot ask, because nothing in that instrument touches her or her brother or anybody on that bank. Adley cannot ask, and Doreth Ferrow cannot ask, and the boy cannot ask, and I cannot ask on any of your behalf, and **there is not one person in ninety-one households that instrument touches alive.**

"And that is where the road closes, and I am going to say the last of it now, and then I have said what I came to say.

"**A document about nobody cannot be answered. It cannot be refused. It cannot be held to be a forgery. It cannot be produced against a person in any proceeding, and it cannot bind a person who has not been heard, because it has not bound anybody at all. And it cannot be asked anything by anybody either.** There is no form for asking a table about a thing that touches nobody, because the form is built for a person, and a person has to be somebody, and nobody is not somebody.

"So your nine households, or your ninety-one, are not in a position to find out what they owe, and there is no clerk in this empire who can put them in a position, and the only thing that could is a person, and the only way a person gets to be a person in front of an instrument is by being touched by it, and **the only way anybody gets touched by an instrument in this matter is by being harmed by it, and nobody on that bank has been harmed yet, and if the bank goes in the fourth month then somebody will be, and I am telling you now, on the fourth of the second month, with a day on it, that I have said this, so that when it happens you cannot say nobody thought of it.**"

---

The man at the sill read her foolscap page back. He read it standing up, in about a minute, and he read it correctly, and then he asked her a question she was not expecting and which she has thought about since.

"You said the sentence on your wall protects a person from an instrument. And the thirty-one were sent a letter and a letter is not an instrument. That is your finding and it is on the wall in the first person and I have read it four times."

"It is."

"Then it cuts both ways and you have known that since the sixth month and you have not written it down," said Marek Kest. "**That sentence is the reason the thirty-one cannot ask what they were sent. And it is the reason the ninety-one cannot ask what they owe. It is a shield. And a shield is not a road, and the only way a shield becomes a road is if a person goes and is found, and finding a person is a different act and starts a case, and everybody in this matter has been spending fourteen months trying to make a shield into a road by handing out sheets at four pence, and a sheet with two boxes on it does not open a door, it only tells a person that a door is somewhere.**

"You have known that since the sixth month," said Ivet Sarn, "and I have known it about `None` for a year, because `None` protects a holder and does not protect a paper, and a man once explained that to me in about four minutes in a room and I did not understand one word of it and it took me eleven months."

"Yes," said Marek Kest.

"Write it on the wall," said Ivet Sarn.

"I am not going to write it on your wall."

"That was a reflex," said Ivet Sarn, "and it is nine years of a reflex and I am not blaming you for it. It is not mine and you are not mine and there is no office on this sill. And I am telling you that in about four years somebody is going to come along this road and ask a person at this window what she knows about a document about nobody, and there is going to be a wall, and **the wall is going to say the half of it that I wrote in the sixth month, and the half about nobody can ask anything is going to be on it because I am going to write it tonight, in the first person, with the day on it, and it is my wall.**"

---

The man with the satchel said one thing in the whole of that time and she wrote it down and he did not object.

"About forty minutes of that was four people finding out how to be careful in a room," said Tamsin Rook. "And I have been in about nine hundred rooms in fourteen months and I have watched every single one of them do it wrong, and this is the first one I have watched do it right, and the woman who did it has been doing it for nine years and there are about four people in this empire who know that she does it, and one of them is in a lane in a furnace town four hundred and forty miles from here and I have never met her and I have said her name in a room twice and I am saying it now, and I am sorry, I have said the lane before and I am saying the name and I have thought about it for four days and I am not taking it back."

"That is a name in a file," said Ivet Sarn, "and you have put it in one on purpose, in a room, at a window, in front of a man who writes things down."

"Yes."

"Then it is yours and not mine and I have not heard it and I am going to say that I have not heard it in the same way that I have not written it in my book, and I am going to put both of those in my book tonight, and the day on it, and I am not going to be the woman who unmasked a person because a copyist in a city was kind to a clerk in a district."

---

She wrote one line in her own book that night, and it is the first line she has written in a year that is not about a letter a court wrote, and it is short, and it is in the form the book uses.

> *The fourth of the second month, at about the ninth hour. Two persons from Auremar at the window, both paid, both unknown to this court, and I did not ask either of them anything about themselves and the reason is on the wall in the first person and it is nine words long.*
>
> *What I am to write down. That a scale instrument is certified by a notary's table and nowhere else, and that a copy made by a clerk of a district is a piece of paper. That a body may not ask a notary's table a question about anything, and that the reason is not a technicality, and that a person may only ask about an instrument that touches that person. **And that a document about nobody cannot be asked anything by anybody either, and that this is the second half of the sentence I wrote on that wall in the sixth month, and I have had it in my hand for eight months and did not look at it, and I have looked at it, and a person from a city stood at my window and said it back to me in about nine words and I have written down that he did.**
>
> *And that the magistrate sits on this terrace on the ninth, and that if these people come up here they will come as nine persons and not as ninety-one, and that there is a form in this district for nine persons and there is no form in this district for a company, and that I have known the second half of that for nine years and I have never once had to say it to anybody who could not do anything about it, and that about nine people in this district could not do anything about it and I did not tell them and I am not going to be able to say that again on this wall.*
>
> *— I. Sarn.*

A flood terrace, a window with a sill, a shilling taken before an answer, and a wall about nine feet long that has one more sentence on it tonight than it had this morning, and the sentence is the second half of a sentence from the sixth month, and a man who came up a road with a copyist is going four hundred and thirty miles back down it with a fact and no certificate.
