# Chapter 145: A Column She Is Not Going to Write

The counting room is off a dyers' lane in Greyfen and it is about eleven feet by fourteen and it has a bench, a window with a broken light, a stove that is lit in the ninth month, and about forty books on shelves that were made for taller books.

**Nell Kest is twenty.** She is a dye-house worker, which is what she is paid for, and she keeps the books of the eleven houses on this lane and of four other houses in three other districts, which is what she is not paid for and has done since she was seventeen.

The lane has eleven houses. There have been eleven houses on it since before anybody living. Seven of them took the levy on the mill and got it off again in the same year, in the year she was eleven, and the assessor who did that is dead, and eight of the eleven are still trading and three are not.

---

The book on the bench is the association's, and it is about ninety years old, and it is the fourth book, and the fourth column of the fourth book has been empty since the book was opened.

The first column is a house. The second is a year. The third is a count of pieces. The fourth has no heading over it, and the first three have had headings over them since the reign of the third of the Ashfall line, and somebody has ruled a line under the fourth heading about nine years ago in a different ink and left it.

There is one entry above the rule. It is in an old hand and it is six years old and it is the whole of what is in that column.

> *Asked of me to put names in this column. I have declined. If names go in it, the levy on each house is a matter of record against the person who worked the piece and not against the house, and a house may be sold and a person may not, and a person who has worked eleven years in a dye-house is not a thing that can be sold with a building. I have asked for this to be entered and I have asked that whoever comes after me be told that it was asked twice. — Assessor for the eleven houses, the sixth year.*

And under that, in a different ink again, and about six years old, somebody had written one word: *Noted.*

**That word is the last thing that happened to this book in six years.**

---

The book came to Nell Kest on the ninth of the seventh because the man who holds the eleven houses had it in a press in an office in the second district and had had it in a press for eleven years, and a clerk in the third district had written to a clerk in the first district, and the reply had come back, and it had taken about eleven months to arrive at the room she sits in.

The letter said, in four lines, that the association wished the fourth column to be completed for the current year, and that no objection had been received, and that the column was to be completed by the person then keeping the books.

Nobody in Greyfen had said no. That was in the letter too, and it was the sentence that took the eleven months: **no objection had been received.**

---

She read it four times, and then she went and stood in the lane outside for about nine minutes, and then she came back in and did the thing, which was to get out the book from the ninth year of last year and the one before it, and to look, for about half an hour, at whether anybody had ever put anything in the fourth column of any of the four books.

Nobody had. In the first book the column did not exist. In the second it did not exist. In the third it had a heading and nothing under it and the heading had been struck through about sixty years ago by somebody who had drawn a line through it with a pen and not a rule.

And in the fourth book, under the six-year-old refusal, there was the word *Noted*, in an unknown hand, and nothing else, and the book had been open on a bench in a counting room in Greyfen for about eleven years and nobody had ever turned to the end of it.

---

She did not write for two days. She worked in the dye-house on the eighth and the ninth and the tenth of the eighth, on the indigo, which is the worst of the four and puts a blue that does not come out of a nail on the ninth day, and she had blue in the second knuckle of the third finger of her right hand on the eleventh and it was still there on the nineteenth.

On the eleventh of the eighth she wrote, at the bottom of a page in the fourth book, in her own hand, with the day against it, and it is the whole of what she put in that column while she held the book.

> *Asked of eleven houses and of four other houses in three other districts, being the persons of the works in them, and not of the houses: whether they will say yes, no, or nothing, to a count of the days worked and a count of the levy standing, on a day, in their own doorway.*
>
> *Yes: 9. No: 2. Nothing: 4. The two who said no are the two oldest women on this lane and they gave reasons and I have entered the reasons in the third book where there is a column for it.*
>
> *A count of houses is 11. A count of days asked for is 4,190. A count of days agreed to is 1,203. The rest is a rest day and there is no column for a rest day and there has never been one.*

And above it, before it, on the same page, she wrote four more lines, and it is the four lines that are the chapter.

> *The last person asked to put names in this column declined in writing in the sixth year and asked that her declining be entered and that whoever came after her be told it had been asked twice. **It has been entered. Her name is not entered, because she did not give it, and I am not going to put it in, and that is the second time somebody has been asked for a name for this column and I am the second person and I am saying no to it in the same book.***
>
> *My name is Nell Kest. I am twenty. This book does not belong to me and I keep it because the man who owns it does not read it. I have done this on my own account and I expect to be asked about it and I will answer.*

She read it out to herself, which she did not have to do, and then she put the pen down and sat there for about an hour.

---

Here is the thing she worked out in that hour, and it is the finding of the chapter and she did not expect it and it is not about the levy at all.

A column with a heading over it is a machine for turning a house's problem into a family's problem. She has seen it work. A mill takes a levy; a mill lets it go; the work goes to a person; a person is now a record; and a person who is a record is a person who can be asked about a piece of cloth by anybody with a copy of the right page and a reason and four years.

**And a column with no heading over it is the same book with the machine taken out.**

And then she stopped, because a woman of sixty-four in a bay four hundred and thirty miles down a river had said something in the sixth month about a foreman throwing a paper with a heading on it away twice, and Nell Kest had read that in a trade sheet at a shilling a copy in the seventh month with a queue outside the window, and had thought it was a nice story about a district works.

**And it was not a story. It was the same protection, arrived at in a dyers' lane by two people about four hundred and thirty miles apart, in about four months, independently, for the same reason, and neither of them can tell you why it works and both of them know exactly what it does.**

Nobody in Greyfen had ever heard of a foreman of the Reed Cut district works and Nell Kest did not know it yet and would not for about a fortnight.

---

The letter had said that no objection had been received, and she has thought about that sentence more since than about anything else in the four months of work that put it there.

**Nobody objected. Not one person, out of about forty people in eleven houses and four other houses in three other districts, over about eleven months.**

She asked three of them, in the ordinary way, in the lane, standing at the door, and got the same answer three times, and the answer was the same sentence with different nouns in it, and the sentence was:

"**It is in a book and I have never been in it. I have never read it and I do not know anybody who has and if you asked me who holds it I could not tell you, and a thing you cannot name is a thing you cannot complain about, and I have been in this trade for thirty-one years and I have never once wanted to complain about a thing I could not name.**"

And a woman of about sixty at the eleventh door, who has cut plates out of a bench under a window on this lane for twenty-two years, said the other half of it, and Nell Kest wrote it down and has read it back to herself about nine times.

"You put a count in and it is the same as having put nothing in, and better, because a count is a thing a person can check." The last time anybody put a count in this lane in a book somebody kept was about sixty years ago and it was the number of pieces off the frames in a bad year, and I have never once been asked whether that was a good number of pieces, and I have never once wanted to be. **Nobody has ever asked us anything. That is not a defence of it. But it is why nobody has ever stood in this lane and said no.**"

---

On the thirteenth of the eighth she got on a cart and went six hours to a lane where a woman who had refused this column six years ago was still doing the round, and she did not knock on a door, because she did not have to.

The assessor is about sixty-eight. She has a slate and a satchel and about nine houses in that lane and she does the whole of it in a day and she has done it for nineteen years and the fourth of the eleven she is at when Nell Kest gets there is not the fourth of the eleven, and it does not matter.

"I am not going to ask you for anything," said Nell Kest. "I have written a line in the fourth column and it is not a name and I have put your declining above it in the same hand, and I have not put your name in, and I wanted you to know that before anybody else did."

The assessor read the slate. It took her about four minutes and she read it twice, which Nell Kest had told her she would be able to do in a letter.

"**Six years**," the assessor said.

"Six years."

"**I asked for that to be entered because I knew in about nine years somebody would send me a letter saying no objection had been received.** I did not think it would be six years and I did not think it would be a girl of twenty with blue in her finger who would do it instead."

"I did not do it instead. I did it after. I want it recorded that I did it after and that I had your sentence in a trade sheet in the seventh month and I did not know whose it was until I read the book."

---

Then the assessor said the sentence that has been owed on this lane for six years, and she said it standing in a lane with a slate under her arm, and it took about nine words and it is not on a slate and it is not in a book.

"**A person has to be able to say no in a room. Not only in a book.**"

And Nell Kest said, "**Why a room.**"

And the assessor said, "**Because a book is a thing somebody else reads at a time of their choosing, and a room is a thing where somebody has to stand there while you say it.** I have watched two of these books in my life get filled in by a man in an office about forty miles from where the cloth was dyed, and I have never once been in the office, and the men who filled them in did not know what the cloth was.

"So I said no in a book and it did about as much good as saying no in a room would have done, and **that is not a reason to say no in a room instead. It is a reason to say no in both.**"

---

Then Nell Kest said the thing about her own family, and she said it once, and she said it to the assessor and not to the lane, and it took about nine words and she had clearly had it ready since the seventh of the seventh.

"**We got the levy off the mill. The shop lost its credit in the same month. Both of those are true and one of them is why there is one job in this town and it is not mine.**"

The assessor said, "**That is the best sentence anybody has said to me about the sixth year and I have been listening to them for nineteen years.**"

And then she said the other thing, and it is the reason the chapter is not a chapter about a book.

"**And there is your brother in that shop, and he has not written to you since the second month, and you have not written to him, and neither of those is a bookkeeping matter and I have never once been asked about it by anybody and I am not going to start.**

"**But you are twenty, and you have just written a column with no heading in it and refused a name, and there is going to be a man in an office in the second district in about four years who reads that line and works out what you are, and he will be right, and he will not be able to use it for anything, and you will be findable anyway. That is the whole of it. There is no way to be in a book and not be findable and I have been looking for nineteen years and I have not found one.**"

---

She got back to the counting room at about the ninth hour with blue in her finger and four books on a bench and about forty on shelves, and she did not put anything else in the fourth column that year.

**The letter from the third district had said that no objection had been received, and there were about forty people, and eleven months.**

**And the fourth column of the fourth book of an association of dyers in a town four hundred and thirty miles from a river now has six years of nothing in it and then two days, and a refusal above the two days in a hand six years old with no name in it, and a woman's name — a girl of twenty, in her own hand — at the foot of her own line, in a book that is not hers, with the day on it.**

And that is the whole of what the second thing in this chapter was, and it came to her on a cart and not in a bay, and it is the same protection twice, about four hundred and thirty miles apart, arrived at by two people who have never met, and neither of them can prove that it is worth anything, and both of them are the only ones in their trades who have written it down.
