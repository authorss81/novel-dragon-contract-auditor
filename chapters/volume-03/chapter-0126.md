# Chapter 126: Seven Shillings and Sixpence

The tool shed at the Slade end is about nine foot by eleven and it has a door that shuts and a bench along the north wall that four men can sit on, and the lamp is not lit until about the seventh hour because there is not enough daylight in there to work by before that and has not been for sixty years.

Halla Wray is fifty-one and has been the foreman of the Slade Cut since about the age of forty-two, which is a thing nine families agreed to about eleven years ago and did not write down and cannot produce. Her term ran out in the third week of the third month. She has held the spring bank and the ford. She has put her own name at the foot of three lists of works and it is the first name at the foot of anything on that cut in sixty years.

The book in the hired store says the spring bank was nineteen days and five men and a boy at eight pence and five, being forty-five pence a day on the five and one, and the weigh-house ledger at the Slade end calls the three pounds eleven and threepence **held**, chosen in about four minutes among *given*, *paid* and *advanced*, and it is out of the wage of a clerk in a city who earns seventeen shillings and a penny the week, and it was three weeks of it.

She has said out loud in a shed, in about nine minutes, standing in front of nine people, that she is not going to pay a crew twice in one year out of two men's pockets and say nothing about it.

**That sentence is what this chapter is about, because it is about to cost a ford.**

---

On the tenth of the fourth month a miller had put a price on a list in this shed: nine tons of kerb and coping, delivered, five men and a boy for two days, **seven shillings and sixpence**, and a date of the second week of this month, and not one day past it, and under that, in her own hand, that the money had not come.

Nobody brought the money on the eleventh, the twelfth or the thirteenth.

The hundred and forty feet of ditch on the south side and the scour at the third-of-the-way place were on nobody's list then and are on nobody's list now.

There are about one hundred and forty feet of ditch on the south side. It is a figure off the Company's own book and it is the only figure of a ditch anybody in ninety-one households has.

On the morning of the fourteenth she did the sum out loud in the shed, which she does, and there were nine men in it and the lamp was not lit.

"Right. **Two days of a crew is seven shillings and sixpence.** Five men at eight pence and a boy at five, forty-five pence a day on the five and one, ninety pence. I have said that figure out loud twice in the last four days and both times I have had to stop myself, because it is the smallest bill anybody has ever been asked to find for a public work on this cut and it is a stupid thing to be emotional about at my age.

"And we have got four shillings a year.

"**That is the whole of the money this Company has had in sixty years and it is the rent of the south end of that store, at a shilling a quarter, taken out of the book four times a year, and it is there to keep about nine hundred and forty names dry.** And we are three shillings and sixpence short of a bill that would cost about a year and a quarter of the rent.

"So you all know what I am going to say and I am going to say it anyway because a foreman who does not say the arithmetic out loud in a shed is a foreman who has somebody else doing it in a kitchen. **We are not going to take three shillings and sixpence out of a store to put a ford right. The day that book is wet is the day names go off it, and there is nobody left in ninety-one households who is going to write them out again.**"

Somebody at the back said, "Then it does not get done."

"Then it does not get done," said Halla Wray, "**and the ford is not a ford, and a boy of fifteen is on a wall four miles up that lane with a leg, and the two posts are on the low side stacked neatly and the nine stones of the throat are beside them, and in about five days there is going to be a water in the third week of this month, and I have written all of that down twice and I am not going to write it down a third time.**"

---

The foreman of the Reed Cut district works came up the road four miles at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the fourteenth, and he had not been in the shed in eleven weeks, and he had nine pages in his hand, and he was the wrong side of sixty and had a coat open in weather that did not want it open.

He put them on the bench in the middle of the shed, which is not a bench in the middle, it is a bench along a wall, and he put them there anyway.

"Sayer," said Halla Wray. "You have come four miles in the rain in the middle of a season with a paper under your arm and you are going to tell me what it is before you sit down."

"I am going to tell you what it is and then I am going to ask you for the third time, and I want the third time said out loud, and I want it said before I sit down as well, because the last two times I did it sitting down and I have thought about that for about a week."

"You have not asked me twice. **Rennick has asked me twice.** A foreman in a shed on the thirtieth of the last month and a book-keeper in a shed on the same day, and about a fortnight ago I have been asked nothing at all, and the first time was in a mill yard in the first month and it was not about the bank at all."

"Then this is the third time about the bank and the ford, and I have come to ask, and I would like it in front of nine men and I would like you to say no out loud if you are going to say no, because **I am going to put it in my own return book either way and I have got nine men's names in that room and I would rather your no was said than inferred.**"

---

She said it in about eleven seconds and she did not sit down.

"**No.** And the spring bank as well, and the reason is the same and shorter.

"I set the condition. The crew is paid out of something with a figure on it and not out of a man's wage in a city, and if nobody can pay for it out of anything then the work waits rather than be done a second time for nothing. **I said that in a shed in front of nine people on the thirtieth of the last month and I have said it in three rooms since and I am not going to un-say it because there is a piece of paper on the bench.**"

"Then that work is lapsed," said Wendt Sayer. "**I am going to enter the lapse and the day and the reason, with my own name at the foot of it, in a book four miles down that road, and you have just given me the reason in front of nine men, and that is the second time in nine years that a person has given me a reason instead of a figure and I am not going to forget which of the two it was.**"

He put the nine pages down flat on the bench and squared them, which is a foreman's habit and not a clerk's.

"Now. **This came to my gate yesterday afternoon and nobody sent it. I have had it overnight and I have not slept and I am going to say what it is and then I am going to ask you a question that I do not know the answer to and you are not going to know it either, and I have wanted to ask somebody who was not in my own office for about nine years and this is the first chance anybody has given me.**

"It is an instrument. It moves one specified consequence — **the outer face of about four hundred and forty foot at the third-of-the-way mark and the seventh work of eleven on the page from ninety-one years back** — off the two people named in a sheet that is void, and off the district, and onto the Slade Cut Company, and onto ninety-one named persons in rotation, each for a term, with a review date, and with a right of refusal that costs the person refusing nothing.

"It has my own name at the foot of the last page as the man who holds it, and his name at the top of the first, and there is a miller of about fifty at the end of this bank who has had a piece of paper in his own yard for six years and gave it away, and I am told his name is the first one in it.

"**And I want you to read the fourth page out to those nine men and then I want to ask you a question, and the question is what a man does on the first morning of his term if he is on the rotation.**"

---

She read the fourth page out, standing at the bench, at about the fifth hour, to nine men of whom three could read it.

It took about nine minutes and about four of the nine asked her what a word meant and she said the word again louder.

Then she put it down and said: "**It is technically good.**

"I want that said in this shed by me and not by him and not by anybody in Auremar. Nine pages, a consequence named, a term, a review date, a right of refusal that costs the person refusing nothing, and a clause saying that nothing in it survives the last term of the last name in the schedule. **I have been doing this work for nine years off a book and a nail and I have never once seen anything come up that road that looked like that, and if you had brought it to me in the first month I would have read it out and I would have been glad of it.**

"And I cannot find the wrong in it. That is what I am going to say next and I have been sitting on it since about the fourth hour. **I cannot find the wrong in it and I have been in this matter eleven weeks and I know exactly what being on the wrong side of a thing feels like, and this feels like it, and those two facts are sitting in the same room and they are not going to get on.**"

"Then ask me the question," said Wendt Sayer. "**Because I have been in a district works for thirty-one years and I have kept a book for three of them and I have had about four hundred people walk into my yard in three years and say a thing, and not one of them is answerable for it, because I ruled a column in thirty-one years ago and nobody told me to write a heading over it. And I have had the names of all of them, and I have thought about that heading in the first month of every year for nine years and thrown the paper away twice.**"

"What does a man does on the first morning of his term who is on the rotation," said Halla Wray.

"That is the question and I cannot answer it. **I have been in this district thirty-one years and I have sent nine men to a bank in the first month of this year because a clerk in a city paid for them, and that is the whole of what I have done for that bank in nine years, and I have written it in my own book with the day on it and my own name at the foot of it, and there is not one figure in that book about anybody on that bank.**"

"You can't answer it because nobody's asked him."

"Nobody's asked him," said Wendt Sayer, "**and there is no form in this district for asking him, and I know that better than anybody alive because I have been the man who has not asked, and I have got about four hundred names, and I have not asked one of them, and I have been proud of it for three years and I have been wrong for three years and I found that out about a month ago in a yard.**"

---

Then he asked her the thing that nobody had asked her, and it was in a shed with nine men in it and it had been the whole of the second month.

"Wray. **The first sheet of the season came up the road on the first of this month with a body in the box. Does your Company know it is in the box.**"

"No."

"Do you know it is in the box."

"No, and I am not going to be told in a shed this afternoon, and if you have come four miles in the rain to tell me then you have had a wasted four miles, and I would rather you had."

"Then I have had a wasted four miles," said Wendt Sayer, "and I will have a wasted four miles again in about a year, and I would like it entered in my own book that I asked a body whether it knew it was in a box and the body could not answer, and that a body cannot answer is not a finding and is a fact."

And then, before the door, she said the thing that had been sitting in her for four days and that she had not said to her brother and was not going to.

"**I have been in a box for eleven weeks and I did not know it, and I have been out of it since the last day of the last month, and nobody asked me to be out of it.** My brother said one word in a water office four miles up the road and a keeper wrote it in a book with a date on it, and the district's flood authority for four hundred and forty foot of bank went off me without one person asking me whether I would take it.

"And I want it noticed that I have been relieved of a decision I never had, and that I did not know I had it until about the fourth hour this afternoon, and that I am the only person in this shed who could not have refused it, because nobody thought of asking a woman of fifty-one who was standing in a shed in the third week of the last month whether she would rather have the work or not."

"Wray," said Wendt Sayer. "**I heard that. I am going to write it down tonight and it is going in my own book in my own hand and it is not going in a return and it is not going in a letter to you.**"

And then, at the door, with his coat still open, he said the other thing, and he said it to her and not to the nine men, and Halla Wray has repeated it since and has not been able to improve on it.

"**I have never once been asked whether I would hold anything, and I have wanted to be asked since I was about forty-two.** And I have got about four hundred names in a column with no heading over it and I have never asked one of them, and you have been in a box for eleven weeks and you did not know it, and I am going to write both of those on the same line of my own book tonight and I am going to write my own name under them, and I have got nine men outside and nobody in this district who could tell me I am not allowed."

Then he went four miles back down the road in the rain.

---

**A foreman who lets a work die rather than lie about where the money came from is a foreman somebody can be shown to have been right.**

She said that out loud to the shed afterwards, to nine men, and she wrote it on the lapsed list under the two figures — **seven shillings and sixpence**, and **four shillings a year, of which the whole is the rent** — and then she wrote the spring bank under that, **three pounds eleven and threepence, out of a wage of seventeen shillings and a penny the week, being three weeks of it, and paid in full, and there are no unpaid days in this matter**, and then she wrote **no** and put her own name at the foot of it and the date, which was the fourteenth day of the fourth month, and not one day past it, and Rennick Adley entered it in the store afterwards with the day on it and read it back.

And about one hundred and forty feet of ditch on the south side and the scour at the third-of-the-way place went on to being on nobody's list, and the ford, the stones and the two posts were on a list with a name at the foot of it and a date and two figures and the word **lapsed** on it, and the bank stood eleven feet high where a crew had left it on the twenty-first of the second month, and the water was in the third week of this month.
