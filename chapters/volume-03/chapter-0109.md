# Chapter 109: The Weigh-House

The weigh-house at the Slade end is a shed of a different kind from the tool shed. It has a floor of boards and a bench and a pair of scales under a lid and a ledger on the bench, and it stands where the cart track comes off the towpath, and about nine hundred loads of wet earth have gone over those scales in nine years and the ledger has every one of them in it in a hand of about fifty-eight.


The weigh-master's name is not on anything except the ledger and she has said that she does not want it on anything. She is fifty-eight and she weighs earth and she is the only person on that bank who gets paid every week whether the work happens or not, and she is paid out of the same head of the works fund as the men, and the difference between her and the men is that her wage is a wage and theirs is a day.

She has been the weigh-master since her mother was, and before her mother it was a man called Searle, who is on about nine hundred lines of a page from ninety-one years back and whose widow is a woman of about eighty-one four houses past the mill.

On the fourteenth day of the second month a man came into the weigh-house with a copyist and asked, in about four minutes, standing at the bench, what a person had to do to be paid for five men and a boy for a fortnight, and she told him, and told him twice, because he wrote it down the first time and she wanted it right.

"Eight pence a man and five the boy, for fourteen days, and it is one pound five and sixpence, and the list has to be the list. **The list is the list.** The list goes to the works with the sheet and the sheet comes back with the sheet and if the list is a different list the whole thing comes back and nobody gets paid for a fortnight, and it has come back twice in nine years and both times it was because a man came in and put his own name at the top instead of at the bottom, and I do not know how a man does that and I have never done it myself and I have never had to look at why."

"How is it paid."

"Out of the fund. The head says bank works. There is nothing in it. **There has been nothing in it since the third week of the twelfth month and there will be nothing in it in the second month and there may be nothing in it in the third.** A quarter is three months and the head has about one pound five and sixpence in it in a good year and about four shillings in a bad one, and the four shillings goes on a store in a mill yard, and that is the whole of the money in the Slade Cut Company and it is not spent on the bank, it is spent on the book, and the book is more important, and I know that is a mad thing to say and I have said it out loud four times in nine years to about four different people and nobody has ever agreed with me."

---

Marek Kest had a sheet of foolscap in the inside of his coat, and it had been in there for nine days, and on the sixteenth day of the second month, in the weigh-house, at about the fourth hour, with Halla Wray and about four men and a woman of about twenty-four standing at the bench, Tamsin Rook asked him for it.

She did not ask for it loudly. She said: "You have had a sheet in your coat for nine days that goes to a household, and I bring you every one of those, and I read it in front of you before it goes, and I have not said one word about it for nine days, and I am saying one word about it now, in a room, out loud, and the word is *hand*."

He handed it over.

She read it standing at the bench. It is four lines and it took her about a minute and a half to read and about four seconds to understand, and she read it twice, and then she said one sentence, and then she said the other thing, which took about nine minutes, and the other thing is the reason the undertaking of the fourth of the first month is in this weigh-house.

"I have now read it," she said, "and I am going to give you my reason against it in a room and out loud and not on a page, because that is the arrangement and you have known the arrangement for a year and you have just broken the first half of it for the first time in nine days by keeping it in a coat.

"My reason is not that it is wrong. My reason is that I read it in about a minute and a half and understood it in about four seconds, and if I can do that then anybody in this room can do that, and about four of them can already.

"And the reason I understood it in four seconds is that it is a good instrument. That is the whole of what is wrong with it. **It is the best piece of drafting anybody in this matter has put in front of me in fourteen months, and I have been waiting since the twentieth of the eighth month for somebody to write it, and I have said in a book with no office on it that I was waiting for it, and I was, and I was wrong about wanting it.**

"Look at what it does. It takes one specified consequence — five men and a boy, a fortnight, the outer face from the mark to the eleven feet — and it moves it off two named persons onto ninety-one named persons in rotation, and every one of the ninety-one is a person, and every one of the ninety-one can be heard, and every one of the ninety-one can say no, and that is the best sentence in the world.

"And it makes the Company the guarantor of every flood on that cut from this day to the end of time, and it does it by making ninety-one people each hold it once, and **nobody holds it twice, and so nobody is ever in a position to say *I have held this and I know what it is*, and the ninety-first household to be asked will be told by their neighbour that it is nothing, and the hundredth will be told that, and the thing will be a thing that happens to a household about once in ninety-one years, and by the time a child on that bank is seventeen it will have happened twice and he will not know it ever happened.**"

She put it down on the bench.

"And you knew that. You have known that since you wrote it, and you have had it in your coat for nine days, and you did not bring it to me, and the reason you did not bring it to me is that you knew I would say no and you would have had to do something about my no."

"I did not write it to be sent," said Marek Kest.

"You wrote it to be *shown*," said Tamsin Rook. "That is worse and you know that it is worse."

---

Then she asked the question he had not thought to ask, and she asked it in the ordinary way, in a weigh-house, in about four seconds, to a man standing at a bench with a copy of his own handwriting on it.

"Have you asked the ninety-one households."

"No."

"**No.**"

"I have not asked ninety-one people whether they will hold a fortnight of bank work each in rotation over the next nine years, and I have not asked them, and I wrote a piece of paper about it, and I have had it in my coat for nine days while I have been in this district being useful at a window and a gate and a court, and **not one of the four times anybody has asked me whether I have asked anybody anything, I have been able to say yes.**"

Marek Kest put both hands flat on the bench, which is a thing he has never once done in this matter, and which the weigh-master, who has watched about nine hundred loads of wet earth go over a pair of scales, described afterwards as *a man who has decided to stand still.*

"That is the fifth time," said Tamsin Rook, "and it has caught three words and one fact and one absence, and an absence is worse, because a word can be argued about and a fact can be checked and **an absence is just there and it does not argue.** The undertaking is not retired and it is not amended and you have not beaten it by keeping a thing in a coat for nine days, and I have read it and my reason is on the record of this room and the record of this room is a book in a grain store and my name is not going in it, and I would like it entered in that book by the man who keeps it, in his own hand, that on the sixteenth of the second month a copyist of a city four hundred and thirty miles away gave a reason against a document in a weigh-house at the Slade end, and did not give her name, and the reason she did not give her name is the third line of a sheet with two boxes on it that says a person is not to be given advice."

---

Halla Wray had been standing at the other end of the bench through the whole of it, and she has said since that she did not understand about half of it and understood all of the part about the sheet, and that she understood it in about nine seconds and has said so.

"That is the fourth question," she said, when it was quiet. "*Who read that to you, and do they get paid if I sign.*"

"Yes," said Marek Kest. "Nobody read it to me. I have not written anything like it before. I am not permitted to write it, and the reason I am not permitted to write it is that I am eligible to be trained as a thing I have spent fourteen months refusing to be and I have not been trained and there is no office behind me and no fund and nobody has asked me to write one, and I would be writing it in a weigh-house on the strength of a certificate that does not exist.

"And I am not paid. I have never been paid for anything in this matter except a day of a plate-cutter and about two hundred and forty miles of my own carriage, and I have not been paid for this and nobody has offered and nobody in this district knows how to."

"Then you are a person who turns up," said Halla Wray.

"Yes," said Marek Kest.

"Then you can hold it for a fortnight like anybody else, and so can she, and she is stood there with her hands behind her back waiting for somebody to say she is not allowed to."

---

He paid it out of his own wage and he did it in about four minutes and he would not let anybody else touch it.

"One pound five and sixpence," he said, writing it on the back of the list of works in his own hand and not on any form, "and it is nine weeks of my wages, and I am going to say the whole of the shape of it out loud because Tamsin Rook has just made me say things out loud for nine minutes and I have noticed that I am a different animal when I do.

"It is not a gift. I want that in a book. **A gift is a thing a person does out of what they feel and it makes the other one owe them something and there is no figure on it and it is the worst kind of money there is.** It is not out of a fund and there is no fund, and if anybody at any point tells anybody that a fund paid for the spring bank at the Slade in the second month, that is a lie with a clerk's name on it, and the clerk's name is mine, and you may all have it.

"It is not an instrument. There is no instrument, there is no form, there is no seal and there is no office and I have written nothing that anybody in ninety-one households has to do or sign or answer for. It is a man in a weigh-house paying a bill that the office that ought to pay it has disclaimed in writing, and that is the whole of what it is.

"And I am not doing it to be liked and I am not doing it because I think it is the answer. I have been in this district for a fortnight and I have found out that the answer, if there is one, is a list of works with a person's name at the foot of it and a date against the name, and that a list like that costs nothing, and that **the reason nobody has one is not the law and it is not the water office and it is a shilling a quarter for a store in a mill yard and four hundred and forty feet of bank**, and a man from a city can pay a fortnight and about nine hundred feet of face and cannot do the other thing, and the other thing is the whole of this matter and has been for fourteen months.

"So I am paying for the fortnight, and the bank will be done for the season, and in the fourth month I will not be here, and the district's flood authority is two people's names and a dragon who has given her authority away, and the fund is empty, and **none of that is fixed and I have not fixed it and I have not pretended to.**"

The weigh-master took the money and put it in the drawer and wrote it up in the ledger, and she wrote it up as *carried*, and then she stopped and asked what word she should use, and about four people in that weigh-house argued about a word in a ledger for about four minutes, and the word they settled on, at about the fourth hour and a half, was *held*, and it is in that ledger and it is the only line in nine hundred that is not a load of earth.

---

Marek Kest wrote it out that night in the room over a chandler's shop in a lane in that town, in his own book, with the day on it, and he read it back and crossed nothing out, and it is the last of it.

> *The sixteenth of the second month, at about the ninth hour. Four pounds eleven at Lowcross, three years old. Four hundred and thirty miles. Six days on a towpath and in a cart and a bench. One pound five and sixpence paid today out of my own wage, being nine weeks of it, for a fortnight of five men and a boy at eight pence and five, and entered in a weigh-house ledger under a word we spent four minutes choosing, which is **held** and not **given** and not **paid** and not **advanced**.*
>
> *What I did not do and why, and the why is the finding of the fortnight and I would like it kept in a book I keep rather than in one somebody else keeps.*
>
> *I could write it. I have written it, in my own hand, in four lines, on foolscap, in about eleven minutes, and it is the best piece of drafting I have done in fourteen months and it is valid. It moves one specified consequence off two named persons onto ninety-one named persons in rotation, with a term, and a date, and a right of refusal, and every one of the ninety-one is a person and can be heard and can say no. It is exactly the instrument. It is the only instrument. **And it makes the Slade Cut Company the guarantor of every flood on that cut for ever, and it does it by never asking anybody twice, and a duty that nobody holds twice is a duty that is held once and forgotten, and the children of that bank will inherit it the way a district inherits a parish that has been dissolved.**
>
> *I am not permitted to write it. I am eligible to be trained and have not been trained, and there is no certification, and no fund, and no office behind me, and the grant I hold is over, and the person who would have to certify that instrument is a court and I have no proceeding in any court and could not start one and am not a party to any. So the instrument that would fix this district is one I am not allowed to write, and the reason I am not allowed is that a court of the jurisdiction, on a question referred by a man of sixty, put an eligibility and a watch in one sentence and put the watch there deliberately, and the watch is the price and not a qualification.*
>
> **And that has stopped feeling like a wound and it has started feeling like a fact, and I do not know what to do with that, and I am recording the day it happened and not the reason, because the reason is in the other half of this book and the other half is about a woman of twenty-four in a weigh-house at the fourth hour, standing with her hands behind her back, waiting to be told that she is not allowed to do a thing she has not been asked to do either.**
>
> *And the last of it, and this is the part I would want a stranger to read in nine years if there is a stranger.*
>
> *A shield is not a road. A sheet with two boxes on it at four pence does not open a door. A precedent that is open to challenge does not supply the challenge. A body cannot be asked and a body cannot say no, and a body made of ninety-one people is a body, and **the road to a document about nobody is a person being found, and finding a person is a different act and starts a case, and I have not started one, and I have told a woman in a weigh-house that I have not started one, and she said the reason out loud, and the reason was that I was afraid I would start one, and that was true, and it was the only true thing anybody said in that room for nine minutes and it was said by a person who was not talking to me.***
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the sixteenth of the second month, at about the ninth hour, four hundred and thirty miles from the desk, with a watch on me and nothing in my hands.*
