# Chapter 133: The Office That Cannot Give It Back

The Reed Cut district water office is a stone building two hundred yards above the gate, so that a man can come up out of the water and see the office and the water at the same time. It has a counter, a back room with a stove, a room with the day's sheets in it, and about nine hundred forms on a shelf, and the forms are the office's whole learning.

**Sivra Oris keeps it. It is not her House's and she keeps it on her own.** She is the scale counterparty to the original Venn instrument. She is not the First Seat and is not that seat's agent and is not a mouth for one, and she said so in the first minute of a room in the first month, unasked, and she has not said it since, and nobody in this matter has asked her to since, and she is glad of that in a way she has not told anybody.

In the first month she entered two human names into a box headed *who is to answer* on a flood sheet, without asking either of them, wrote on the face of her own sheet that she had asked nobody, left a boy of sixteen out of the box on purpose and entered the leaving-out as the only protection the office was able to give anybody.

**And to do that she disclaimed this office's flood authority in writing.**

It is in the front desk-book. It is in her own hand. It is about eleven lines long and it says that the office does not hold the flood authority in respect of the works on the Slade Cut and cannot be instructed to act in respect of them and does not claim to hold it, and it is dated, and there is no form in this empire for getting it back and she looked for one for nine weeks.

---

The question came up the road on the twenty-fourth of the fifth month at about the seventh hour, carried by a boy of about fourteen who was told it was not urgent and was therefore in no particular hurry.

The clerk of the office is about twenty-three and has been in it two years, and let him in, and made him sit on the stool by the stove, and gave him a cup of something out of a jug that is not tea.

"That is not tea."

"No," said the clerk. "It is what we have."

> *To the keeper of the district water office named at the foot. From a notary's table of a House, being a water-notary practice, in a matter touching a water guarantee sealed under the fire-and-water device and offered to an unincorporated company working a public cut in this district.*
>
> *We are asked to be satisfied of one fact, namely who is of the Company. We are told the Company is not on the register of bodies. We are told it has no head. We are told that no court may make it a party.*
>
> *We ask this office to certify the fact. The answer is required in the form provided, with a reason, within the time stated.*

She read it twice standing up and then she took it into the back room and shut the door, which she has never done for a piece of paper in nine years, and she stood by the stove for about four minutes with it in her hand.

**The thing the House has asked her for is the thing she signed away in the first month.**

That is the whole of it. Not the flood authority over the works — that is a different absence and she could have given that. The House wants to know who is of ninety-one households, and for eleven weeks the only person in this district who could have said anything about the water of that cut with a seal on it was this office, and this office put two names in a box on purpose and gave the authority away in a sheet of foolscap to keep a boy of sixteen out of it.

**She did that to stop a child being in a box. And it has taken her own office out of the only room in the district where a person could have been the answer, and she is going to find that out from a House four hundred miles up the river, in a form with one box on it.**

---

She filled the form in standing at the counter, which takes a minute and a half, and then she read her own answer and did not like it and read it twice and then left it alone, because it was not wrong and she was not going to improve a true sentence.

> *Answer: **refused.***
>
> *Reason: this office cannot certify who is of a body. It has no power to do so, has never had it, and there is no form in this district or in the Empire by which it could acquire it. It is entered in this district's own front desk-book in this office's hand, with a date, that this office does not hold the flood authority in respect of the works to which the question is directed and cannot be instructed to act in respect of them.*

And under the reason, because the box on that form is two lines deep and what she had to say was not, she wrote on the face of the form itself:

> *The reason is longer than the box. It is on a page in this office's own front desk-book, in this keeper's hand, with the day on it, and any person may come and read it, and the entry is dated the twenty-fourth day of the fifth month.*

And she wrote on the back of the form, in the same hand, before she folded it: **There is no space on this form for why. Nobody has ever wanted one and I have wanted one for nine years and I am not going to invent a box.**

---

Then she read out the second half.

The boy had it in his hand and she did not take it back, which she thought about afterwards.

"There is a second question in it," she said. "It is the one underneath and it is the one that matters, and the answer to it is yes, and I am going to say the answer out loud because a thing in an office is worth what it is worth out loud."

"The form only has one box."

"The form only has one box and the paper has two questions in it, and the second one is: *whether a court may make an unincorporated body a party.* The answer is **no**, and it is not my answer, and a magistrate of this province entered it on the ninth of the second month on her own motion, and the answer is true in every court in this empire because there is nowhere in it for it to be otherwise. **Take the yes back with you. It is free and it is correct and it is worth about four hundred and thirty miles of carriage, and I am giving it to you for nothing because it costs this office nothing to be right about a thing that is already written down in nine books.**"

The boy wrote it out, badly, and she made him write it out again, and she read his copy of her own answer and initialled it, which is a thing this office does not do and which she decided to do and has not explained to anybody.

---

Then she did the other thing, which is the thing she did first and has been not telling anybody about since the ninth.

She wrote the void in the front desk-book in her own hand with the day on it, in about a minute, and put the sheet flat on the counter with the writing turned for the person standing on the other side, which is a thing a girl of seventeen taught her by turning a sheet round in a kitchen for eleven people and has never once been thanked for.

The entry said:

> *Referred to: the entry of the last day of the third month, in this book, in this hand, on the sheet itself. Re-entered here on the twenty-fourth day of the fifth month because a House has now asked this office a question about the same works and somebody in a building four hundred miles up a river is going to want to know whether this office ever gave that sheet out again.*
>
> *It did not. The flood sheet of the ninth of the first month is void, and it is void because a person said no standing in this office, and a person saying no is a thing this office can enter and cannot un-enter. **There is now no name on this bank anywhere at all.**
>
> *The district's flood authority in respect of the Slade spring works and the ford is entered as undispensed, with this date, and is not entered as no person available, because a sentence about people in a return of this office would be read in nine years as a finding that nobody was willing.*

She read that back once and did not read it twice, because the second one is for a reader and there is no reader.

---

The boy went down the two hundred yards at about the ninth hour and the water was down and the gate was shut and nobody came up all afternoon.

She had about nine hundred forms on that shelf and she had been glad of them for nine years, and about the fourth hour of that day she took the shelf down and went through it with a thumb, which takes about eleven minutes, and what she found out is that this office has **a form for a person and a form for two persons and a form for a household, which is a form about a head, and about nine hundred forms have no such box at all, and none of them says *none*.**

**There is a form for a body and there is no form for nobody, and that is the third time in nine weeks that this office has been asked to put a person into a place where the answer is that there is not one.**

She got the stool and the jug and sat down on it in the middle of the afternoon and thought about writing one, and did not, and could not have told anybody in about four minutes why not.

The reason is that a form is a thing a person fills in, and the only person in this district who can fill in a form saying *there is nobody* is this office, and this office is not the thing the form would be about. **A form made by this office saying that no person is available would be read in nine years by somebody in a corridor as a return, and a return about people is a finding about people, and I have spent nine years not making findings about people.**

So she put the shelf back, and wrote nothing, and shut the room, and went home at about the sixth hour, and the courier was on a towpath four hundred miles up the river by then with a refusal in his bag that had a *yes* written in the wrong place on the front of it.

A House's own practice does not ask its own table whether its own seal is good. A House's practice asks the person in the room, and the person in the room was a notary of fifty-one or two with nineteen years in that chair, and it took him about four minutes to say that he would look at it, and it took him nine days to ask for one thing.

**He asked what is on the ground.**

---

The rest of that day was ordinary, and that is worth a page, because three months of this matter has not had many ordinary days in it.

A man of about forty came up the two hundred yards at about the third hour in the afternoon about a gate linkage on the lower Cut that has been sticking since the water went down, and wanted the office to send a man, and the clerk explained to him patiently, twice, that the office does not send men to gates.

"I am not asking for a man. I am asking for it to be written down that it sticks."

"It sticks," said Sivra Oris, from the back room.

"Then write that down."

"That is not what this office's book is for."

"What is it for, then."

**And she told him, standing in the doorway, in about eleven words, which is the answer she gives about four times a year and always to somebody who has not asked for it: *this office keeps the record of what the district's water did and what it is going to do, and the doing of it is not the office's and has never been, and the reason the office cannot be instructed to act is written in its own book in its own hand with a date on it, and you are welcome to come up and read it.***

He said that was a strange way to keep a book. She said it was.

He went. And about an hour later a woman of about thirty-one came up the two hundred yards with a baby on her hip about a boundary stone between her ground and the mill's, and that one the office can do, and the clerk did it in about nine minutes, and it cost the office a nail and half an hour of somebody's afternoon, and it is the only thing this office did that day anybody was ever going to be satisfied with.

And then at about the fifth hour a man from the district works came up the hill.

Not with nine pages. With a slip, and a bad temper, and about four days of it behind him.

"Your flood sheet for the Slade spring works. We have had nothing since the first month. **I have had four men standing at a gate for four days with a list that says nothing on it.**"

"It is void."

"Then give me another."

"That is exactly the sentence I have been wanting somebody to come up this hill and say to me since the last day of the third month," said Sivra Oris, "**and I am not going to say it twice. Listen. This office's flood authority in respect of those works is undispensed. It is in the front desk-book in my hand with a date. I cannot give you a sheet. I have no authority to give you one. If I gave you one it would be a sheet with a name in a box on it, and there is nobody to put in the box, and I have told you already why I have not put a name in a box since the first month of this year.**"

"Then who has."

"Nobody."

"Then who do I ask."

**And there it was, standing in her own doorway at about the fifth hour of the twenty-fourth of the fifth month, and it had been standing in that doorway since the last day of the third month, and she had never once had to say it to a face.**

"The district works," she said. "Ask the district works."

"I am the district works."

"Then I have told you the whole of it and there is no more of it than that." She had her hand on the door frame and it stayed there. "**Go down the hill and write in your own return book, in your own hand, with the day on it, that you asked this office for a sheet, and this office has none, and cannot make one, and is not to blame, because an office that has given its authority away in writing has nothing left to give anybody and cannot be asked to produce it. Put your own name at the foot of it. You have not done that in nine years.**"

The man of forty said, "That is a foul thing to be handed."

"Yes."

And he went down the two hundred yards, and she stood in the doorway for a while after he had gone, and the clerk came out and asked whether she wanted the stove lit, and she said not yet.

---

She had done two things in one afternoon and she did not like either of them, and she sat on the stool by the cold stove and worked out why, which took her about eleven minutes and is worth setting down because it is the same finding twice in two different rooms and about four hundred miles apart.

The first thing she had done was hand a House four hundred miles up the river a *yes* about a thing she had never been asked about, in the wrong place, on the front of a refusal.

The second thing she had done was tell a man to put his own name at the foot of his own entry, and he had gone off the hill to do it, and he would do it, because he had been angry enough to and that is the only fuel this works on.

**Both of those are the same act. They are a keeper making a person findable so that a thing can be asked about. And the whole of the trouble with this office is that it has spent nine years refusing to put anybody in a box, and it has now twice in one day made a person findable, and it is going to be asked about both of them in about four years by somebody with a reason.**

She went and got the front desk-book and wrote one line, and the line is short, and it is this:

> *The twenty-fourth day of the fifth month. I have today made two people findable — a House, by a true sentence, and a foreman of the district works, by telling him to sign his own name. I record it because nobody else is going to and because the second one I did on purpose and the first one I did not notice, and the whole of the difference between them is about eleven seconds and I did not see it happening.*

And she read that back once and did not read it twice, because the second reading is for a reader and there is no reader, and she has been doing that since the first month and has never once minded until this afternoon.
