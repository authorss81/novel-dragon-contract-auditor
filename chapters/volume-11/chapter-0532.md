# Chapter 0532: A Woman Of Thirty-Four Going Out Of A Room She Had Not Left In Eleven Years At About The Fifth Hour, And The Sixth Hour Done Without Her

That floor opens at the second hour and shuts at the sixth, and about four hundred people a year come down those four flights carrying something that has nothing whatever to do with the business of the room at the top of them. None of them has ever been sent up by anybody in that building. None of them is this month.

Marn Ottery is thirty-four and she has not gone out of that room once in eleven years. Four times in that time she has said the shape of a thing out loud in it, and standing up, before the first of the four, she told that floor that another one was not coming.

Tamsin Rook is twenty-four and has been at her own end of those boards for two years, and she is in that room a full twenty minutes ahead of the hour on every working morning of it. She has never once worked out which morning she began doing that on, and she is not going to.

A clerk of about twenty-nine has sat at the far end of the two joined tables for four years. Beside her book there is a pen that has been lying on the wood for about two months, squared to the edge of the table, where anybody could reach it.

It is the second day of the second week of the eleventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fifth hour.

---

He came up at about the fourth, four times a week for eleven years, and he came up the four flights the way he always came up them. This time he did not put the bag down on the wood at the far end of the boards. He stood with his shoulder to the room and both hands on the strap for as long as it took a clerk of twenty-nine to rule a line and a woman of twenty-four to come in from the stair, and then he went down again at about a quarter past.

Nobody on that floor said one word to him either way.

The strap was not squared. He squares it. It has been squared every one of those four times a week for about two months, and there is nobody in that building who has ever seen him do it and no reason on earth that he should.

Tamsin Rook noticed the strap. She noticed it the way she notices the twenty minutes, which is to say that she noticed it and then went on with what she was doing, and she was not going to say so out loud to anybody, and she had not decided to notice it, and she was not going to decide that either.

---

The room was an ordinary room with ordinary work in it and the work was done well.

She had been at the end of the boards since the twenty minutes, which is a thing she has never been able to account for. Marn Ottery had been at the far end since the hour, and the clerk of about twenty-nine had her book open and her pen lying beside it. There is a stretch of about forty minutes in the middle of every morning in which the three of them say almost nothing to each other and get through more than they would get through talking.

Nobody in that room was waiting for anything. That was the point of it, and it had been the point of it for eleven years. Four times the woman at the far end had said the shape of a thing out loud, and each of the four had taken about nine minutes and had cost the room the whole of the rest of that morning, and standing up, before the first of the four, she had told the floor that another one was not coming, and the floor had believed her, and eleven years had gone a long way past that.

At about half past four the woman of twenty-four squared a stack of sheets that did not need squaring.

At about the quarter to five the woman of thirty-four put her hand flat on the boards.

At about the five o'clock she put both hands flat on the boards.

They were flat on them long enough for her to look at her own hands while they were there, which she had not expected to do. Then she took them off, and she came round the end of the boards, and she went out of that room at about the fifth hour of an ordinary morning and down four flights and out of that building, and she was not in that room at the sixth hour.

"You told this floor, standing up, before the first of them, that there would not be another."

"There has not been one in about a year and there is not going to be one now."

"You have not come back into this room for anything."

"I have not come back into this room for anything, and if you had asked me where I had gone I would not have told you, and that would not have been a refusal. **The case goes in behind those boards at the sixth hour and those four things go up onto the wall above it at the same time every working day, and I have watched that happen about four thousand times from the inside of this room, and this morning I got as far as the door and went out of it, and nobody in this building is going to be told which way I turned at the bottom of those four flights.**"

"You are not going to be thanked for it," Tamsin Rook said, "and I have not asked you for anything, and I have not done anything for you, and I would like it noticed that those are three separate things and that you are about to make a speech about the first of them on the strength of the second."

Marn Ottery stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"I was not going to," she said. "Four of those are on record in this room and not one of them was ever thanked for, and I am not going to be thanked for eleven paces down a stair either. That is the whole of what I came down for."

She looked at the two of them for a moment longer, and the clerk of twenty-nine had her face down over the two joined tables and did not look up when the door of that room opened, and neither of the other two said one word after the woman in the doorway went out into the stair.

---

She took the four flights down the way anybody takes a stair, which is fast at the top and slower in the middle, and she kept her hand on the rail the whole way without deciding to.

The building empties downward between the fifth and the sixth. There is a stretch of that stair about halfway down where the window is at the height of a man's chest and the light comes in level off the yard and lies along the treads in a long pale bar, and she came down through that bar and her own shadow went out ahead of her onto the wall and then went back up over her feet.

She stopped on the turn above the ground floor. She was not tired. She stood there with her hand still on the rail while the boiler below her went through what it goes through, and she worked out that she had one of two things to do with the rest of that morning and that both of them were the same size.

Then she went down the last flight and out of that building, and the door of it shut on its frame behind her, and at the bottom of four flights in a building where nobody had ever been told anything, she turned, and which way she turned is not on a piece of paper in this empire and was not going to be.

The sixth hour was done without her.

The case went in behind the boards at the sixth hour, on time, the way it goes in every working day of the year. The clerk of about twenty-nine wrote the line she writes with her own hand, and the line says the date and the hour and the number of sheets that came out of the case, and it says nothing whatever about who was standing at the boards.

What changed at the far end of those two tables was that her hands stopped once, long enough for the nib to leave a dot on the page, and then went on.

The number in that line was the same as the number in the line of the day before, and it was the same as the number in the line of the day before that, and in eleven years it has never once been a different number on a morning when the woman who owns the room was standing at the end of the boards. That is not a thing anybody on that floor is going to put beside anything, and nobody on that floor is going to tell the woman of twenty-four what is in the line she cannot see, and nobody is going to tell the clerk that the door of that room opened at all.

Four things went up onto the wall above the case, one to a nail each. The lowest of those four has been in one hand for about four years, and it is the one that none of the three women at those boards can account for. There is a drawer under that wall and it has not been opened in eleven years; four things are in it, and two of the four have never been looked at by anybody, and the knife is in a drawer of its own, and not one of the three has ever said which one.

The door of that room stood open from about the fifth hour to about the sixth, and nobody in that building walked past it and looked in.

---

She came back in at about the seventh hour.

She came in the way she had come in for eleven years, which is to say she came in and sat down at the end of the boards and put her hand flat on the wood, and she did not say one word about where she had been or how long she had been gone, and neither of the other two said one word about it, and the clerk of about twenty-nine did not look up then either.

The pen was on the wood beside the book and not in it. It had been like that for about two months and it was like that at the seventh hour, and at the seventh hour of that morning it was the only object in that room that anybody had moved and nobody was going to be told about it.

Marn Ottery looked at the wall above the case for a while. The lowest of the four was where it had always been and it was in the hand that put it there, and she did not take it down, and she did not say anything about it, and by the time that floor shut she had not said one word out loud in that room for the first time in eleven years, and there was nothing whatever on a piece of paper anywhere in this empire to say that she had.
