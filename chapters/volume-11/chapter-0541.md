# Chapter 0541: A Man In A Felt Apron Saying Out Loud That He Is Going To Stop Stopping At That End Of The Run, And A Woman Who Said No

She had been on the passage side of that bar for eight weeks with her two hands flat on it, and he had come down that run four times a day across the whole of them, and neither of them had put one word in the other's ear since a month that is gone.

He is in a felt apron and has gone up and down that stone for forty years. She is about twenty-six, and four years at the near end of that run is the whole of what she is in that building, and there is nothing about her anywhere in it that anybody could go and look up.

Nothing has come down that run for four years. The lock at the far end of it belongs to somebody else and the room beyond it is shut, and the four hundred yards between the two ends is not walked this month by anybody in this matter.

It is the fourth day of the third week of the first month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fourth hour.

---

He came out of the cold with the rack riding on his hip and nothing on the rack, and he got to the last nine foot of it and stopped, and he did not put the rack down.

The cold comes off flags in a fixed order. Off the stone first, while a man is still walking toward it, then off the front of a coat, then off the backs of a pair of hands. He came the last nine foot of it with his jaw shut against it and came the last of it slower than he had come any of the other crossings that morning, which is a thing he would not have admitted to.

The apron had gone the colour of the ground it stands on, everywhere except across the middle of it, where two hands have kept it darker for forty years. For four years the rack has carried nothing and has still been on the same hip four times a day, and his hand goes to the same place on it without his deciding to put it there, and when he stopped, the rack went on swinging a little by itself and he stood and let it finish.

"**I have worked a thing out this month and I am going to say it out loud where you can hear it, and then I am going to do it, and I have not asked you for one thing and I am not going to. From tomorrow I come down that stone four times a day the way I have for forty years and I do not stop at this end of it.**"

She did not take her hands off the bar.

"You have not asked me and I am not going to help you with it and I am not going to be thanked for the not helping."

"I have not said I wanted you to help me."

"You have said what you are going to do and you have said it nine foot off my hands, and that is the same as asking, and you have known that as long as I have."

"Then take it as asked and say no to it. I will take it."

"I am not going to say no to a thing you have not asked me."

"You have just said no to it by saying that."

"I have said nothing at all. If I had said no to you this morning you would have had it. You would have carried it up that run in your head for the rest of the day and you would have been a man somebody had refused, and that is the only thing about you in this empire that anybody could ever produce."

He went a little way along the stone with his hand on the rack and came back.

About four foot of it, turned round on the same boot, and four foot back. The rack swung an inch on his hip when he turned and did not knock anything on the way.

"That is a long way round a thing that takes one word."

"That is the whole of what I have got and I am not going to spend it on one word in a passage."

---

He stayed at the last nine foot of that run for the rest of the hour.

He had said he was going to stop stopping at that end from tomorrow, and he said it about the fourth hour, and then he did not go. He stood there with the empty rack against his leg and the light coming off the flags in a long bar and reaching about halfway, and he was in the bar and she was in the bar with him, and neither of them put anything into the other for about as long as it takes that housing to swallow the bar and give it back.

At about the half hour the cold came off the stone the way it does when the light has gone off it, and she took one hand off the bar and put it back.

Three or four seconds is how long it was off. She had not been aware of taking it off and she was aware of every part of putting it back, and from nine foot away there is nothing there to be seen.

"You are standing at the last nine foot of a run telling me you are not going to do it tomorrow," she said, to the bar, "and you are doing it now. You are doing the opposite of the thing you came to say and I am not going to be the one who points that out, and if I point it out I am agreeing with you that there is something here, and there is something here and I have known it for eight weeks and I put myself on this side of that bar on purpose."

"I have not said you did."

"You have not said I did."

"I said I am not going to ask you why and I am not going to, and I meant it when I said it and I am not going to go back on it because I have changed my mind about a stone."

"That is the whole of what you have ever said to me and it has taken you a month to say the rest of it, and I am not going to be thanked for the month."

---

He went up the run at the hour the bar goes home and stopped before he was out of the light and said one more thing, flat, to the flags, and then he took the run up and it was gone out of the passage.

He stood with his shoulder across the run, which is what a man does when he is talking down a corridor and not to a person in it. He did not turn round. Four short arcs in the flags under the prongs take the edge off, so the rack made no sound at all when he shifted his weight from one leg to the other, and he stood there for the length of the sentence and then went.

"**The housing wants oil about four times a year and it is that time of year. I am not going to put it in your hands and I am not going to ask you for it, and there is not going to be a second time that either of those is said out loud in this passage.**"

She stood with her hands on the bar after he was gone and did not answer that either, and the bar went home into its housing at the hour it always goes home, with her two hands flat on it, on the side of it a man coming down that run can see her from about halfway.

---

She waited.

She had worked the distance out herself in a month that is gone, by walking, and she knew what it was about: a thing said at that bar in a working voice does not carry to a man coming down that run at all until he is inside about the last nine foot of it, and she had the figure to within a few yards of where he had actually stopped.

She waited until the sound of his boots had gone up past the end of the light. Then she said it, out loud, to the bar, at the top of her voice, with nobody within nine hundred yards of her who could hear a word of it.

Her hands stayed where they were. She put her chin up a little, the way a person does across the length of a room, and she said it twice as loud as the working voice she uses for a man nine foot off her, and the flags took it and gave back something thinner, and the passage went on being four hundred yards long in every direction.

"**No.**"

And then, because a word on its own is not a thing anybody does and she wanted it to be the thing she had actually done, she said the rest of it into the cold, once, and it took about as long as it takes a bar to go home.

"**You are going to keep stopping at this end of that run. I am not going to let you not stop, and I have said it now to the only object in this building that cannot answer me, and there is not a column in this empire a woman at a lock can be put in for having refused a thing nobody asked her, and there is not going to be one, and neither of those two is going to change. I am not going to say it to your face, and it is not going to be said twice, and nobody is going to be thanked for the stopping or asked what it was for, and if you heard none of this then I have said a word out loud in an empty passage, which is what I am, and I knew that when I did it.**"

Nobody heard it. She was the only person in that building who could have heard it and she was the one who said it.

The passage was exactly as it had been the moment before, and getting through the rest of the hour is the whole of what is left. A lock, a bar, a housing, a broom, one high window with the light off the far half of the run, four hundred yards of flags with a woman on them. Not one item on the list had a person in it who could be told.

---

Then it was done and it was in her, and there was nowhere to put it.

A refusal that nobody hears is not a refusal that has been made. It is not a refusal that has been refused either. It is a word in a woman's mouth in a passage with nothing standing in it, and the only party in this matter that can be entered nowhere and can be asked nothing is a room, and that is the whole of what she had to say it to.

She had told a man at a bar in a month that is gone that a decision is a party, that she had made two since the spring, and that she was not going to make a third.

She had made one, and she had not known she was making it when she opened her mouth, and she had made it in the four hundredth repetition of something she had been doing since a spring that is gone. A refusal is a decision the same way a hand on a bar is a decision, and neither of the two is a thing a person can be shown having chosen or having not chosen.

The rack had been empty for four years and she had been standing at that end of the run for four of them. The housing wanted oil about four times a year. A man had told her in that passage that he was not going to leave it for her, and he was going to leave it for her, and she was not going to be thanked for anything. Nothing in that building or out of it had changed at any hour of that day except one thing, and the one thing was a word that had gone about nine hundred yards and stopped.

She did not know whether he had heard it. She was never going to know, and he was never going to know that she had said it, and the two of them were the only people in this empire who had anything to do with it, and neither of them could put it anywhere.

The figure she has had in her head since the spring of her fourth year is on no paper, it has got no heading over it anywhere, and it is not going on anything this month. A word said out loud at a bar has not moved it and is not going to be handed to anybody.

Her hands had gone from being hands to being a way of holding herself up, which is a thing nobody standing in one place longer than an hour can account for noticing, and there is no way to be shown having noticed it. The passage went up behind her into the dark the way it goes every working morning of four years.

She stood with her hands on that bar until they were the only part of her that was not the colour of the stone, and then the light went off the flags and she was in the dark at the end of it, and the last nine foot of the run was empty.
