# Chapter 0533: A Woman At A Lock Putting Her Two Hands Back On The Bar Where A Man Coming Down That Run Can See Her From About Halfway, And A Man In A Felt Apron Who Is Not Going To Say What He Thinks It Means

She is about twenty-six, and four years at that end of it is the whole of what she is in that building. Her name is on no roll in it and never has been, and she has never been one of that building's clerks, and there is nothing about her anywhere in there that anybody could go and look up.

The bar stands at the near end of the flags, and at the hour it always does it goes back into the housing that holds it. The lock at the far end of that run belongs to somebody else, and whatever stands beyond it is neither opened nor looked into, and the four hundred yards between the two ends is not walked this month by anybody in this matter.

There has been nothing coming down that run for four years.

It is the fourth day of the third week of the eleventh month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fourth hour.

---

He came out of that cold with the rack riding on his hip and nothing on the rack, the way it has been every one of the four times a day for about four years. The felt of the apron is grey with the dust of that run and goes a shade darker in the middle of it, and the rack has four prongs and has not carried anything for so long that the prongs have polished the stone in four short arcs where the same four arcs have been going for four years.

There is one window high up that run and about the fourth hour the light from it comes down the stone at a slant and lies along the flags in a long bar, and it reaches about halfway. Everything past the middle of that bar is the same dark it is at the other three hours of the day, and a person standing in the bar is standing in the only part of four hundred yards that has anything in it.

She had her hands in the sleeves of her coat and her back to the cold and her face to the wall, and a man on that run does not get within about nine foot of her at all before he is beside her, and that was where she had put herself about a month ago.

Nine foot is about four paces for a man carrying nothing and it is about five for a man whose hip has a rack on it, and she has known both figures for four years and has never once had to use either, because what she wanted was to be a person who could not be put in front of anything, and the cold on the passage side is the price of it and has always been the price of it.

He was at about two hundred yards when he saw the shape standing in the light with nothing in front of it, and he stopped.

The rack went on moving a little after he stopped, because the hand that is on a hip does not stop the same moment, and the prongs went through four short arcs in the stone doing about the same as they had been doing for four years, and it was a small noise and it carried in a passage a long way.

A man who has gone up and down a stone four times a day for forty years does not stop for a woman at the end of one. He went on coming down it. But he came the last hundred yards slower than he had come the first three hundred, and he was at the last nine foot of it before he was beside her, and he stopped there.

"**You have gone back round to the passage side of that bar.** I have not asked you why and I am not going to, and I want it said out loud where you can hear it that I said all of that. I told you in a month that is gone that I had settled in my own head that I was not going to make a rule about an empty rack, and I have not changed that and I am not going to."

"I have not asked you to notice a bar."

"You have not asked me to. **What I have got is that a person who can be seen from halfway down a run is a person a thing can be put in front of. The whole reason you went round the back of that bar in a month that is gone was so that nothing could be, and you have undone it on purpose, and I can see you from two hundred yards because of where you are standing. I do not know what you think you have got now and I am not going to be told, and I am not going to say it a second time.**"

The bar was cold under her hands. It had been cold every morning for four years and it took about twenty minutes to get past the cold and then it stopped being cold and became a thing she was holding, and this morning it had been cold the whole of the time she had been standing at it.

She put her hands on it.

A bar that has stood in a cold passage for four years is not the same as one that has not, and a person learns it and then stops being able to feel it, which is the trouble, because it all comes back the moment she steps off the flags and the thing starts being cold under her again.

It took about as long as that bar takes to go into its housing and come out again, and she did it on the passage side, where she has stood for four years with both hands on it, and the hands went on flat and did what they have always done.

"There was a time in a month that is gone," she said, to the bar, in the ordinary way, "when I went round the back of this and worked out that being the cheapest person in a building is a rate and not a state, and that the only way out of a rate is to be somebody who is wanted something from. I did not take that and I have not taken it, and I stood on the cold side of a bar for a month in the meantime and nobody put anything in front of me and nobody ever was going to. **A decision is a party. I made one at the end of a run in a month that is gone, and there is nowhere standing at which a decision of a woman at a lock could be put, and I have made two since the spring and I am not going to make a third. That is the whole of why my hands are where they were this morning, and I am not saying it to you, and you are not going to be thanked for hearing it.**"

He had the rack on his hip and he was nine foot off her and he had said he was not going to say it a second time, and he did not say it a second time.

---

He stood there for a while with the empty rack against his leg.

He had worked out on the way down that she had not come back for the light and had not come back for the warmth, because the passage side of that bar is the colder of the two by a long way and any person in that building knows it. What she had come back for was the two hundred yards, and there was no such thing as asking her about it, and he had said out loud that he was not going to be told, and a man who says that out loud in a passage is a man who has given the whole of it away for nothing.

So the cheapest second hearer in this city had put herself in the one place on that line where a man coming down it does not have to get to the last nine foot to find her. By her own act. On purpose. In about half a minute, with a man nine foot away who could not do one single thing about it.

He took the flags back up with the rack still empty on his hip. That was the fourth crossing of the day, and the month has fourteen of them left in it, and every one of them is going to be exactly like this one, and the second half of his own sentence is still the half he has not said. He said in a month that is gone that he was not going to tell her, and he has not told her, and the first half of it went nine foot up those flags and the rest of it went down that passage and is not going anywhere, and she has not asked and is not going to.

Going up is slower than coming down on the same stone, and a man who has done both four times a day for forty years does the two at different rates without either of them being a decision. He took the last nine foot at the rate he takes them with, and then the flags went on being four hundred yards long and the bar went on being a box at the near end of them with a catch on it.

---

She stood with her hands on that bar for the rest of the hour.

There is not much to do at the near end of a run that nobody walks. There is the stone to be swept, and it is swept, and it is swept again; there is the housing the bar goes back into at the hour it goes back in, and the housing wants oil about four times a year; and there is the cold, which is not a thing to be done anything about and is simply the shape of that building from about the ninth month to about the second. She had done all of it four thousand times. She had got so good at the sweeping that she could take a length of it in about the time it takes a sheet to be signed, and she had got so bad at standing still that standing still had become the hardest work at that end of the run.

A body standing still at the end of a run does three things and none of them is work. The shoulders go down. The breath goes short and then goes back to where it was without being told to. And the hands, if they have been put somewhere out of the way, stay on for a length of time that has nothing to do with the person standing and everything to do with the fact of taking them off being a decision, and she had got so far as to know all three of them and not to be able to stop any one of them happening.

She had worked the distance out herself. She had done it in the first fortnight of standing where she stands now, and she had done it by walking, and she knows that a person coming down that run in good light sees the shape of her at about two hundred yards, and sees the back of her coat at about a hundred and fifty, and has to be inside eighty before there is anything in it to look at.

He was stopped at ninety.

She had not put herself at the bar to be seen. A man in a felt apron, who has gone up and down that stone four times a day for forty years, told her in a month that is gone that a person who cannot be shown anything is the cheapest person in a building. She had gone round the back of the bar that same month to find out whether he was right. He had been right. And there had been nothing at all in the four weeks since that she had wanted to be protected from.

The light came off the flags as the hour went on and left her in the dark at the end of it, and her hands were the only part of her that was not the colour of the stone. There is a figure she has had in her head since the spring of her fourth year. It is on no paper and it is not going on any, and it has got no heading over it anywhere in this empire, and it went out of that building once in a spring that is gone without ever touching a sheet on the way out, and it was staying where it is this month.

Four hundred yards of flags with one person standing at the end of them, and the light going off all of it from the near end backwards, and the only thing in the passage that had not changed since the fourth hour was the pair of hands on the bar. That is a thing about a person and not about a bar, and there is nothing anywhere in this empire that could be shown a woman standing still at the end of a run.

The bar went home into its housing at the hour it always goes home, and her two hands were flat on it when it did, on the one side of it where a man coming down that run can see her from about halfway.
