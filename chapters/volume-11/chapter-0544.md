# Chapter 0544: A Man In A Felt Apron Leaving A Tin Of Oil On The Flags Nine Foot Off A Bar, Three Weeks After Saying Out Loud In That Same Passage That He Was Not Going To Leave It For Her

He had said it in that passage about three weeks ago, in the ordinary way, to the flags, on his way up, and nobody had thanked him for it and nobody had asked him one word about it since.

He is in a felt apron. Forty years is how long he has gone up and down that stone, four times a day, and there is a rack on his hip with four prongs and there has not been anything on the prongs for four years, and the prongs have polished the stone in four short arcs that the same four arcs have been going for four years.

The rest of the thing he has been not saying since about four years ago is still not being said. It did not come out this morning either. It is not hers and she has not asked for it.

It is the second day of the second week of the second month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fourth hour.

A thing said at a bar on the way up a run in the ordinary way is said to flags. The distance between a man saying a thing and a woman hearing it is about nine foot, and he had known it for a long time, and it had never once stopped him.

---

He came down out of the cold with the rack on his hip and a tin under his other arm, and he got to the last nine foot of that run and put the tin down on the flags.

It is a small tin and it is about a hand high and it has a handle off one side and it is about three-quarters full of something that has gone the colour of the stone. He set it down on the flags about nine foot off the bar, upright, the way a person sets a thing down when they mean to leave it where it is, and he did not push it, and he did not say one word about it.

A tin standing on stone in a cold passage does not move and does not go over and does not leak. What it does is go slowly colder than everything else around it. By about the fourth hour a hand put on it would feel it at once and a hand put on the flags either side of it would not, and that is the whole of what a tin does for four weeks before anybody opens it.

Then he took the run up and it was gone out of the passage.

She looked at it for a while after that. Nobody had ever wanted one single thing out of her in that building in four years, and nothing had ever been put in front of her in that building in four years. A tin of oil on the flags is a thing that has been put in front of her, and there is nowhere in this empire to put a woman who was offered one and did not want it, and there is nowhere to put one who did.

She did not go down to it. She went back to her two hands on the bar and the tin stayed where it was, and there was nothing at all in her hands, and she stood there with them until the light had laid itself along about half the flags and stopped.

---

He came down the next working morning at about the fourth hour and stopped at the last nine foot of it, and the tin was on the flags, and she was at the bar, and neither of them said anything for about as long as it takes that bar to go home.

Neither of them was looking at the tin. He was looking at the far end of four hundred yards of flags and she was looking straight along the length of her own arms at the middle distance between her hands, and both of those were the ordinary way of not looking at a thing in a room.

"You said in this passage about three weeks ago that you were not going to leave it for me."

"I said a number of things in this passage about three weeks ago."

"You said that one about that tin. You said it to the flags on your way up and I heard it and you knew I heard it and you have not asked me whether I did."

"I have not asked you anything about that tin and I am not going to and nobody is going to thank me for the not asking, and I have not asked you for it either, and I never said what I was going to do with it."

"You have now told me what you did with it, by doing it."

"I have put a thing down on a floor. That is all I have done and I have done it in a working passage at an hour I have been in that passage about four times a day for forty years, and you are not going to be thanked for noticing it and you are not going to be asked what I think you are going to do with it."

"I am not going to be asked what I am going to do with it either. That is the arrangement of this end of this run and it has been the arrangement for four years and neither of us put it there."

---

She did not open it.

That is the whole of the first week. It stood on those flags where he had put it, and the catch of that housing went the same way it has gone for four years, which is to say that it took about as much doing as it takes to shut a drawer, and the housing wants oil about four times a year and this was that time of year.

A drawer that has gone dry in the cold does not open. It goes a hand's width and then it stops, and then it goes the rest of the way with a noise, and the noise of it is the whole of what a person hears at the hour a bar goes home. She heard it every working morning of the fortnight and could not have said afterwards which part of it had been the noise.

On the fourth working morning the tin was about four foot further along the flags than he had left it.

She had not opened it and had not carried it and had not thrown it and had not put it anywhere. She had walked it about four foot with the side of her boot, in the ordinary way a person moves a thing that is in the way of a broom, and the nine foot of stone that is not swept in the middle of that floor runs up the wall on the other side of the passage, and the tin is now at the near end of it.

She did not tell him she had done that and she is not going to.

What she had done was move a tin out of a broom's way and nothing else, and a man nine foot off at the end of the run would have had to be watching the floor rather than the passage to see it, and neither of them was watching the floor.

---

On the fifth working morning he came down with the rack and got to the last nine foot and looked, and he did not say one word, and he picked the tin up and put it back on the same flags he had put it on the first morning, about nine foot off the bar, and left it standing there.

Then he took the run up and it was gone out of the passage.

That is the first time he had touched it and it is the first time she had, and there have been about nine movements of that tin between the two of them in a fortnight and the two of them are the only people in this empire who have any standing at all in the matter of it.

His hand went down on the handle of it and not on the body of the thing, which is what a man does who has decided in advance that he is not touching it, and then it came up and went across the flags and went down again with the same care. A tin going down onto stone from a hand is not a sound anybody four hundred yards off would hear, and about nine foot of flags is a long way to carry anything at all.

**He did not open it and she did not open it and neither of them is going to open it. A thing in a passage that two people have each moved and that neither of them has opened is not an offer and is not a refusal and is not a decision anybody could be shown having made. The housing wants oil about four times a year, and it caught dry, and it went on catching dry for a fortnight after that.**

---

It was still on those flags at the end of that month and at the end of the month after.

A tin standing on flags does not improve by standing. It thickens at the top and dulls at the sides and does not go back to where it came from, and after a month or so the only thing about it that would tell anybody anything is the sound a boot makes going past it, and there is one boot in the building that passes it four times a day.

Nobody in that building is going to be told that it is there, and nobody is going to be thanked for having brought it and nobody is going to be asked why it has stayed. There is no column in this empire for a man who goes up and down a stone four times a day and leaves a tin on the flags, and there is none for a woman who boots it four foot along a floor.
