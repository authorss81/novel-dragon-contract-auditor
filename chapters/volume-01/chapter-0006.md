# Chapter 6: The Emergency Stair

On the tenth of the month the Reed Cut held up a black flag on the sluice post, which meant the water was coming, and the flag came down again at noon, which meant it was not, and by the time Marek came down the terrace steps the flag had been up twice more.

Marek had slept four hours in a cot above the district register and had not slept well, and he had spent the first hour of the morning being told, by four separate people on three different pieces of ground, what the hold had done.

It had saved the field. That was the first thing, and everybody said it the same way, and none of them said it happily.

The hold came down from the provincial clerk's office on the eighth, sealed, with a First Seat administrative reference, and it said the Venn file was not to be enforced, removed, or assigned pending further notice. So no creditor could take the standing barley. So the girl could not be made a debtor by seizure of the ground she stood on. So for four days the Venn lower field was, in the plain and legally operative sense, untouchable, and the district registrar had told Mara Venn so in front of two witnesses and had then told her what else it meant.

"It means you can't sell the early cut," the registrar had said. "You can't assign the crop forward to a seed factor. You can't put the ground up for winter work-share. Anything that moves the field or what's on it is an assignment, and the hold forbids it."

"How am I to feed myself till the fourteenth?" Mara had said.

The registrar had not had an answer to that, and had said so, and had been honest about it, and Mara had not forgiven him for it and had not shouted at him either, which Marek thought was the hardest thing in the district.

The second thing the hold had done was this: the ditching was not being done.

Aldren Venn's duty, as the original instrument had it, was the field-side upkeep, and Aldren was dead, and no one had accepted it, and so on the seventh morning the ditch on the western side of the lower field was three feet into a bank that was due to be cut by the water office crew tomorrow, and no one would cut it, because cutting it would be an act of maintenance by a person who had not accepted the duty, and everyone in the lane knew the sentence, and nobody wanted to be the first one to be an example.

Tavi Venn had been cutting it anyway. Alone, at first light, for three days, with a spade and a bar, and getting the ground out of it a foot at a time, and Marek had watched her do it for an hour on the second day and had not once been able to make himself say *stop*, because what would he have said? Stop, because if you are seen maintaining the field-side then you are the field-side maintainer, and if you are the field-side maintainer then you are assuming a continuing duty, and the original instrument required a signature to assume it, and if she signed then the thing Marek was trying to beat was beaten anyway, by his own client's hand, in a lane, at dawn, for free.

"You cannot make her not do it," Tamsin said, coming down the bank with her case. "You can only stop her being the only one doing it."

---

The Mosswake Roll Court was built for weather. It had a low door onto the embankment that a river could push open and a clerk's window that a court could push shut, and on the tenth the whole building smelled of wet stone and the tar they had put on the terrace steps, and the terrace itself was half under.

Getting a matter listed was, Marek discovered, not a matter of justice. It was a matter of arithmetic.

"The docket is closed," Ivet Sarn said, from behind the window, for the fourth time in two days. "The fourteenth is the fourteenth. A rehearing cannot be listed into a closed flood docket unless the matter is a matter of immediate public necessity, and if it is a matter of immediate public necessity then you do not need to list it, you go to the magistrate's stair."

"Magistrate Rell is on the terrace," Tamsin said. "At the emergency stair. With the district crew."

"Then go to the emergency stair," Sarn said, "and do not come back and tell me that I refused you, because I have not refused you anything, I have told you the arithmetic twice."

The emergency stair was a stone stair outside the court with a rail on the seaward side and a canvas roof that the wind was working at, and at the top of it, on a landing with the door open behind her, was Magistrate Leda Rell.

She was perhaps fifty. She had a river-court look: boots that had been resoled more than once, a face weathered at the cheekbones, and a clerk beside her who was writing on a board held against the doorframe because the wind had taken the paper twice. She was reading a list of houses.

"Clerk Kest," she said, without looking up. "I know who you are. Sarn has sent me four pages about you in two days, which is a record, and the last of them said that if you came up the stair she would not put you off."

"I'd like the matter listed for a public evidentiary rehearing before the fourteenth."

"On what ground?"

"The correction in the Venn file was filed on the ninth of the eighth month under a flood memorandum dated the eighth of the eighth month, which the district water office says it issued to clear a backlog before the season, and the correction contains a continuation of a fixed-term repayment attached to the land instead of the named signer, and its witness mark on the summary was copied. I have a comparison, I have a witness, and I have a second file in the same district carrying the same sentence in the same place in the line, dated four months earlier, in a form that names a minor."

"Who is the second file's claimant?"

"Orra Dunn, of the upper seed barn. Her daughter is fifteen. The daughter has signed nothing."

"Mm." Rell handed the list to the clerk and came out onto the landing and stood in the wind, which she did not appear to notice, and which did nothing to her, and Marek understood that she was a person who had been in worse weather than this and had stopped discussing it. "And if I list it, what do you think happens?"

"I think the court hears the source leaf, the witness, and the keeper, and rules on whether the field-side duty and the winter-seed repayment survive Aldren Venn's death, and whether they can attach to his daughter."

"I think that too. Do you know what I think happens when I list a matter into a flood docket in the tenth month?" She turned and looked at the water. "The first thing that happens is that eleven other matters that were ready fall off the end of the docket into the spring, because the week is a week. The second thing that happens is that every hour of the court goes into a precedent question instead of into eleven hundred relief rolls, and I have four hundred and sixty households who do not care about Aldren Venn and will not be able to eat on the fourteenth because of him. The third thing that happens is that the district office will send me a note about procedure, and the provincial clerk will send me a note about the hold, and I will have to answer both, and while I am answering them the water will do what it does."

"You could issue a closed order," Marek said. "Tonight. Preserve the standing crop and release the seed against the Venn field, without a hearing, on the ground of immediate necessity."

Rell turned her head.

"Yes," she said. "I can. I have the power and I have used it eleven times in nine years, and it is the reason this district feeds itself in a bad year. I would do it tonight if you asked me to, and here is exactly what it would be. I would order that the standing crop of the Venn lower field be preserved and not assigned, and that the district issue seed against it, and that no creditor, tenant, or surety may take the crop or the ground until this court sits again. And no party would be heard. You would not be heard. The widow would not be heard. The girl would not be heard. And the order would be founded on the corrected file, because that is the file, and because I have held the corrected precedent as ordinary for five months and I have no evidence that it is bad except a clerk's comparison, and I would rather feed four people on the strength of a bad instrument than feed nobody."

"You'd be deciding about their field without them."

"I would be deciding about their field without them," Rell agreed, "which is what I do, in bad years, and it is the reason eleven hundred households ate late once and nobody starved, and I have never once apologized for it." She looked at him. "You want a hearing. I am not refusing you a hearing. I am telling you what a hearing costs, in this district, in this month, in this year, so that you can stand in front of four hundred and sixty households and say out loud that you asked for it. Can you say it?"

"Yes."

"Then say it to me now, because I want to hear whether you have understood it, and then I will list it, because the seal on the hold is not mine and a provincial hold on a district file is not a reason to leave a girl of seventeen outside the only door that can hear her."

That was the moment Marek understood that Rell was not his opponent, and that this made everything harder.

---

Tamsin was at the bottom of the stair with her case open on the stone, and Marek told her, in order, everything.

He gave her the file. Not the packet — the file, the whole working file, opened out on the stair, in the wind, with the clerk from Rell's doorframe looking down at them and not saying anything because a clerk standing near a magistrate does not read papers.

"This is the comparison. This is the correction, and the reverse of the correction, which has a certificate impression on it and a fee entry, and the fee entry is a payment authorized to a certifier at Reed Cut and Auremar named Dray. That is a broker, not a court. This is the memorandum your Sarn entered, and this is her refusal, with the two inches, and this is the index of the received tray, and this is your own copy of the Dunn summary, which is yours and not mine, and I am not putting it in my satchel, I am showing it to you. This is the hold." He put it down last. "And this is the notice of the hearing, once Rell lists it, and I will not have the list made out until you have read every page of this."

Tamsin read for a long time, on a stair, in the wind, with the light going.

"Eight pages," she said. "I have four of them in my own books and you have had them all in your bag since the fourth month."

"Yes."

"You will not do that again."

"No."

"That is not an apology. I do not need an apology. I need a rule." She squared the pages on the stone. "New rule, from tonight, and it stands after the case is over: nothing you hold goes back into the bag. Anything you learn, I learn the same day, even if it is bad, even if it is about your mother, even if it is the thing you would rather handle alone. Especially that one. I have decided about that one already, actually, and I did not tell you, and I am telling you now so that you can plan around it."

Marek looked at the water.

"Agreed," he said.

Then they went up the lane to the Venn house, and Marek did the thing he had been walking two miles to do, which was to stop deciding.

He told Mara and Tavi about the closed order first, because the closed order was the thing they were being asked to refuse, and a person cannot refuse a thing they have not been offered.

"Magistrate Rell can preserve your crop tonight and release seed against it, and it will keep you both fed through the fourteenth, and it will be signed without either of you being in the room, and it will be founded on the corrected file, which means that in six months somebody will point at it and say the court itself had already decided the field is owed. That's the price of the closed order. Not money. What it teaches the district about this piece of paper."

"And the hearing?" Tavi said. She had not come in from the field. She had come in to the step and left her boots in the lane.

"The hearing is on the twelfth, in the morning, in public, with the source leaf if the cabinet cooperates and without it if it does not. The court hears the instrument, the witness, the keeper, the person who wrote the correction if he can be found, and you. Both of you. The hearing pauses the enforcement of the debt until the court rules, which means on the fourteenth nobody can read a notice to you as a matter of routine — and the flood may take the lower cut in the meantime, and nobody can promise you it will not."

Mara was standing in the doorway with the dish cloth again. "You want us to say yes to a hearing that may cost us the lower field, so that you can be proved right about a sentence."

"No," Marek said. "I want you to say yes or no. I will present it either way. If you say no, I go to the stair at first light and I ask Rell for the closed order, and I do not tell her why you refused, and you get your winter and I do not get the leaf."

"Why would you do that?"

"Because it is your field and your winter and you have both been in a room with a man reading you a document you did not ask for, and I have been doing that to everyone I care about since the first week of the eighth month, and it stops here."

The wind came round the corner of the house. Tavi Venn looked at her mother, and her mother looked at the field, and neither of them looked at Marek.

"I'll testify," Tavi said. "Not about my father. I don't remember my father's signature and I'll say so and they'll think it's convenient. I'll testify about the gate linkage and the ditch and what the work actually is, and I'll say that a girl of seventeen has been doing a dead man's field-side maintenance since the fourth month because nobody else would, and I'll say that on the record, because if I say it in a room it's evidence and if I say it in a lane it's just work." She pulled her hair back. "And you write down that I said it, and I get a copy, and my name on it is on a *hearing*, not on a *debt*."

"Done."

"And I want the Fenn woman. The guild examiner. Odile Fenn, she's at the court on Thursdays, and if a canal job is going to be counted against my name I want the person who counts it in the room."

Marek wrote it. He wrote it exactly as she said it, with the day and the words and the fact that she had asked for a copy, and Tavi read it back over his shoulder and made him correct one word, which was fine.

Mara spoke last, and what she said was: "I'll say about the paper on the field. I'll say the date it came, and that it was in a young man's hand, and I refused it. I'll say it twice and I'll say it the same way both times, because I have learned that a thing said two ways is a thing the other side can choose between."

"Two ways is what they do with a witness," Marek said. "One way, twice, is what stops them."

---

That night, on the terrace, with the list for the twelfth made out and the notice of the hearing typed and the witness order set for posting on the court board at the eighth hour, Marek and Tamsin walked the channel with a lamp.

She asked him the question she had said she would ask at the top of the stair, and she asked it on the bank, in the dark, with the water going past.

"When you stand in there on the twelfth, you are going to have the field, and the leaf, and the notary's refusal, and a broker's fee, and a whole year of somebody's plan," she said. "And you are going to want to say all of it. Every piece. You are going to want to explain where the sentence came from, and who has been copying it, and why there is a First Seat seal on a district field, because you have been carrying the whole architecture in your head for a week and it is a very good architecture and it is going to be sitting right there behind your teeth."

"Yes."

"Promise me you will not turn it into that."

"How do I promise that in a way that means something?"

"By what you do with the first hour." She stopped walking. "You have got about forty minutes of a clerk's memory in that room, and a widow who can only say the same thing twice, and a girl who is going to be asked the same question eleven times, and a magistrate who will let you talk until the water comes up. So the promise is this: you spend the first hour on the field, and the leaf, and the marks, and the two women, and you spend it in that order, and you do not mention the second household until the court has decided the first. And when you do mention the second household, you mention it as a thing that has happened, and not as a reason why you are right."

Marek stood in the dark by the Reed Cut gate and thought about the architecture. It was very good. It had a First Seat instruction and a broker's fee and a copied notch and a Dunn summary and a nine-year clerk's caution and a word that was beginning to feel like a door.

"That is asking me to be worse at my own case," he said.

"It is asking you to be better at theirs," Tamsin said. "The case will still be there in the ninth month. They will not."

"I promise."

"Say it properly. In words. In case I ever have to quote it to you."

"I will not use the Venn family as a route to the network," Marek said, "in the first hour, or in front of a magistrate who needs to rule on a field before the fourteenth."

"Write it down."

"It is the fourth thing on a piece of paper in my hand. Everything of mine is a piece of paper in my hand."

"Then let somebody else hold one," Tamsin said.

At the eighth hour the next morning the witness order for the twelfth went up on the Mosswake court board, printed on the court's own sheet, with the seal of the clerk's window pressed into the corner of it, and Marek came down the terrace at half past six and read it standing up, the way a man reads a thing he already knows, and went in to find Tamsin.

"You have not read the order," she said.

"I have read the order."

"You read the top of it and then you read the bottom of it and then you stood there." She was standing with her arms folded and her copy case open on the clerk's counter and there was something on her face Marek had only seen twice before, both times in a copying room, both times about a page. "The witness order was drawn on the ninth, in the morning, in this building, and it is dated the ninth, and it is sealed. It was set with the certificate broker's man first, ahead of the widow, ahead of the girl, ahead of the keeper, ahead of the district. I was in the room at eleven and the bench was at eleven and the copyist's window was at eleven and there was no one else in this building but the three of us and a lamp-trimmer. And Marek, the first name on that order is not a person. It is a certificate."
