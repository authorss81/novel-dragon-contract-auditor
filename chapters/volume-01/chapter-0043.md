# Chapter 43: Twenty-Three Doors and a Man Who Already Knew

The fourth question, asked in Greyfen about a guild levy, finds a different answer than it found at Millbrook Reach three hundred miles down the water, and the difference is a woman who is paid four shillings a day to be in a room with a sheet of paper and eleven houses in it.

Her name did not go in Nell Kest's book, because Nell Kest had been putting names in her book all winter and had learned that the second worst thing that can happen to a person is a stranger knowing her name, and the worst is a stranger writing it down.

She went round with the quarter's sheet in a leather satchel on the twenty-sixth of the fifth month, in the two hours after the sixth, when the vats were going and the doors were open, and she read the amended clause and the levy and the date out loud in each of the four houses that had signed, and she read it in the seven that had not, and she read it twice in the two where somebody had asked her to.

"You ask me the fourth one," she said, in the yard of the last house on the lane, before Nell had got the words out. "You have got it in your hand. I know the words; I say them in kitchens. Who read this to you and do they get paid if you sign."

"Yes."

"I read this to you," said the woman, "and I am paid four shillings a day to be in a room with a sheet of paper, and I am paid by the quarter and the quarter is struck in the assessment, and if the quarter comes out at nine pounds then the eleven houses in this lane are what keeps me in this work and not the Priar's factor or a man with a rack." She put the satchel down on the wall. "So the answer is yes, and I want it written down that I said yes first, before you asked the last half of it, because I have had this read at me by a woman off the vats in the third month and I have thought about it every day since and I always thought the answer would be shameful and it is not. It is a wage."

"That is not what I am here for."

"I know what you are here for, love, you are here to write it in a book and you are not going to put it in front of anybody, and that is exactly right and it is the only reason I have said it out loud instead of saying *I do not know* and going home angry about it." She picked the satchel up. "I read it in all eleven. Including the seven that did not sign. I would like that written down as well, and it is not in my wage either."

Nell Kest wrote it down in her own book in her own hand, with the date on it, and read it back, and the woman made her read back the part about the seven, and then went up the lane, and there were two hundred pounds of greyfen cloth in a satchel and nobody in the lane would ever know that the woman who read it to them is the reason.

---

Mike Caudle had been in the lane since the first of the third month, and it turned out that he had not known his name was in a box until the fifteenth of the fourth, and it turned out that he had known since the fifteenth of the fourth and had said nothing to anybody for a month, and it turned out that the reason he had said nothing was not dignity.

Nell found it on the twenty-seventh, at the fourth hour, in the yard, with the bar of soap on the wall because somebody had hung it there.

"He asked me a question," Mike Caudle said. "The fifteenth of the fourth. He put his thumb on the line that says I owe nothing and he said, *is that your name, Mike*, and I said it was, and he said, *well, nobody has asked you anything and nobody is going to*, and he was pleasant about it, and that is the part I have not got past." He turned the bar over in his hands. "So I have had a month of my name on a piece of paper in my own kitchen and I have not asked my wife about it, and if you are going to write that down I would rather you wrote all of it and not the first half."

"Nery knows."

"Nery knew on the sixteenth and did not tell me for a month, and the reason she did not tell me is that she has been the house everybody is kind to since the first month and she did not want to be the house that got a man in a good coat to come and be told things by her husband." He set the soap down. "That is her whole reason and it took her a month to find it and I have had a month of thinking I was being patient."

"Does the guild's second instrument ask anybody whether you object."

"No."

"Then nobody is going to ask you, Mike, and that is not a thing anybody has done to you. That is a thing the paper was built to do." Nell Kest put her book away. "It was built so that the person who holds is the person who owes, and it was built in a warm room by people who never once thought about a man with nineteen years on a bench who is not going to be asked, because in their version of the world somebody is always asked. And there is no part of it that says *unless nobody asks him*, and there never was, and it is not going to be fixed by a woman with a book."

"So what do I do."

"Keep the thumb off it," said Nell. "Every time a man puts his thumb over the line, say the line out loud before he does, and make him move it. It is four words. It is the only thing I have got and it is not enough."

Mike Caudle looked at her for a moment.

"Four words is not nothing," he said, "in this house."

---

The twenty-three was a number she had written in the twelfth month in a book in a lane and had carried for six months, and by the end of the fifth month she understood it as what it actually was, which was three and a half hours a door done properly and four hours a day after the sixth hour, which is a day's pay to ask a person one question and to sit there while a person works out an answer while a vat goes.

At the eleventh door on the twenty-sixth, a woman of fifty-two read all four of them back to her without being asked twice and then said, "I have got one of these on a paper of my own and I have not asked it of anybody, because I have nobody to ask it of, and I have been carrying it since the second month."

Nell Kest stood in that doorway for about four seconds and understood, in a way she did not enjoy, that she was the person people told things to, and that being the person people tell things to is the exact thing she had gone to a furnace town to stop being, and that it was also the only reason a paper in a kitchen in Greyfen had ever been asked a question at all.

"What is the paper," she said.

"A guild thing. About my boy."

"Then ask him the fourth one first and tell me what he says, and do not tell me what to do with it either, and write down the day." She wrote the woman's name in her own book, in her own hand, with the date on it, because the woman had given it and it was hers to give. "And if he asks you who told you to ask him, say the lane and not me, and if he comes up to the table I will stand four feet away and I will not say a word."

---

The Nine Elms had signed on the twenty-ninth of the twelfth and had wanted something for it since the middle of the second month and had not been told what, because the thing they wanted for it was the assessment, and the assessment was in the fourth month, and after the fourth month the answer to a question about the assessment is a room you have to be in.

They asked her on the twenty-eighth, at the sixth hour, with a woman of forty-one in the doorway who had signed because her husband had signed and not asked her.

"Tell me what we get," she said. "I have signed a thing and I have not been told what it is for and I am not asking you to tell me what to do. I am asking you to tell me what we get, and if the answer is *not very much, and it is cheaper than the alternative*, then I can go and be a person who chose that, and I would like to have chosen it."

Nell Kest stood in the doorway of the Nine Elms and found that she had four hours that day and twenty-three doors to do and had just been asked the only question in this trade that she had an answer to.

"Two things," she said. "One, and this is the one that is a fact: the Tool Fund holds four pounds eleven and fourpence and it has held between three and five pounds the whole time I have been copying its schedules, and it has never been empty, and there have been three claims on it in two years. Nine pounds a quarter into that is money into a box that is not empty, and I do not know where the rest of it goes because nobody in this trade has ever read the balance, and I have written that down twice in two books in two districts and I am the only person who has both."

"And the other."

"The other is that I do not know, and Marl Crenn has said in advance in her own room that this will be fought and lost inside a year, and she is right, and I would rather you heard the losing from me in a doorway than from a notice." She put her hand on the doorframe the way Nery Caudle put her hands flat on a sill. "What you get is a clause that says a girl who leaves takes her own tools, and the next woman on your bench is not answerable for what the last one owed, and you will never in your life see the effect of it because the thing it stops is a thing that has not happened in this lane yet. That is what a thing is worth before it has happened. Ask anybody who has kept a bridge."

---

The professional copyist on the Greyfen road had charged for four hundred sheets in the month of the fifth and had a waiting list, and the waiting list was a piece of paper nailed inside the window with *copying* painted on the glass above it, and the list had nine names on it and four of them were trades.

Nell went past it on the twenty-ninth at about the fifth hour and stopped, which she had not planned to do.

"Four hundred sheets."

"Four hundred and eleven," said the woman in the shop, "and the six free ones I told you about in the ninth month were anger and I would like people to know there is a price, and there is a price, and it is four pence and it has been four pence for eleven years in this window." She did not stop working. "What is it this time."

"Twenty-three houses."

"Twenty-three houses is a fortnight at four pence and I will not do it at four pence for a lane with a board on it." The pen went on. "I will do nine of them at four pence and I will do the other fourteen at two and I will not tell you which is which, and if anybody in this lane asks me for all twenty-three cheap I will say I am full until the sixth."

"You have never been full."

"I have been full since the eleventh," said the professional copyist on the Greyfen road, "and I have had a waiting list since the second week of the third month, and I want you to understand what has happened, because nobody has written it down and you are the only person I have seen who writes things down. I went from being a woman with anger and a window to being a woman with a queue. That is what happened to the four questions. Somebody else's anger went out of a door and turned into my week."

---

She went to the next province on the twenty-ninth, on the cart, in daylight, and told nobody she was going, which was the third time in five months she had done that and the first time she had done it in daylight.

She took nothing in. Not the four questions, not a copy of the amended clause, not the levy book, not the sheet with the four names and her apology in chalk under it. She took her own book and a pencil and a loaf of bread and a shilling for the cart, and she got the sum wrong by a penny and gave the penny and was short-told for it and did not say so.

The assessor came sixty miles. She had done that in a cart in a leather case in the first month, and she had been in a room with eleven women, and she had said *yes, I am paid to read instruments to people, and no, I am not paid for the answer*, and she had looked at Nell for about four seconds while she said it.

Nell Kest asked her for the fourth question in the room in the association's own house, in front of the eleven, and she got the same answer, and then she asked the eleventh house in the room something she had not asked anybody, which was: a question about a girl of nineteen who had been on one of their benches since Michaelmas and had been paid every week-day out of the same wage, and whose name was on a wage column and on nothing else.

"Is she a person in your file," said Nell Kest. "Or is she the person who holds."

The assessor of a Weavers' and Small Dyemasters' Association with eleven houses looked at her for about eleven seconds, which was twice as long as she had looked at anything else in five months.

"The book has eleven houses in it," she said. "The book has a wage column and a rate column and a quarter column and a column for the levy. It has not got a column for anybody's name and it never has, and that was a decision in the year the association was formed, and I signed the book out of a drawer with that in it and I did not think about it once in nine years, and I have been asked it in my own association's room by a person who is not a member of my association."

"Could it have one."

"It could have a column," said the assessor, "and I could put eleven names in it in an afternoon, and if I did that then in about two years there would be a line in this association's book with a girl's name on it and a rate beside it, and somebody in Greyfen with a form would be able to point at a person instead of a house, and I have read what a form with a person's name in a box does to a bench in a lane, because you read mine out in a room of eleven in the fourth month and I have not been the same since."

"Then you are not going to put a column in."

"I am not going to put a column in, and I would like the eleven of you to know that I have been asked and I have said no, and I would like it in whatever she is writing in that book, because the next assessor will be a different person in about six years and she will not have been in this room." The assessor closed her leather case. "And the answer to your question is in the guild's book and not in mine, and the guild's book has her name on it, and the guild's book is the one with the fifth line on the back of its form, and I have never been able to get at it, and neither can she."

---

Nell Kest got back onto the cart at about the eighth hour and rode forty miles with a shut mouth, which is a thing she can do, and came down off it at the top of the lane at about the tenth with her hands stiff, and the first thing she did when she got in was to write the whole of it in her own book in nine lines and read it back twice, and the nine lines were not about the fifth line on the back of the form and had nothing in them that anybody could use in a room.

*Asked of the assessor of eleven houses, in her own room, in front of the eleven: whether a girl of nineteen on a bench since Michaelmas is a person in the association's book or the person who holds. Answer: the book has eleven houses and no column for a name and has not had one since the year it was formed, and she will not put one in, and the guild's book has the name in it and the guild's book is out of her reach and out of the girl's.*

*I am going to write this down and put it nowhere near a court and I am going to say why, and the reason is that the fourth question worked in a room of a hundred and a hundred is a different instrument from the same question in a kitchen, and in a kitchen a woman can say I do not know and go home, and in a room of a trade a woman who says I do not know has said it in front of eleven houses and has made herself a person who does not know, and the person who holds the book does not have that choice, and she has just told me she is not going to make it, and that is the most useful thing anybody has said to me in five months.*

*And the other thing. She is the only door in that trade, same as Emberwatch, and I asked her one question four months ago and she answered it plainly and did not build a case out of it and told me she knew what I was doing, and I have been writing about the asking and the answering for five months and I have never once written about the fact that a person can say no to me in a room of eleven and be thanked for it.*

*That is a thing I have not got questions for.*

She still had four pounds in a cloth in a drawer in Greyfen and had been to no doctor since the second week of the twelfth, and the coach fare for sixty miles came out of the same wage the cloth was out of, and she did not do the sum and she knew roughly what it was, and the not-doing-it was a decision she had made at a vat flat with a piece of chalk in the second month and had not revisited, and she was not going to revisit it on a road at the tenth hour with a hand that would not open.
