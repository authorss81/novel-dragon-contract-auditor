# Chapter 33: Four Names in Chalk

The levy is nine pounds a quarter and it is not going to be spread over eight houses, and the four that signed are going to pay all of it, and the reason it falls hardest on the Caudles is that Nery Caudle signed on the twenty-ninth of the tenth month, which is the earliest date on the whole sheet, and two clear months before the last of the other three put a name to anything.

Nell Kest had known that for about ten days and had not said it, and on the sixth day of the third month she walked an hour down the road to the last house on the dyers' lane with the levy book in her bag, because there was one person on that lane whose permission she needed and she was going to go and get it before she did anything else, and because she had learned in the second of the second month that a woman who has been four days away and comes back having done a thing to her neighbours without asking is a different animal from a woman who turns up.

The Caudle house is the last house on the lane, and it has a bench, a stove, four children, and a bar of soap on the kitchen table that is never taken off the table.

"Read it out," said Nery Caudle, before Nell had got the book open.

So Nell Kest read it out, at the kitchen table, with the children coming in and going out.

Two pounds and five and a quarter a quarter, out of one wage. The other six pounds and about fifteen shillings between the Nine Elms, Roke, and the Two Sisters. The Caudle's share could not be made even with the rest, because the Caudle had asked for her share to be taken in the same coin she had paid it in, which was two pounds five and a quarter, and that was a farthing over, and the whole was still nine pounds, and Marl Crenn had written *balance to the first-named* at the bottom of the sheet in a small hand.

Nery Caudle listened to the whole of it without moving.

"Take the quarter," she said.

"Take the quarter."

"Do not take it off the sheet, take the quarter. I mean the round quarter — three farthings is three farthings." She put her hand flat on the book. "Now say to me why it lands on me hardest, because you have not said that part, and you have come an hour to say it and you are not the sort that walks an hour for arithmetic."

"Because you signed first," said Nell Kest. "On the twenty-ninth of the tenth. And the recalculation is possible because a house signed first, and a house that signed first is the house that made it fair for the other three, and it is the house carrying the most for it, and there is no version of that in which the first house feels better afterwards."

"That is correct and I want it on the table."

"What?"

"I want it on the table at the trestle in the lane, in front of people, in chalk, with the four names on it and my name first, and I do not want you to have come round the back of me with a soft version of it at my own kitchen table at six in the evening so that I would be the kind of woman who was told something kind about herself in her own house." Nery Caudle took her hand off the book. "Mike keeps the bench. Mike has kept that bench nineteen years and we have had four children on it and there is one wage and I put two pounds and a quarter of it in a cloth off my own pay because I was not going to hand a stranger a levy off a woman's wages on the day I signed. And I would rather every person on that lane walked past a board and saw that the house which signed on the twenty-ninth of the tenth is carrying the most of what it cost, and saw the name of the woman who put it there, than be the house that had it explained to her privately."

"Nery—"

"You write my name first," said Nery Caudle, "and you write underneath that I asked for it, because I am not having that chalk board doing to me in a year's time what it did to me in January. I have not forgotten that I am the house everybody is kind to. Write it, and then go up the lane, and if the Pry comes down and gives you any trouble, you come back here and tell me and I will come and stand in the lane myself."

---

She did the household sums at the Caudle kitchen table after the washing-up, with the book open on a dry patch, because a levy is not a number, it is four households at about three and a half shillings a week each and a quarter is about thirteen weeks.

Caudle: two pounds five and a quarter, or three shillings and five farthings a week, out of one wage, and the washing is in that wage, and there is a cloth of four pounds in a drawer with a vicar's sum in it that is not the levy and is not anybody's business and has not been mentioned in this house since October.

Nine Elms: two pounds four shillings, or about three and three eighths of a shilling a week, between two wages, and the Nine Elms has eleven people in it and the two who earn are the two who signed.

Roke: the balance, or about three shillings and sixpence a week, out of a foreman's wage, and the foreman's seat is a thing Roke has had for six years and is examined every year by a man from the guild who comes on a Tuesday.

Two Sisters: the balance, or about three shillings and a halfpenny a week, and there are two women in that house and one bench between them and the older of them is fifty-eight and the younger is thirty-one and neither of them has worked anywhere else since she was twenty-two, and if either of them stopped working the other one could not keep the bench, and the levy does not care and would be the same on a house with one wage in it or four.

Then she did the one that is not a household, which was the thing Marl Crenn had not put on the sheet because the sheet is not a place for it.

The Greyfen Dyers' Tool Fund holds four pounds eleven shillings and four pence.

It has held between three pounds and five pounds for the whole of the time Nell Kest has been copying its schedules. Nine pounds a quarter off four houses is money into a box that already has money in it, and the box pays out tool debts to girls who leave, and there have been three of those in two years, and the box is not empty and has never been empty.

And nobody had ever asked where the balance went, because a levy is a levy and a fund is a fund and the amount is set by a form in a room that four women walked into in the tenth month of last year and signed, and the form does not ask a person to read the balance.

She wrote both sums out in the book, side by side, the way Hallis Dren had written hours and shillings in one column in November so that they could not be taken apart by somebody in a warm room who only wanted the money.

*Nine pounds a quarter into a fund holding four pounds eleven and fourpence. The four houses are paying to fill a box that is not empty. The box has never been empty. Three claims on it in two years and the claims are not the reason the amount is nine pounds.*

*Nobody in this trade has ever read the balance. That is the same sentence twice in four months in two trades in two districts, written in two hands, and I am the only person who has both of them, and I did not go looking for the second one, and I have not told anybody I had found it.*

---

She did it on the seventh of the third month, at the sixth hour, in the cold, with about nine people, which was twice as many as she had expected and none of them anybody she had asked.

Nine pounds a quarter. Four houses. The Caudles' two pounds and five and a quarter, read out twice, because the first time nobody said anything and the second time a woman at the back said *say the Caudle's again*, and she said the Caudle's again.

Then she took the chalk and wrote the four names on the board at the bottom, under the four questions, where anybody walking up the lane could read them.

The board's rule is that anybody may add their own name and nobody may take another person's off. The four names were not anybody's own name. They were four names of houses, entered by a woman off the vats, and the Pry was in none of them, and the four houses had been asked and one of them had asked to be first.

She wrote under them, in the same chalk, in a smaller hand:

*These four were put here by N. Kest. The Caudles asked. The other three did not. She is sorry. — N.K.*

---

The Pry had come down to look at it, and the Pry has been on that lane forty years and has never had a dispute with a man in a good coat, and the woman who kept the books there was extremely polite and had offered Nell tea in January.

"It is not a court and it is not a levy list," she said. "That board is for people to add their own names to."

"Yes."

"And you have written the name of my house on it."

"I have not. The Pry is not paying anything and that is why you came down here, and you did not need to." Nell turned the board round. "Those four are the four that signed. You are not on it. What is on it under your name is nothing, because your name is not on it."

The woman looked at the board for a while.

"And who is that line under the four."

"That is me, saying which of the four asked and which did not."

"Which asked."

"The Caudle asked me to come to her house first and put her first and say she had asked. The other three did not know they were going to be on a board until they were on it."

The woman from the Pry looked at the four names for a while longer.

"I will tell you what I think, Miss Kest, and then I will go up the lane. I think you have done a brave thing and I think you have done it about me. My house is in the next list down, the four that pay nothing, and there are four of them, and in about a year somebody is going to come to that board and say the whole levy is unfair because four houses were named in a lane. And on that day it will matter that not one of them was the Pry."

Nell Kest wrote that down in her own book that night, because a person who is owed a sentence by somebody she has just injured is owed it in ink.

---

Roke came as well, and Roke is the second house, and Roke has a foreman's seat that is not the same seat as Ostyn Vare's but is a seat.

"I want to say something about the farthing," said Roke.

"Go on."

"The quarter's sheet has the Caudle at two pounds five and a quarter, and the other three at the balance, and the balance does not come even, and every one of us has known for three weeks that the even way would be about two pounds five apiece and change, and the even way is not what is on the sheet." Roke stood with her arms folded against the cold, which is what everybody does on that lane. "The farthing is the Caudle's, because she paid hers in a cloth with her own wages in it and asked for it in the same coin. Do you know what happens to a farthing in this trade."

"No."

"It does not get paid. It gets carried. A farthing is not money. It is a thing a house says about itself, and in about two years it will be in a box somewhere, and somebody will find it, and it will be the oldest thing in the box, and it will mean that the Caudles were the first house on that lane to sign, because they would not have their share rounded to make the others comfortable." Roke uncrossed her arms. "I have told Marl Crenn this and she wrote it down and said she could not put it on the sheet, because the sheet is not a place for a house's opinion of itself. So I am telling you, and you are the only person I have told."

Nell wrote it down at the table, in the lane, in the cold, with nine people watching a woman of twenty write down something a foreman's wife had said to her, and when she had finished she read it back and Roke said it was right except for one word, and named the word, and Nell changed it.

Then she put the chalk in her pocket and went up the road to the assessor's room, because there was the other thing and she would rather have it in the same day.

---

The assessor from the next province had decided, and had not told anybody in Greyfen.

Her association is a Weavers' and Small Dyemasters' Association with eleven houses. It is not the guild. It has its own assessor, its own book, and its own view of what a second instrument does to a house, and the view it has taken is in writing, and it is dated the twenty-second of the second month, and it says that where a house in the association has entered a second instrument since the sixth year, the first instrument is read with it, and if the second instrument does not name a person, the exemption is not held.

It is the fifth line on the back of the exemption form. It is the same fifth line Marl Crenn read out loud in January in a room with a foreman in it and then made a woman from another province read out herself.

What it means in the next province is that eleven houses are being told by their own association that a clause about girls who leave is going to take their certification, and that it will do it in the fourth month, and that no form has to be broken and no clerk has to be unkind to anybody.

Marl Crenn had the sheet in her hand on the seventh of the third month, and she had been holding it for nine days, and she had not sent it up the road.

"I am not your enemy and I am not a good news service," said Crenn. "Read it."

Nell read it.

"You have had it nine days."

"I have had it nine days because she wrote to me before she wrote to her own houses, and she did that because of what happened in this room in January, and I have sat on it for nine days because every day I sit on it is a day eleven houses are not being told a thing that is going to take their certification, and every day I sit on it is a day you have not heard it and cannot do anything about it either."

"You have been protecting them."

"I have been protecting Greyfen." Crenn put the sheet down on the desk. "There is a difference and it is a mean one and I have not slept properly in nine days. If I put that sheet on your lane's table on the seventh of the third month, then on the eighth the assessment has a question in front of it about a form four houses have already read, and every house on this lane hears about the next province the day before I strike the quarter, and I lose the levy argument — and the levy argument is the one that matters to the four houses that signed, and the reason it matters is that it is the only one anybody can win."

"Then say what you want said."

"I want it said in the room on the assessment, in the fourth month, by me, in my own hand, before I strike anything, and I want the woman from the next province in the room when I say it, and I want her to read it out herself as she read the fifth line out in January." Crenn put the sheet down flat. "And I want you to put on your own board, in chalk, tonight, that this trade has now been told twice, by two different offices, that the fifth line on the back of the exemption form means the end of an exemption, and that neither office will give one single person a name to ask."

Nell Kest wrote it on the board that night, in four lines, under the four names and under her own apology, and it is the only thing on that board that is not somebody's own name, and it is still there.

---

What she wrote about afterwards, in her own book, in a page nobody has ever read, is that the four questions on that board are no answer at all to being the one the number is about.

The four questions are for a person who has been handed a paper. They are for a person who is going to be asked to sign something, or who has signed something and does not know what it did. There is no version of them that helps the person who has put the number there, and she had spent four months learning how to be the person somebody came to, and on the seventh of the third month she discovered that there is a second job and it has no questions in it at all, and that the second job is the one where you are the reason for the number.

On the seventh of the third month, at about the seventh hour, the lane's table got a letter.

It was not brought by a boy and it was not in the post. It came in the hand of a woman who had come up from the lower road in a cart with nobody in it, who asked at the table whether a letter was read there, and then handed over a square envelope with a furnace-town postmark four hundred and forty miles away on it.

It was not sealed. It was empty. And on the outside of it, where a person writes the name of the person they are writing to, somebody had written in a good clerkly hand:

*B. Rahl, Assessor, over the weigh-house office, Sillrow, Emberwatch. By the hand of a house on the lane. To be returned.*

Which is not a thing anybody writes on an envelope.

It is a thing a person writes on the back of a letter they have taken out and read, and are putting back into the post, so that the next postman will see the name on it and take it to the right street.

Nell Kest turned it over twice. There was nothing on the back.
