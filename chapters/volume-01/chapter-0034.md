# Chapter 34: In the First Person

Ivet Sarn had run a hearing window for eight weeks and had never once written down what it had cost, and on the twenty-first day of the third month she looked at the board and worked out that she had nine days to put it on there in her own hand, and that the reason she had not already done it was that doing it would mean admitting something she had decided on the twenty-ninth of the eleventh and had not told anybody about, including the magistrate.

It went on the board on the twenty-sixth of the third month, between the two registers, in the same hand as both of them, at the same height as the rule about the window stopping, with the day on it.

It is not a report. A report is asked for. This is a wall, and it is three feet high and four feet wide, and there are two registers on it and a cost and a reason, and anybody who comes to the window on a day that is not a hearing day reads all of it on the way past without being asked to.

She wrote it in the first person. That was the hard part, and she did it on the twenty-fourth, in the copying room, at night, over three attempts, and threw the first two in the stove the way a younger person throws things in a stove.

The first attempt began *The trial was conducted at this window between the first of the twelfth month and the twelfth of the first* and it was, in her opinion, the voice of a woman putting a distance between herself and a thing she had done.

The second attempt began *We held thirty-four hearings* and it was worse, because *we* in a court clerk's minute book means the office and she was the office and there was nobody else in it.

The third attempt is what is on the wall.

> *THE TRIAL. WHAT IT WAS, WHAT IT COST, AND WHY IT RAN WHEN IT RAN.*
>
> *This court agreed on the twenty-ninth of the eleventh month to try a named process for six weeks on the flood-season continuity filings of this district. It ran from the first of the twelfth month to the twelfth of the first month. I ran it at this window. There was no magistrate in the room at any hearing and there was no magistrate in the room now.*
>
> *The process has four boxes. The first is the hearing. The second is the witness, who is not the applicant and is not owed anything in the matter, and who is told in advance in writing what they have agreed to, and may say no. The third is the cost. The fourth is the next review, and the next review is the fourth of the fourth month.*
>
> *Thirty-four hearings. Three hours each. A hundred and two hours of a window that is open six hours a day.*
>
> *Four of the thirty-four people said the words "I do not understand this" and were written down as having said them, which is a complete and acceptable answer and is not a refusal. I had told the roll-keeper in the eleventh month that every person would say it because it is the polite answer and a lie, and I was wrong about the number and right about the shape of it. The polite answer is given about four times in a hundred, and when it is given it is given by somebody who has been in a room too long and cannot say that a form is in a foreign shape.*
>
> *Of the thirty who said they understood, eleven signed something else within a month of the hearing. Both facts are in the same box, in the order they happened, and anybody who reads the file after me will have both of them. That is what the box is for. It was not for stopping anybody.*
>
> *Eleven shillings was the sum stated before the window opened. Nine shillings and fourpence has been spent, which is the paper and the second chair and the oil. The remainder is not returned to the works fund; it is not mine to return and it will be carried to the next quarter and shown in the same place.*
>
> *THE REASON IT RAN WHEN IT RAN, AND NOBODY ASKED ME FOR THIS LINE.*
> *The certification window for this district is the forty-two days from the twenty-fifth of the second month to the sixth of the fourth month, and the last relief roll must be certified on the sixth because the rate is struck on the sixth, and there are four hundred and sixty rolls. A hearing is three hours of that window. Fifty-two of them is a hundred and fifty-six hours, which is twenty-six days, which leaves sixteen days, which is a hundred and seventy-six rolls certified against four hundred and sixty needed, and two hundred and eighty-four households off the rate late in the worst month in nine years.*
> *That is a price and it is a good price and nobody should pay it before somebody has watched whether the thing works. I asked the roll-keeper on the seventeenth of the eleventh whether the trial could be run outside the window. I asked her the question and she gave me the answer, and I did not ask it because I had worked it out and I wanted it to be put in somebody else's mouth so that it would be in the file in their hand and not in mine.*
> *So the trial ran with my window shut. That was the decision. It was mine and I did not tell the magistrate, and I have written it here on a public wall in the building, with the day on it, because the arithmetic in the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth month quotes that price and a price that has not been explained is a claim, and a claim is a thing a person can stop believing.*
>
> *Nobody has been late because of this. No household in this district has waited for a seed parcel because of the six weeks on the wall above your head. That is not because the process is cheap. It is because the process was run in a month when it cost nothing, which was arranged, and the arranging is written above.*

---

The lamp-trimmer came at the ninth hour on the twenty-seventh, which was late for him and which Sarn noticed.

Wend had been in the copying room in Auremar on the ninth of the ninth month from the first hour, next to a boy with a tray, and his name had been in open court since the twelfth of the ninth, and nobody had ever asked him anything at all.

"You have been trimming the lamps in this room for how long."

"Nine years, and the stair before that." Wend took his time. "What do you want to know."

"I want to know which nights this room had its lamps lit past the seventh hour since the first of the twelfth month, and why, and whether the door was shut."

Wend put the tray down on the bench, which nobody was supposed to do.

"Twenty-two nights," he said. "I have counted them because I counted them in January when I asked the lampwoman and she said you do not count them, and I counted them. Twenty-two nights the front lamps were lit past the seventh hour and I trimmed them at the eighth and again at the tenth, and the front door was shut on all twenty-two."

"All twenty-two."

"All twenty-two, and I have been in this building nine years and I will tell you what I know about a door and a working light, which is that a door is shut because a room is being used for a thing and not because a clerk is cross." He picked the tray up again. "And the reason I counted them is that the roll-keeper asked me the same question in January and did not want my answer, she wanted a number, and a man does not forget being asked a thing he was not supposed to answer."

"I did want your answer."

"I know you did. I answered it in January and you did not write it down, and I have thought about that for two months and I have decided that a clerk who asks a question and does not write the answer down is a clerk who was checking whether I would give one twice."

Sarn wrote it down, all of it, with the hour on it, and read it back, and then did a thing she had not done for a person at that window in nine years, which was to write a question down first, in the register, with a line number, before the answer existed.

*Asked of Wend, lamp-trimmer, on the twenty-seventh of the third month, at the ninth hour: whether the front door of the copying room was shut on the twenty-two nights in the six weeks of the trial on which the lamps were lit past the seventh hour, and why.*

*Answered: all twenty-two, and a door is shut because a room is being used for a thing.*

---

The twenty-two nights were not spread evenly and Sarn had not known that until the twenty-seventh, and it is the only thing Wend told her that she had not already worked out for herself.

Of the thirty-four hearings, twenty-nine were inside the six-hour window. Three of the rest were at the seventh hour because a woman had walked down from the flats and arrived after the door and had been let in and had been done late. Two of the rest were at the ninth hour at night, and both of those were the same person, and her name was on the slate, and Sarn had written *heard at the ninth hour* in the register, and the witness slip for it had been filled in by Wend because there was nobody else in the building.

She works the channel from the sixth hour to the sixth. She came on the nineteenth of the first month and she said, before she sat down, that she had never been in a court in her life and did not want to be there and had come because there was nobody else she could ask and the hearing was the only thing that had ever offered to be a day.

"Does the witness order say we can come at night," she said.

"It says nothing about the hour."

"Then somebody should write on it what the hour is." She looked at the four boxes. "I have been told my whole life that I can ask for a hearing. I have never once been told that I can ask for one at a time I am not at work."

---

Magistrate Leda Rell came down to the window on the twenty-ninth of the third month, at the fourth hour, and read the wall from the bottom up, which is not the way anybody reads a wall, and stood in front of it for about six minutes with her hands behind her back.

"You have written that the decision to run it outside the window was yours."

"It was."

"You asked a copyist."

"I asked a copyist and I wrote down the answer I had already worked out so that it would be in her hand and not mine. That is on the wall as well, in the same paragraph, and I have not softened it since you began reading."

Rell read that paragraph again, which took her a while.

"I have a flood docket that closes on the sixth of the fourth month," she said, "and a rate struck the same day, and a district that is nineteen sacks short of what its own parcels need, and there is a sentence of mine in nine hundred and forty printed copies in a lane four hundred miles from here. And I have not asked you a question about any of it since the ninth of the eleventh month, and you have not asked me for one, and I have been waiting to see which of us would go first, and you have gone first, on a wall, without a single one of them being in the room."

"I would have told you."

"You would have told me on the seventh of the fourth month, in answer to a question, as part of an accounting, in a room with a rate in it." Rell took her hands from behind her back. "That is not a fault. It is the correct order of operations, and if you had told me in November I would have said no, because in November I had a docket and no evidence and a good instinct and nothing else. You gave me the evidence first and the sentence afterwards, and that is a thing I could not have done at twenty-nine and did not think of."

"Will you read the wall to the district."

"I will read it aloud in this room on the fourth, in my own words, and I will not improve it, and if anybody asks me whether the court thinks the process works I am going to answer the question they asked rather than the question they wanted." She looked at the third paragraph. "Thirty-four. Four that did not understand. Eleven that signed something after they did understand and had a month to think about it. I am going to have to say those eleven out loud in a room, because they are the worst number on that wall and it is the one nobody would have printed."

"It is the one the box is for."

"It is," said Leda Rell. "Write my name at the bottom of it. If I am going to say it out loud in a room then it is a finding of this court and not a clerk's private arithmetic, and if it is a clerk's private arithmetic then in four years somebody takes it off the wall and says the trial was never anybody's business."

Sarn wrote *Leda Rell, magistrate, this court* at the foot of the third paragraph on the twenty-ninth of the third month in her own small hand, and initialled it, and did not tell anybody that she had done it.

---

Two other things went on the wall before the end of the month, and one of them was a rule.

The rule was that **the review on the fourth of the fourth month is not a hearing of any application.** Not the Venn, not the Dunn, not anybody's. The court had nine filings on the docket for the day and three of them were real business, and if a person came to the room on the fourth with a paper about their own flood filing then she would be turned away at the door, because the one thing a review must not be is another hearing, or it becomes a hearing that somebody can win and then the next one is a hearing that somebody can win, and there is no end to that and the date was supposed to be the end of it.

And anybody who wanted to say something about the process itself — not about their filing, about the process — had to say it in the room, out loud, on the fourth, in front of other people, and could not send it in.

Sarn wrote that on the wall in the same hand, and then, on the twenty-eighth, she found out what it cost to mean it, because a man came from a factor's office in the last week of the third month with a piece of paper and asked whether a hearing could be added to the fourth.

"You cannot," said Sarn. "It is a review, not a hearing."

"I have come from the top of the row."

"Sir, the fourth of the fourth is on the wall, in my hand, with the day on it, and you may read it on your way in, and if you have a paper about your own flood filing it will be heard on its own day or it will not be heard at all, and I would rather say that to your face than put it in a book." She gave him a copy of the wall. "That is a shilling. Nobody is charged for reading the wall."

He bought two copies, and it cost him two shillings to learn nothing, and Sarn wrote the two shillings in the day's column because that is what the column was for, and they were the only income the trial had ever had.

---

The third thing on the wall was five lines long and it went four hundred and forty miles.

*You have been named to witness a hearing at this window. You are not the applicant and you are not owed anything in the matter. You will be told what the hearing is about before it happens. You may say no. You are not held to your answer for saying no. The court cannot require you to come, because you do not live in this district, and it will not write to you again unless you ask it to.*

She addressed it, through Bettin Rahl of the Emberwatch Dyers' Combination, to a woman of that trade whose name she had on a list in a book under a winter coat and had not spoken to, and the reason she went through Rahl and not direct was written on the envelope in her own hand, and it was that a house in that trade may not write to a court about an instrument without the assessor's leave in writing first, and it is thirty-one years old, and it is at the second leaf of the Combination's book, and it is the reason four people in that town have held four copies of a court ruling since the twentieth of the ninth month and have never asked anybody a question.

She paid the postage herself, a shilling and fourpence out of the money in her own drawer, because she asked the district works fund for it and was refused, and the refusal was correct: the fund buys the running of the window and the paper and the oil and nothing else, and Sarn had said so herself on the wall in November.

It came back on the second day of the fourth month, and it came back signed, and it is the first witness order that has ever gone out of this district and come back signed, and it is now on the wall next to the cost.

Bettin Rahl wrote three lines across the top of it in a good clerkly hand, and the third line is the one Sarn copied out and put on the wall in her own writing, because it is the sentence she wants anybody who reads that wall to have:

*You asked what it will cost them. It will cost them one thing, which is that a person they have never met has written their name down in a book, and it cannot be taken out again, and I have told them that, and they have signed anyway, and I would like it noticed that I did not ask them to be brave.*

Sarn looked at the four lines of her own and the three lines of his and the wall with about nine feet of writing on it, and found that she had stopped being a woman who records other people's decisions somewhere between the second of the fourth month and the twenty-seventh of the third, and that she had not missed the moment, and that nobody was going to hand it to her.
