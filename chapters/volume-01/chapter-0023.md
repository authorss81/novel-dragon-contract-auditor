# Chapter 23: Four Boxes

The list came up the river on the twenty-sixth of the eleventh month with the afternoon post and sat on Ivet Sarn's window sill under a stone for two days, because Tamsin Rook had not wanted to arrive with it, and a woman who has written something and does not want to be watched while she gives it away will find a reason to be two days late.

She came on the twenty-eighth, at the eighth hour, with her bag, and she had drawn a form on the packet boat.

"You have drawn a form," said Sarn, before she had said good morning.

"I have drawn a form and I want you to tell me it is wrong, and I want you to do it in front of the person it is for."

"Who is it for."

"Magistrate Rell."

---

Rell gave them nine minutes in the anteroom with the door open and a clerk writing in the outer room, and she read the sheet standing up, and she read it twice, which was more than Marek had expected and less than Tamsin had wanted.

"This is yours," Rell said, at the end of the second reading.

"It is a list of what the process has to contain," said Tamsin. "It is not a process. He wrote down what must be in it and he has sent it to me and to the water office and to the court, and the court's is the copy that matters and I am carrying it up the river myself."

"You are carrying it up the river yourself," said Rell, "which means that in a week there will be a letter in a file somewhere saying the roll-keeper brought it, and in five years a person will say the roll-keeper thought of it. You understand that."

"Yes, magistrate."

"I am going to say three things and then I am going to leave, because I have a flood docket in the fourth month and so do you and so does the man who wrote this, and none of us has the time." Rell turned the sheet over and held it up. "One. The court will try it, and the court's own act will carry it, and it is not his and it is not yours, and when somebody in a different province asks in a year who invented this, the answer is that a district court in Mosswake tried a thing for six weeks and wrote down whether it worked. That answer is worth more than the truth."

Tamsin wrote that down.

"Two. I will not put the court's name on it until the fourth month's docket is finished, because the moment my name is on it every clerk in the district will treat it as a rule and start applying it to guild instruments and barn terms and marriage settlements, and I have three lawyers writing to me a week about matters that have nothing whatever to do with a flood season and I cannot afford a fourth one."

"And three."

"Three is that I am not going to be in the room for most of it," said Rell, "and if you build a thing that needs a magistrate in it, it dies in the fourth month, and it will die quietly and nobody will be able to say why. So build it for a clerk on a bad morning with a full bench behind her, or do not build it."

She put the sheet down on the table.

"You have until the sixth of the fourth month and after that it is your window and not mine, and I am going to want to know on the seventh what it cost."

---

Sarn would not look at the form for eleven minutes.

Tamsin put it on the sill, flat, with the four boxes uppermost, and then stood at the window and did not say anything, which is a thing she has never been good at and which cost her more than the eleven minutes did.

"You have been in my building three days," Sarn said at last, "before this. You arrived on the thirty-eighth —" she stopped, and did it herself. "On the thirtieth of the tenth you came off the boat and told me at this window that you were not looking for your own file, and you wanted your letter put in the record with your name on it, and I wrote to nobody and asked no questions and let a woman stand on my step for a quarter of an hour."

"That is right."

"And I have thought about it every day since, and here is what I have thought about it." Sarn put her pen down, which is what she did when she was going to say the thing she had prepared. "That letter of yours had a provincial file stapled inside it that I had never seen, from a court four hundred miles away, put into a matter in this room by a copyist of twenty-four who was not the applicant and had no business here. And I wrote it into my book. And two days later two documents were admitted on my authority, and a magistrate has never formally been told, and I have a piece of paper on her bench about it that I have looked at four times this week and thought: this is the second thing I have started in a month that turned into a different thing."

"You are saying I put a province into your court."

"I am saying you put a province into my court and I let you, and I did it because you were right about the copy that goes out and cannot be recalled, and because I would rather be the clerk who copied it out than the magistrate who read about it." Sarn turned the form round so that the four boxes were toward herself. "And now you have come up the river with a form and I am supposed to start a second register, in the same month, in the same building, in the fourth month, with four hundred and sixty relief rolls to certify and a window that is open six hours and a bench behind it that is a bench for certifying things."

"Six weeks," Tamsin said.

"Six weeks," said Sarn, "and if I open a file that is not the relief rolls I am a clerk who has chosen, and there are two things a clerk can be in a flood season. You can be the clerk who certifies, or you can be the clerk who looks into things, and I have been the first one for nine years and I have got a district that ate four months late in the fifth year on my conscience and I would like to keep being the first one for the rest of this one."

---

So Tamsin did the thing she had come up the river to do, which was to stop defending the form and start taking it apart.

"You are right about all of it," she said. "So let us count it. What does one hearing cost?"

"Three hours. You have it from the water office."

"Three hours. And how many in the district."

"Fifty-two, if the count is right, and it is the count as it was made, which is a count of things in a column, and I do not know what is in the column."

"Fifty-two times three is a hundred and fifty-six hours. Your window is six hours a day. That is twenty-six days, Sarn. And you have forty-two days from the eighteenth of the second month to the sixth of the fourth, and your window is the only window there is, and there are four hundred and sixty rolls on the other side of it, and your own rate is eleven a day when you are certifying and nothing else, and that is four hundred and sixty-two. My six weeks are a trial inside those forty-two days and not the whole of them, and I am going to lose twenty-six days of your window doing it."

"Nine."

"Two hundred and eighty-four," said Tamsin. "Sixteen days of that window at eleven a day is a hundred and seventy-six certified, and the district needs four hundred and sixty, and the difference is two hundred and eighty-four, in a district nineteen sacks short of what its own parcels need. And the man who worked it out has had the list of which two hundred and eighty-four households in his head since the eighth month and has never shown it to anybody, and I have now said a number out loud in a window that has a household attached to it, and it has stopped being an abstraction."

Sarn looked up.

"You have done the arithmetic on my window."

"Somebody had to. A man in a wet district did it on his own board and put his house on it and I am not going to pretend to a clerk of this court that I worked it out first."

"So two hundred and eighty-four."

"Two hundred and eighty-four. And that is not the worst of it, because the worst of it is this: the process is only safe while somebody is standing at that window. That is the entire finding of this month and it took me six weeks and a floor in a corridor to learn it. If the window is shut for the middle of the day, and it is, and a woman walks down from the flats with a paper in her hand and gets told to come back on Friday, then the ask has not failed, it has evaporated, and she will not come back on Friday because nobody has written to her and nobody has to."

Sarn had picked up the form. She was holding it the way she held a thing she had decided about.

"Then what is box one," she said.

---

Box one is the hearing.

Tamsin had written it as a single line and Sarn made her write out the whole of it, standing at the sill, in a fair hand, and the whole of it took a quarter of an hour and they argued about four of the words.

*Box one, the hearing. What the person says they have been told. What is actually said to them, read aloud. What the person says back, in the person's own words, and the words "I do not understand this" are a complete and acceptable answer and are to be written down as the answer and not as a refusal. What was agreed, if anything. The hour. Whether the person was heard themselves or whether somebody was heard for them.*

"They will all say they do not understand it," said Sarn. "Every one of them. It is the polite answer and it is a lie and it will be in my book in their handwriting in about four months' time as a thing they said."

"Then it goes in as the answer," said Tamsin, "and the person who reads it out has to watch the face, and if a person says they do not understand it and then signs something, the two facts sit next to each other in the same box for ever, and the person who comes after has both of them. That is the whole point. Not to stop them. To leave the two things in one box where a person can see them."

Sarn wrote *not to be treated as a refusal* in the margin in her own small hand, and then, after a moment, crossed out *not* and wrote nothing, because she had worked out halfway through that it had to be a complete answer and not the denial of one.

Box two is the witness, and box two took twenty minutes and three attempts and the discovery that the person at the window cannot be the witness because the person at the window is the court and the court is not a person.

"It cannot be the applicant," said Sarn. "It cannot be somebody who is owed money in the same matter, or who is owed it in any matter against the same person, or it is a witness somebody can be frightened of. And in a flood district that leaves you the man in the lane with the chalk and the woman who does not give her name, and a foreman whose seat is about to be looked at, and about a third of the district who are not in the district."

"So we ask."

"We ask, and about half the time the person they name is somebody who wants something."

Tamsin wrote it out.

*Box two, the witness. The name of a person who is not the applicant, and who is not owed anything in the matter, and who has agreed to it. If the household cannot name one, the court names one from the list of people who have agreed to witness, and the person named is told in advance what they have agreed to, in writing, and may say no, and the saying no is not held against them.*

"A list," said Sarn.

"A list of people who have said, in advance, in cold blood, that they will witness a hearing at this window if they are asked."

"Who keeps it."

"You do. It is four names now. It will be eleven by the fourth month. In a year, if it works, somebody else will keep it, and I would like it to be a person who is not in this room."

Sarn made her write the witness order out as a separate slip, five lines long, because a form with a box is a thing people bring and a slip is a thing a person can be handed in a lane, and she would not say why she wanted it and Tamsin did not ask.

Box three is the cost, and box three is where Tamsin Rook lost an argument she had not known she was in.

*Box three, the cost. The sum stated before the window opens. What has been spent of it. The day the sum is spent.*

"The sum is eleven shillings," said Tamsin, "for six weeks. Stove, candle, oil, a second chair, and the paper for these forms, and the office at Reed Cut has agreed to find it, and it is the whole of the cash cost and it is a third of what the process really costs, which is you."

"And the hours?"

"The hours are not in the box. The hours are the cost and there is no money in them and there is no way to write them, and that is the thing I cannot get past."

Sarn put the pen down and looked at her for about four seconds.

"No," she said, "you cannot, and I am going to tell you what happens in a district when a thing costs money it cannot pay in money. It stops, and it stops quietly, and it stops on a Friday afternoon in the fifth week with a slate on a nail and fourteen entries on it, and there is no person to complain to, because the form does not say anybody is obliged to run it."

"Write that in box three."

"I am not writing that in a form that goes to a household," said Sarn. "A household is frightened enough. I am writing it on the wall of this room, and I am writing it in the slate, and when your sum is spent my book is going to have a line in it with the day on it, and the line is going to say the window stopped on that day and what it had cost and what it had done, and it is going to stay in the book after whoever is next."

Box four is the next review, and box four was the easiest and the only one of the four that nobody argued about, because it was a date, and everybody in the building already had one.

*The fourth of the fourth month.*

---

They put both registers on the same slate.

That was the last thing and it took the least arguing and it was the one Tamsin went away thinking about for a week, because Sarn had refused it three times and then asked for it herself.

"You want me to hang a hearing list next to a duty list," Sarn said, "on the same board, where a creditor comes to read one of them and has to look at the other."

"Yes."

"That is a hideous idea."

"Yes."

"A register of duties tells you what is owed. A register of hearings tells you who came." Sarn got up off the stool and went to the board on the side of the window, the one with the day's attendance on it in a hand that had been going thirty years. "That is why it is worth the wall. Everybody who comes to this window in the next six weeks is going to read that board on the way past, on their way in, and a person who has been in a room about a duty is a different kind of fact from a person whose name is on a list of debtors, and the second one is what this window has been for nine years."

She wrote the two headings at the top of the board in her own hand, on the twenty-ninth of the eleventh month, and put the dates under them, and the register of hearings was empty for two more days.

---

Pella Osk wrote the forms out.

There were two hundred and forty of them by the end of the second week of the twelfth month, because Sarn would not print them and would not have a series and would not have a number, and Pella Osk is the fastest copyist in the Mosswake building and had been asleep over the relief rolls for most of the ninth of the ninth month in a chair by the stove.

Tamsin asked her to do it, and then asked her the other thing, standing at the copying frame, in the way of a person who has decided to ask a question out loud because it is easier to be refused than to have not asked.

"You were named in open court," Tamsin said, "on the twelfth of the ninth. A clerk put your name to a thing — that you would say what the copying room was like on the ninth. I have been in this province twice since and nobody has asked you anything, and I have been in this building nine weeks and I did not know that until three weeks ago, and I want to ask you now and I want the answer in the record, if you want it in the record."

"Ask me."

"What was the copying room like on the ninth."

Pella Osk thought about it for a moment, and then said: "It was like a copying room. That is the whole of it and it is more than people expect. It was hot and it smelled of the sealing wax off the other bench, and Wend had his lamp in early because the corridor is cold, and I was asleep on the relief rolls from about the second hour and I woke up at the fourth with a seal on my hand where I had put my palm flat on one that was not dry, and I put it in my apron and washed it off in the yard because I did not want to explain the mark.

"Nobody came in between the second hour and the fourth. Nobody. I have thought about it since because somebody named me in a room and I did not know anybody had, and I have gone over the morning the way you go over a morning, and the only thing I can tell anybody is that there was nobody in that room, and the only thing that moved in it was a lamp and a boy with a tray."

Tamsin wrote it down and read it back and did not add anything to it.

"You have been owed that for nine weeks," she said. "It is in the record now. Whatever else happens in this building, that is in it."

---

The first person to use the form came on the first of the twelfth month, at about the eleventh hour, and she was not on anybody's list, and Sarn had never seen her before, and she had been to this window once, in the ninth month, to pay something.

She had a paper in her hand of the kind Tamsin had been copying for three days out of a corridor rack behind a gallery, and it had a series at the top and a run at the foot.

Sarn told her, before she had the form filled in, in the ordinary way, in the voice of a clerk giving out a fact: "I want to say this to you now and I am saying it in the correct words. If somebody has told you that you do not have to come to a court about a paper like that one, they have read you something they were not entitled to leave out the last line of. Come in. Sit on the bench. I will write down what you came about and somebody who is not you will hear it and write down what they heard, and you will get a copy of it before you leave, and it will cost a shilling."

The woman looked at the paper in her hand, and then at the board on the side of the window, where two headings had been written on the twenty-ninth of the eleventh month and one of them was empty.

"What do I have to do," she said.
