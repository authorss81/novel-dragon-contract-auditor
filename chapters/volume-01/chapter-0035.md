# Chapter 35: A Register Read Aloud

Iselle Oster had lost a cooper's gauge on the fourth of the twelfth month and had been owed something since the ninth of the first, and on the sixteenth of the third month she got a letter from a copyist saying that she was coming up the water again, on her own money, and that it was not to help.

*You asked me for everything I had and I am going to send all of it, because all of it is the point. And I am coming myself, because on the twelfth of the first month you said in your own shop that a person with no document has nothing to show anybody, and I have been carrying that sentence for two months, and I have worked out that you are wrong about half of it, and I would rather say the half that you have got wrong in your hearing than in a letter.*

It took three days on the water and a night's sleep in a coach and the cost of a fortnight off her wages, and Iselle Oster met her at the landing on the nineteenth of the third month and said, before she had got her bag down, "Say that again in a room, because there is a man from the top of the row in my shop at the fourth hour and I want him to have heard you think it."

---

Sabra Ollett was asked the question on the twentieth, in the landing office, in front of the wheelwright from the yard at the far end of the row and a woman called Tace Oster, sixteen, four days out of a school term, who had asked to be there and had not been told she could.

The question was not the one anybody had asked her in two months.

"What *is* the stamp," said Iselle Oster.

Sabra Ollett looked at it for a while.

"It is this office's stamp," she said. "It is not a court's and it is not a guild's and it is not anybody's but mine, and I put it in the top right corner of a sheet that a man brought in himself, filled in, in a hand that was not a clerk's, and I put it there because a district landing office stamps what is put in front of it, and I have stamped about four hundred of those in nine years, and I stamped yours in about four seconds, and I have thought about those four seconds every day since the ninth of the first month."

"You cannot produce a number for it."

"There is no number for it. The model says the filing is entered in the routine column and not itemised, and if it is not itemised it does not get a number, and if it does not get a number then it cannot be found by anybody who has not got the book in front of them. I have got the book. I found it on the nineteenth of the first month and I read it out to this woman in about a minute." Sabra turned the book round and put her finger on the ninth line of the routine column for the tenth month. It says: *guarantee, shop goods, bond, and the name of a district office.* "That is all it says. There is not one word on that line about a woman or a girl or a shop."

"And you stamped it in good faith."

"I stamped it in good faith and I would stamp it again on Thursday." She put her hand flat on the cover. "That is the part nobody wants. I have been waiting since the nineteenth of the first month for somebody to ask me a question where the answer is that somebody in this building is at fault, and it has not come, and I would like to say why, and I would like it written down."

Nobody wrote it down. Iselle Oster did, in her own book, in her own shop, afterwards, in her own words, and she put a date on it.

---

She wrote this on the twenty-first of the third month and she wrote it for a copyist and not for a court, and the copyist read it in the shop on the bench on the second of the fourth month and did not improve one word of it.

*NOBODY DID ANYTHING WRONG. I want that written down by somebody, in a place where it can be read, because in about a year the only thing anybody will remember about this is that a gauge went out of a shop in Millbrook Reach, and the way a thing is remembered is what it is going to be used for.*

*There is no bad man in this. The man from the top of the row thinks he holds a bond and he holds a bond, and I have never once seen him lie to me. Sabra stamped a sheet a man brought her, in four seconds, because that is what a landing office does with what is put in front of it. The district prints a model. The district sends it out. The district told four hundred offices to put these in a column and not to give them a number, and the reason it told them that is not a trick. It is so that a form can be counted by a person four hundred miles away who has never seen a cooper's shop. It is a good system. It is the best system anybody in this town has ever had for knowing what has gone in and out of a landing.*

*And it has taken a gauge off my bench, and put my daughter's name on a line she did not sign, and put my son's trade out of reach, and it has done all three without one single person in the chain breaking a single rule, and every one of them would have been within their rights to do exactly what they did, and two of them would have been within their rights to do it again.*

*I am not a person who is owed something. I am a person who has no paper, and there is a difference, and the difference is the whole of what has happened to me since the fourth of the twelfth month.*

*Everywhere else I have taken this, somebody has wanted to know what I was owed. Nobody has wanted to know what the stamp was.*

---

The fourth month is not a deadline anybody chose. It is the month the hoop work comes back in, and a cooper's shop in a landing town that cannot fit a hoop to the trade standard cannot bid for the season's work, and cannot bid for the season's work in the fourth month cannot be examined for the trade in the fifth, and cannot be examined in the fifth cannot have a journeyman in the sixth.

Iselle Oster had known that since the ninth of the first month and had not written it down, because a person who writes down a date in January has a date in January.

The other three shops in Millbrook Reach had a gauge between them. One of them belonged to a man of fifty-eight who had a grandson on a term and who had, in the fourth week of the second month, offered — through the wheelwright, not through himself — to lend the Oster shop his own gauge for the season, and the offer had not been taken, because the fourth question has an answer about people who get paid, and the wheelwright had taken no money and had not said whether the fifty-eight-year-old had told him to make the offer.

"You could have taken it," said Tamsin Rook.

"I could have taken it and I did not take it and I would like it recorded that I did not take it." Iselle Oster put the copyist's book down on the bench. "If I take a gauge off a man of fifty-eight for a season, then in the fifth month there is a shop in Millbrook Reach with a gauge and in the sixth month a grandson on a term there is a journeyman, and in about four years there is a shop in Millbrook Reach with no gauge in it at all and it is my shop, because the other three will have gone the same way, one after the other, the way they all do."

"That is not a reason. That is a prediction."

"That is a reason I understand," said Iselle Oster, "and I am not going to be talked out of it by a woman who is here for three days, and if the two of us write it down in this book I would like it written that I said no to it before the book was open."

Tamsin Rook wrote it down, with the date on it, and the date was the twenty-third of the third month, and there are now two pages in that book about a gauge and not one of them is about a debt.

---

The other half of it came out of the bundle on the twenty-second, in the shop, with the fire in and Dain Oster doing a hoop at the far bench without turning round.

The returned envelope went first. It was square and empty and it had a furnace-town postmark on it, and on the outside, in a good clerkly hand, a name: *B. Rahl, Assessor, over the weigh-house office, Sillrow, Emberwatch. By the hand of a house on the lane. To be returned.*

Tamsin Rook read that out twice and then put down the four lines the assessor had sent with it, and read those out too, and the fourth one of the four was the sentence about a house in a trade of dyers not being permitted to write to a court, a guild, a factor's office, a district office, or a person it had not dealt with before, about an instrument, without the assessor's leave in writing first.

Nobody in the shop said anything for a while.

"That is not fear," said Tamsin.

"No," said Iselle Oster. "It is a rule they wrote themselves thirty-one years ago because three disputes in a year took half their work off them, and it worked, and they have had no dispute in forty years, and it is the reason four women in that town have been sitting on four copies of a court ruling since the twentieth of the ninth month and have never once asked anybody anything." She put her hand on the flat of the bench. "And here is the part I have not been able to get past since the nineteenth of the first month, and it is the same thing, and it is not the same thing."

"Go on."

"Sabra's office obeyed a form it was given, and nobody read it. Emberwatch wrote a rule nobody asked for, and everybody obeys it. And both of them are the reason a person has nothing to show." She looked at the wheelwright's bond receipt, which was on the bench with the four questions copied onto the back of it in a bad hand. "And the third one is the four questions, and I have had them in this shop since the twelfth of the second month and I could not tell you this afternoon, if that man from the top of the row asked me where they came from, whether they came from a woman in a dye-house in a lane in Mosswake or off the top of a child's head in a seed barn in the same province."

"You could say the lane."

"I could say the lane and I could not say a name, and I would not know whether I was telling the truth, because the woman who gave the last of the four will not give her name to anybody and there are four women in those seed barns and it is not me." Iselle Oster put both hands flat on the bench. "I have spent two months being told that the difference between a form and a question is that anybody may ask a question who nobody. And it is true, and it is the whole of what the four questions are worth, and on the twenty-third of the third month I worked out that it is also the reason not one word of this can be put in front of a court, and that is not a fault in the questions. It is the price of them, and somebody ought to say out loud, once, that the price is real."

"It is real," said Tamsin Rook. "I have been the one paying it for four months. It has never once stopped a person being hit and it has never once been worth nothing, and those two things are both true, and there is no sentence that makes them comfortable."

---

The creditor's man came at the fourth hour on the twenty-fourth and he was the same one who had taken the gauge on the fourth of the twelfth, and he was polite about it, the way he had been before, and he had a paper in his hand that was not the paper.

Iselle Oster asked for the register to be read out in her shop and did not explain why, and the copyist read it out, because it said on the first page that anybody may read it, and she read it standing up, and she read the whole of it, including the part at the top that says it has no seal, no office, and no authority, and that nothing in it is evidence of anything.

It took about eleven minutes.

When she had finished, the creditor's man said, "That is not a document."

"No," said Iselle Oster.

"Then what is it."

"It is four questions somebody asked me in my own shop in the second month and four answers I gave, and a thing my daughter said twice, differently, and my name at the foot of it in my own hand, and the answer your office gave a woman about a stamp." She put the page down on the bench and turned it so that he could see the top of it. "A document is a thing that can be stamped, and produced, and filed, and put in front of somebody as proof. That cannot be stamped, because there is nobody to stamp it, and there is no office to file it in, and if you take it to a court today the court will look at the first line and give it back to you, and I have known that since the twelfth of the first month and I did not want to know it."

"You have had it eight weeks."

"I have had it eight weeks and I have not shown it to anybody, and the reason is that the first time I read it out loud in a room I was going to be in a room with a man who could take something off me." Iselle Oster stood up straighter than she had come in. "And you are the man, and you have heard it, and there is a woman in this town with a book that a factor's man has now heard about, and I would like that written down as well, because I chose the day and I chose the room and I am not going to pretend afterwards that it happened to me."

Dain Oster put a hoop down at the far bench and said, for the first time in three days, "You should have written to me before you wrote to him."

"I did write to you. On the eleventh. You have not answered it and I did not put it on the table because you are twenty-two and I am not spending you on a Thursday."

---

The girl asked to be asked on the twenty-eighth, in the shop, after the others had gone, and she asked for it herself, which was the first thing about the two months that had been hers.

"You keep asking me whether I knew," said Tace Oster. "The man on the shop step asked me if I knew. The man in the office asked me if I had read it. You asked me four times. Everybody asks me whether I knew."

"What do you want to be asked."

"I want to be asked whether the shop is worth my name, and I want it asked out loud with the man in it, and I want the answer to be no, and I want the answer to be written down that the answer is no, and then I want to be examined for the trade." She had her hands on the back of the bench. "Because that is the actual thing. I do not want a hearing. I want the gauge back, because with the gauge in this shop a person can be examined for the trade, and Dain cannot be examined and I cannot be examined and neither of us can be examined for a year or two or ever, and the trade in this town is four shops and it is going to be three."

"Dain, do you want—"

"I want the trade," said Dain Oster. "I have wanted the trade for a year and there is a bench and there is the work and the only thing that has been standing between me and being examined is a quarter of an inch of iron that a man carried out of my mother's shop in a cart in December."

"So the answer to your own question is yes," said Iselle Oster.

"Yes, and I would like it said in that order." Tace Oster looked at the floor. "Nobody has asked me that. I have been asked four times this winter whether I understood a form and not once whether I was a person who owed anything, and I have answered the first question every single time and it has been true every single time, and it has been about the wrong thing, and it has been the wrong thing because it is the one I can answer and I am sixteen."

Tamsin Rook wrote it down, on the page, in a copyist's book with nothing behind it, and read it back, and did not improve a word of it, and asked the fourth question a third time, in the shop, on the twenty-eighth of the third month, and got the same answer the second time she had given it, which was: *He was paid by the man who wanted the gauge, and I do not think he read it twice because I could not read and he could tell.*

And at the top of the page, under the line that says nothing in it is evidence, she wrote the date and the place and the name of the person who had asked to be asked, and the name of the man who had heard her ask.
