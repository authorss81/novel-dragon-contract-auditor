# Chapter 27: What the Form Does to a Stranger

The Nine Elms used its four weeks and signed on the twenty-ninth of the twelfth month, and it was the last of the four, and when it was done there were four houses on the dyers' lane that had signed the amended clause and four that had not, and the four that had not had been asked once, in the second week of the tenth month, by a man with a rack.

Nell Kest went round the second four herself in the last week of the month, not to get a signature, because a signature from a house that has not been threatened is worth less than one from a house that has, and she wanted to know what a form does to a house that was getting on perfectly well without it.

The first of the four was the Pry, and the Pry has been on that lane forty years and has never had a dispute with a man in a good coat, and the woman who kept the books there was extremely polite to Nell and offered her tea and then said the thing that turned the whole fortnight round.

"We have not signed," she said, "because we have not had to. We have not had a girl leave us owing anything since my husband's mother was alive. And now there is a form in four houses on this lane and the assessors are saying the levy goes on the lane, and if we do not sign we are on the levy and if we do sign we are on the levy, and either way we are on the levy, for a clause about girls who leave. What is it for?"

Nell did not have an answer that was any use, and she wrote one down anyway in her own book on the Friday, because a person who asks a question you cannot answer is owed a written record of the not-answering:

*The Pry has done nothing wrong in forty years and is being charged for a clause that protects a person it has never had. The levy is the price of having an amendment, not the price of the amendment. A trade that never had one has never had to pay one, and it is paying now, and the only way to stop paying is to sign, and that is a levy that buys a signature, and it is not a court and it is not a sentence and nobody is going to rule on it.*

---

Marl Crenn put the four houses in front of herself on the first of the first month, in her own room, with the rack of forms behind her and the second box in it, and she did it in eleven minutes because she is a woman who has done the same sum in her head for eleven years and had never once had anybody agree with her about it.

"Nine pounds a quarter," she said. "Four houses. That is the levy for the amendment, and it is the price of the amendment, and the amendment is the only thing standing between a girl who leaves a dye-house and the next woman who takes the bench. I have signed it. Mr Vare has signed it. My own seat is on the line of it, and I signed anyway, and I told you both at the time that I was signing because a form that has been read will be read again and I would rather it was read the second time with my name on it."

"I heard that," said Nell. "I have written it down."

"Then here is the other half, which I have never said out loud." Crenn turned the second box towards her and did not open it. "The exemption survives a second instrument in the house only if that instrument names a person. That is the fifth line on the back of the exemption form and it is the only line on the back of the exemption form that anybody ever reads. And the amendment is a second instrument. So the moment the amendment is in a house, the exemption is read with the amendment, for ever, in both directions. The amendment protects the house. The amendment also puts the house's thirty-one years of certification at the mercy of anybody's reading of the amendment, and there is going to be a second reading, and there is going to be a third, and none of them is going to be as careful as the one we have just had."

"You are saying the houses that signed are more exposed than the houses that did not."

"I am saying the houses that signed are exposed, and the houses that did not are exposed differently, and that is the whole of the trade-off and nobody in this trade has ever been told it exists." She shut the box. "And I am going to lose my seat over it, and I know that, and I have decided that a woman who is frightened of a form is a woman who has let a form do her thinking, and I would rather be out of a seat having read the second box out loud than in a seat that never opened it."

---

The woman from the next province came on the sixth of the first month, and Nell was in the room, and that was luck.

The Beckery had taken a copy of the amended clause to its own assessor in the middle of the eleventh, because the Beckery is a house that has belonged to an association since before the dyers' lane had a name, and its association is not the guild, and its assessor is a woman of about fifty whose name nobody in the room said twice. She had come in a cart with the copy in a leather case and she had come to find out whether an amendment in a house of this guild was good news or bad news for a house of her association, and she had a sheet of her own with a list on it, and the list was the names of eleven houses.

And when the assessor asked whether the amendment touched the exemption, Marl Crenn reached over and turned the exemption form round on the desk and put her finger on the back of it, fifth line from the top, and said, "That one. Read it out. Somebody else should read it out."

Nell Kest read it out, because she was standing nearest and because a person who reads a form aloud to a room is not a person who has taken a side, and because Crenn had looked at her when she said *somebody else*.

"Read it as it stands," said Crenn. "Not as you would say it at a table."

Nell read it as it stood.

*Where a second instrument is entered in a house at any time, the first instrument is read with it; and if the second instrument does not name a person, the exemption is not held.*

The room was quiet for about four seconds, and then the woman from the next province said, "That is the end of us."

"Yes," said Marl Crenn.

"Read with the first instrument *at any time*. Not at the time it is entered. At any time. So if one of my eleven houses takes in a boy on a term next year and the term is a second instrument, then the exemption is read with the boy's term, and if the boy's term does not name a person — and terms do not name people, terms name a year — then the exemption is not held."

"That is what it says."

"That is not what it says. That is what a frightened person reads it as, at about eleven o'clock at night, in a house with a boy in it." The woman from the next province had her hand flat on the copy. "And I have come sixty miles to be told a good thing and I have been told a thing with a fifth line on the back of it, and I have to write to eleven houses before Friday, and what am I writing."

Nell Kest wrote it down. That is all she did in that room. She wrote down what the woman said, in the words the woman used, and read it back, and then she read out the four questions off the back of a sheet she had in her coat, because she had them in her coat, because she has carried them in her coat since the sixth of the twelfth month.

"Does the paper name work? Whose name is on it? If you stop, whose name answers for it? And who read it to you, and do they get paid if you sign?"

The woman from the next province looked at the fourth one for longer than the other three.

"That one is worth a printing," she said. "Not the others. The others are good. That one is a tool, and if I put it in a letter to eleven houses it will be read by a foreman and it will be the only sentence in the letter that a foreman can do anything about."

"It is a question off a board in a lane in Mosswake," said Nell, "and the woman who gave it to me has never given her name to anybody and would not thank you for printing it."

"Then I will write it out in my own hand and put it at the top," said the woman from the next province, "and I will not say where it came from, and if anybody asks me I shall say a woman in a dye-house said it to me in a room with a foreman in it, which is true and which is nearly everything."

---

The levy took nine minutes and it is the fight that Nell Kest is going to be losing for a year.

Crenn had proposed that the nine pounds a quarter be spread over the whole lane — eight houses, about a pound and a shilling each, which looked, in a room, like fairness, and was in fact the reason a form that protects four houses is going to be paid for by eight.

"That is not fair," said Nell.

"It is the fairest arithmetic in this room," said Crenn, "and I want you to hear the reason, because the reason is that the amendment protects the trade and the trade is eight houses, and I am not going to hold four houses responsible for a form that four houses invented. If Mr Vare's house has had a girl leave owing money, that is his house's fact and not the Pry's, and the Pry has been on this lane forty years and has never had one, and I will not charge the Pry nine pounds a quarter for Mr Vare's history."

"Then charge it to the four that have had one, and say on the sheet that the levy is a levy on the houses that took the amendment, and that a house that has not taken it is not liable for it, and stop the four from being on it at all."

Crenn wrote for a while in a small hand and then looked up, and Nell understood that she had already worked out that this was the right answer and had been waiting to be asked, which is the thing about a frightened person with a form: they are not stupid, they are alone.

"Agreed," said Marl Crenn. "And I will write it on the sheet, and I will date it, and I will have it initialled, and I will not put it into force until the next quarter is struck, because I cannot strike a quarter in arrears and a levy that is repented of in arrears is not a levy."

"And?"

"And the next quarter, some clerk in the guild will read the sheet and think: nine pounds on four houses is a great deal for four houses, and if the levy were on eight it would be a better trade, and by the fourth month there will be a motion to spread it again, and it will be a good argument, and I have got about a year before it wins." She put the pen down. "I want that written down as well. That I have agreed to it, and that I expect to be overruled on it, and that I am agreeing to it now because a woman of twenty came round my lane on her own two afternoons and did the arithmetic in front of me, which is not a thing that happens to assessors."

"Then I will lose it," said Nell Kest.

"You will lose it," said Marl Crenn, "and you will lose it in a room of men, and it will not be in a court and there will be nothing to appeal to, and the four that signed will pay the whole of nine pounds a quarter themselves while people who did not sign are better off, and the Pry will be better off because it did nothing, and that is the correct result and you should not feel bad about it in a way that makes you stop doing it."

---

What she took away from that room, and did not write down anywhere, was the thing she had been carrying since the eighth month of the tenth and had not looked at directly since she had copied a clause into four other people's schedules.

A form does not know what it is for.

Her own clause had been written for one girl, in one workroom, by a foreman who would rather lose a house's certification than hand a departing worker a debt that lands on the next woman, and it had been a good clause, and it had been about her, and now it was a numbered form that had gone to four houses on a lane and into a second association in the next province and out of the lane in an envelope, and in the next province it was sitting in a leather case in front of a woman of about fifty with a list of eleven houses on a sheet.

And Marl Crenn had said the true thing about it in a room without being asked, which was that the clause protects the trade, and the trade is eight houses, and there is no version of a form that belongs to the person it was written for once it has a number.

Nell sat on the end of a bench in the workroom with the cold dye in the vat behind her and did the arithmetic she has been doing since she was fourteen, which is the one where you work out what a thing costs the person who made it. And the sum came out the same as it had come out on the evening of the thirtieth of the tenth, in a different part of the lane, and it came out as: two pounds and five a quarter, thirty-one years, a foreman's seat, four houses, a levy that will be fought over in the fourth month, and a year of evenings.

And the part that was new, and that she wrote down at the end of it because it was a fact and not a feeling, was this: that the four questions on the board in the lane had been written by her, and that the woman in the seed barns had given her the fourth one, and that a woman in the next province was about to write the fourth one out in her own hand at the top of a letter to eleven houses and was not going to say where it came from, and that in about two years nobody at all would be able to say whose it was, including her, and that this was the first thing in four months that had made her feel as though the work would outlast her and she was not sure that was a good feeling.

---

The sheet came back from the next province in the second week of the first month and it went to the assessor's room first, and Marl Crenn brought it down to the workroom at the vats on her own two days because she had nowhere else to put it.

It was a single sheet on good laid paper, in a fast upright hand, and it had a question written on the top of it in the same hand, and the question was: *In a house of this guild, where a second instrument has been entered, and a girl of nineteen was taken on a term in the eighth month, and the term does not name a person, and the exemption was granted in the sixth year before that —*

Nell read it out to the workroom, because a thing read out in a room is a thing that exists.

"—does the exemption stand or does it go, and if it goes, does it go on the day the term was entered or on the day anybody noticed, and if anybody has been paid a wage in the meantime does that put it back, and one more thing and I am sorry, the girl asked me whether it is her fault and I said I did not know and she has not spoken to me since."

Ostyn Vare was in the doorway. He had been in the doorway for about a minute before she got to the word *wage*, and Nell saw him put his hand on the frame, and saw him take it off again.

"That," he said, "is a house of ours. Marrowgate. The girl of nineteen is our Lisse and she was taken on in the eighth month, and that is a fact about a wage that has been paid to her every Friday since Michaelmas."

Nell Kest put the sheet down on the flat of the vat flat, in the middle of the room, where anybody could get at it, and put a stone on it, which is a thing she had been taught by a letter from Millbrook Reach three hundred miles away and has been doing ever since.

"Then that is the answer to the first half," she said, "and the last question is the only one in it that is really being asked, and it is not about the exemption, and it will be asked in a house in the next province in about a week by somebody who has not got anybody to ask."

Marek Kest is in Auremar. He is forty miles up the road and a day away and is writing a file. He is not coming, and she has known that since the twentieth of the ninth month, when she told him what she would need, and she is not going to say so to him, and she is not going to write and ask.

She picked up the reed and copied the question out onto the back of her own four questions, in full, with the date on it, and put the original in an envelope and wrote a woman's name on the outside of the envelope and took it to the post herself at the eighth hour, and put two shillings on the counter for the coach, which she did not have, and took the change out of the four pounds in the cloth in the drawer that the vicar and the sum are still in.
