# Chapter 48: Twenty-Nine, and the Woman at the Chandler's Counter

A distributed record is not a controlled record, and Tamsin Rook had spent four months making copies she could not recall, and the second week of the seventh month is when she found out what that looks like from the other end.

She wrote to all of them. That is what the list is for and it has always been what the list is for, and for the first eight months it had meant writing to a person to ask whether they had arrived, and now it meant writing to a person to ask whether they would keep a document.

Forty-one names, thirty-two answers, nine non-answers as at the tenth of the fourth month, and six things on a line of which she had only ever filled five. One of the nine was a man she had never had an address for and had therefore never once asked. Four of them were one house of dyers in a furnace town. She did not tidy the column and she did not move anybody into a seventh thing.

Twenty-nine said yes.

Twelve said no, and the twelve are the part of this that she went back and read four times, because a person who has answered a question is a person you can go back to and a person who has refused one is a person you have to think about before you write again.

Four of the twelve said they had a creditor or a landlord or a factor's man in the house and had been warned by somebody about papers with names on them and were not having another.

Three said they could not read well enough to know what they were agreeing to keep.

Two said the room the sheet was in got damp.

One said that she had burned it, and gave a reason that Tamsin Rook wrote down in full because she is a copyist and reasons are the thing copyists are for: *I burned it because I have a daughter of nineteen and a man came about the Venn thing in the tenth month and asked me whether we kept copies of anything, and I said no, and he wrote that down, and he will remember that I said it.*

One said that she had had one of these come to her door in the second month of this year, a man with a tray, and that she had said yes, and that he had come back in the third month with a second question and a paper, and that she was not going to be the house a man comes back to.

One said that keeping a copy of a decision was a thing you did if you were the sort of person who was going to be proved right, and that she had four children and eleven hundred pounds of debt behind her and was not going to be that sort of person, and that this was a fair position and that she did not think less of anybody for the other one.

And three of the twenty-nine had written back in about four days.

---

The one that mattered was the house on a row by the Kiln sheds.

The counting house took the packet on the sixth of the seventh month, because the counting house takes every packet, because a counting house is the building in that row where a man can find out what a thing is going to cost before it costs it, and it had had the Cinder Court's forty-one pages on its counter at about the fourth hour of the sixth of the seventh month, and it still had them on the sixth day.

It had not used them. It had not sold them. It had not shown them to anybody.

That is the most frightening answer there is, and Tamsin Rook wrote it in her own book on the sixth of the seventh month at about the ninth hour, in the flat form a copyist uses when she does not want to write the sentence she would like to write, and the sentence is: *a copy in a building where somebody sells things changes nothing on the day it arrives and may change something in six months, and there is no instrument in this empire that says a person may not keep a document, and there ought not to be one, and if there were one I would not put it in a list of mine.*

She went and stood in the street outside it on the seventh and looked at the sign, which was a mark and not a name: a filled square with a bar through it and a notch cut out of the top left corner. The mark is against thirty-eight lines in the carry column of a ledger with the word *paid* beside it, in three districts. The mark has now also been read out in the open minute book of the Cinder Court, in room four, on the seventeenth of the sixth month, and the house that keeps it knows that, because the man who keeps it went to the Crown Kiln on the seventeenth with a leather case and asked at a counter.

Nobody has used it. On the seventh that is a day of nothing. On the fifteenth it is nine days, and she stood in the same street and looked at the same sign, and it is the fifteenth that she wrote the sentence below and the second that she wrote the first half of it.

---

Bryn Mawer was copying in the counting house, and had been for about five weeks, and she was seventeen, and she had been Corvin Dray's copying girl in a stone house two streets off the Kiln road for two years before that, and she had not been on the bench at the inventory, and she was not one of the eleven protected people, and she had a line in a file in a building in this city and no wages.

She had also, on the seventh of the seventh month, written to the third gallery, over her own signature, four lines that said: *I copied the Cinder Court record. I have copied it four times. I have been asked by a man in this building what it is worth and I have told him what I think it is worth and I have told him in the wrong order, on purpose, twice, because I have worked out that he does not know that I have worked that out. I would like to be examined in the copying again, in my own words, and not recorded as frightened, and I am not frightened, and I have been frightened for two years and it has not improved and I can still read.*

Tamsin Rook read that letter in an alcove with a stove in it and then went down to Candlewright Lane and found her, and the finding of her was not a difficulty.

"What do you want to be examined in," said Tamsin.

"The copying." Bryn Mawer was seventeen and she said it the way people say a thing they have thought about for a year. "Not the room. The copying. There is a thing nobody has asked anybody in this case and it is the only thing I am any use for, so I am going to say it and you may write it down. Every copy of that record made in this building from the sixth of the seventh month is a copy of a decision of a high court that says no person may be joined as guarantor without being heard and signing. That is worth about four hundred pounds to a man with a bench and a bad ledger. Not to buy. To *use*, later, on a house that has been told that a district court can join the person who holds a barn."

"How many copies have gone out of that building since the sixth."

"Nine that I have made. Thirty-one that I know of, because they do not all come through me." She did not raise her voice and she did not lower it either. "And I will tell you what nobody has thought about, because nobody has ever been in a room where copies are the whole business. A copy of a decision is a weapon and it is a target. The households that kept it are the households that can be shown to have had notice. I said yes and I have a creditor's man at my table twice a year about a debt of nineteen pounds. And in four months, when a man with a tray comes down that lane and asks who in it has got a copy of the Cinder Court's decision, and somebody tells him, then this decision becomes a thing that the people who kept it are *known* by, and I am one of them, and I am seventeen, and I have no trade and no bench and no assessment and nobody who will stand in a room with me."

"So why did you ask to be examined."

"Because you asked me in the tenth month of last year and I said yes and nobody ever did anything about it," said Bryn Mawer, "and because I have worked out that the only thing I have is that I am good at this, and being good at it is not a thing a person can say, and a copyist in a room with a stove in it can say it out loud and write it down and it is then a fact about a person instead of an opinion about a room, and that is the whole of the difference between me and a boy with a tray and a good coat, and it took me two years to be able to say it and I would like it said in a book in case I go back to being frightened in the winter."

Tamsin Rook wrote it down in a book of her own, with the date on it, and read it back, and the girl made her read back the last line twice, the way a woman in a kitchen on a lane had made her read back a small line about a man owing nothing, twice.

---

The chandler's counter is in Candlewright Lane and it is a counter and not a shop, because the chandler lost his trade for a month in the tenth month of last year to a bar on a door and got it back, and the woman who keeps the front room now takes in copying at a penny a sheet, which is half what the Greyfen road charges and a quarter of what the town owes her.

It is Bryn Mawer. She does it on the four days a week the counting house on the Kiln row has shut, and she is paid at the counter, in coin, by a man who took her address in the second week of the eleventh month of last year as a formality and has not used it and does not know what it is for, and which is why the copying in that lane is not a rumour.

---

On the eleventh of the seventh month Tamsin Rook carried four pages of her own book up to the Mosswake Roll Court window, out to the Reed Cut works, down to a chandler's counter in Candlewright Lane, and as far as a cart road that goes down to a dyers' lane in Greyfen, and she put one on the sill at Mosswake for a shilling, which she did not pay, and one into a works book at Reed Cut for nothing, and one on the counter of a shop with a price painted in the window for a penny, and one into a bundle going down the cart road for nothing.

Four hands, and only one of the four is a clerk. A copyist with a shop and a price, a clerk who keeps two registers, a foreman who keeps a works book, and a woman in a dyers' lane.

Each of the four had about eleven lines on it and no heading and no office on it, and they were copies of the same eleven lines out of her own book, and the eleven lines were the rule, which is dated to the twenty-fourth of the twelfth month of last year and is in her own hand and says:

*A list of who holds a copy of a thing is not a list of people who have reported. Nobody is to be asked who has read one. Nobody is to be added to one because they were seen with a copy. If a person says no, the answer is no and it is not a question and it does not go anywhere.*

And on the four pages the four hands disagreed, and the disagreement was written on all four, in four hands, with the date, and nobody tidied it.

Sarn's page had the four women of a dyers' trade in a furnace town entered by their four first names, with the date she had the names, which was the third of the seventh month, off a copy of a copy that came out of that lane in the sixth.

Tamsin's own entry for those four had been *Ferren, fourth past the weigh-house, and four women whose first names I have not got*, and it was still that, in her own book, with the rider of the twenty-fourth of the second month against it.

And on the page at the chandler's counter, in a hand that wrote small and fast and had been taught in a copying room, there was a name that Tamsin Rook did not have at all and had never had and would never be told by anybody: a woman in Greyfen with four children and no candle, one of the nine who had stopped answering in the first month, who had not answered a letter from a Chancery in eight months and had answered, in the third week of the seventh, a letter from a shop with a price on the window and a waiting list inside it, and had given her name, and had asked for a sheet, and had paid for it, and had not told Tamsin Rook.

"She paid," said Tamsin Rook, when she read it, twice, at a counter. "She paid four pence for a sheet of a decision about guarantors, out of a house with four children and no candle, because it was in a shop window and shops have prices and free things need a favour and she has not got a favour from a stranger in a window."

"Is that a bad thing."

"That is the best thing in my book," said Tamsin Rook, "and I did not do it, and I am not going to be able to find out whether it was luck or whether a window is simply better at asking than a woman is."

And the sixth column of her own line is still empty, and there are six things on it, and she has filled five of them in eight months, and the sixth is *why not*, and she has not written it, and she wrote down on the eleventh of the seventh month that she was not going to write it, and that the sentence about the woman with four children and the name she did not have went in the *what* column and not in the sixth, and that this was the correct use of the line.

---

On the fifteenth of the seventh month she was in a room in the Auremar district house with a clerk, and the minute of the eighteenth of the ninth month of last year was open on the table, and she read it out, and it refused a plea, and it said so in a form that had a name at the foot of it, and nobody in that room made any attempt to refuse it again.

"Take the minute of the refusal and read the last line of it," said the clerk.

"It is refused," said Tamsin Rook, "and it is refused, and it was refused once and entered, and this office cannot refuse it twice because the thing has already been decided, and the only business anybody has in this room today is the next date."

The next date was the second month of next year, in the second hour, in the chamber of the Auremar district court, and there were four things attached to it, and she copied all four out and read them back twice.

That the man is not to be released on his own recognisance before the sitting, and is to remain in the district house.

That he is to be given no paper except paper of the court's own, and every sheet is to be accounted for at the door by an officer.

That he is not to speak to any of the eleven protected people named in the order of the seventeenth of the ninth month, except through an officer of this court, and that a breach of it is to be entered.

And that the trial is confined to the local forgery charges, to the entries in his own ledger of ninety-one leaves, and to the thirty-eight lines in three districts against which a mark appears, and that no count arising out of the sealed instruction, or out of any office, is to be tried with it, and that his appearance to be tried is not an admission touching any person or office not named.

"He is not naming a client," said Tamsin Rook, when she had read it all back.

"No."

"He has been asked by four separate people in eight months and he has not named one and he is not going to be asked again for one, because a man who cannot be got to name one can be made to say anything by a person who wants him to." She squared the sheet. "And the mark is in the record as a mark, and the court said in open court on the seventeenth of the sixth month that it does not know what house keeps it, and that is going to be true in nine years, and a counting house on a row by the Kiln sheds has had a copy of the record sitting on its counter for nine days doing nothing, and I would like it noted in my own book, with the day on it, that I checked, and that a thing that has not happened is not a thing that is safe."

She wrote it down. And then she wrote the four lines underneath it that she has been writing at the bottom of pages since the twenty-sixth of the second month, and which are the only lines in her book that are not about a document.

> *Four hands have the list now. I have one page of it and three other people have one page each and the four pages do not agree and the disagreement is written on all four and nobody has tidied it and it is the first thing I have ever handed to anybody that got worse by being handed over.*
>
> *It is better. That is what I have instead of the feeling. I can account for twenty-nine of forty-one in nine provinces and three of the four people who hold my pages can account for more than I can about their own districts, and one of the names on one of those pages is a woman with four children and no candle who paid four pence in a shop window and told nobody, and I will never know her name from my own book and I do not have to, and that is the first thing in eight months that I have got that is actually better than what I had, and it is better because it is not mine.*
