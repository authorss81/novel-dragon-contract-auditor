# Chapter 5: The Next Household Worker

The Greyfen dye-house smelled of hot indigo, iron liquor, and the particular sweetness of madder that gets into a person's hair and stays there through winter. Nell Kest had worked in it for two years and had learned to read colour the way other people read weather, and she had spent that evening standing at the press table with a wage schedule in front of her and one finger under a line she had already read four times.

The workroom was emptying. Eleven women and four men, the vat crews, the press hands, the two boys who turned the drying racks. The last of the light came in through the high slot windows and made the wet floor look like something spilled.

Ostyn Vare came in with the lamp and put it on the bench and sat down opposite her, which he did not normally do, because Vare was a foreman and foremen stand.

"Three readings in the schedule," he said. "I want to be sure you have three readings before we talk about anything at the end of it."

"I've had four."

"Good." He turned the schedule so it was the right way up for her, which was a courtesy, since he had put it down the wrong way. "The wage is four and six the week, up from four and two at the spring, and it holds to Michaelmas. That's real, and it's the best it's been in this house in six years, and I fought for it and I lost sleep over it. Second: the seventh-year certificate. If you're still here at the seventh year you can be examined for the certified dye-hand seat, and that's worth eleven shillings a week and a share of the rinse yard, and I'd rather have you take it than the girl from Salt Teeth who can lift."

"The seventh year is four years off."

"It is. But you're twenty and you're here, which in this craft is the entire qualification." He put his thumb on the sheet. "Third thing is at the bottom, under the trade schedule, and it's a continuation, and I am going to read it out loud because I promised Nell Kest I would read it out loud, and I have read it out loud to two other people this month who are not as calm about it as you are."

Nell had asked him to read it aloud. She had not told him why, and he had not asked, because Ostyn Vare had been a foreman for nineteen years and had learned that a woman who asks to have a document read to her is not asking about reading.

"*Where a worker of this house leaves the craft before the term of her wage is ended, the tools in her charge, and any debt of the house against her in respect of those tools, shall be answered by the next household worker of that house, and by no other person, save that a household which has no other worker shall answer the same itself.*"

The workroom had gone very quiet in the way that workrooms do.

"That's me," Nell said.

"That's the clause. Not you specifically."

"Ostyn. If I go, my debt for the tools in my charge goes on the next woman in this house. And if there isn't one, it goes on the house." She said it evenly. She had been holding the anger at exactly this temperature since the fourth month, and she had learned that if she let it get hotter she would say something about the guild instead of about the schedule. "I have four shillings of debt on the indigo account. I have said so, in this room, in front of the foreman, and I have said I would pay it in the fourth month of next year, and I have said it every month since. And now there is a line in the trade schedule that says if I am not here in the fourth month of next year, the four shillings are not a debt I owed and paid, they are a debt that follows me into whoever stands at my press."

"That is what a continuation is."

"I know what a continuation is. I have read the inheritance desk's leaflets. I know what a continuation does and I know what it looks like when it has been done properly and I know what it looks like when it has been written by somebody who did not want anybody to ask." She turned the schedule round so that the bottom margin was on top. "Do you know who wrote that line?"

"No."

"You do. You read it to two other people this month."

"I did, and I will tell you who wrote it, and you will not care." Vare took off his cap and turned it over in his hands, which Nell had never seen him do. "The guild assessor in the spring. It is a standard trade-schedule continuation. It is in the dyers' schedules, the fullers' schedules, and the pressmen's, and it has been in the dyers' since before I was born. It is the reason this house holds its exemption."

"Say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that the exemption is what lets the house train you and pay you four and six, and if a house can lose its tools to a departing worker, the guild does not certify the house, and an uncertified house does not hold a seventh-year certificate seat, and if we do not hold the seat then in three years there is no examination to sit and you are what you are now, which is a person who is good at a job and cannot prove it." He put the cap on. "So when you ask me for a visible exit clause, Nell, I hear you. And I want you to hear what it costs, because I will not be the man who signs your four shillings over to a stranger and then tells you it was the only way."

"Then what is a visible exit clause?"

"That you can see."

"Then show me one that is not a knife."

He laughed, once, without much in it. "A clause that says a worker leaving in good standing owes the house the sum she has actually borrowed, to be settled inside a named number of weeks, and that no other worker's wage, tools, or name may be attached to it. That is what you are asking for. It is nine words longer than the one you have, and it is legible, and every house on this lane would read it and understand it, and about a third of them could not sign it without losing the exemption."

"And me?"

"And you would be the first person on this lane with one," Ostyn Vare said, "and I would be the foreman who let it happen, and I would have to explain it to the assessor, and the assessor would ask me whether the four shillings came out of your wages or out of the next woman's, and I would have to say it came out of yours, and then the assessor would ask why my house was the only one in Greyfen willing to lose a tool for a person leaving." He spread his hands. "That is what it costs. It is not money. It is me, in a room, on a Thursday."

The door at the end of the workroom opened, and Marek Kest came in.

Nell had not told him she was reading it tonight. She had told her foreman, and she had asked Tamsin Rook to come, and Tamsin had written back five words: *I will be there. Bring it.* She had deliberately not written to her brother, because her brother was a clerk in a city building, and because he had that thing he did, and because she had spent two years watching him decide what a person could safely know.

He came in with Tamsin and he had a satchel, and he looked at the table, and he looked at the schedule, and Nell saw the exact moment he decided to remove the document before she could read it.

"No," she said.

"I was going to ask Nell to—"

"You were going to pick it up." He had not touched it yet, and his hand was still a foot from the paper. "You were going to take it off this table and read it yourself and come back with a version."

"I was going to read the continuation clause to her accurately—"

"You were going to remove it from the room."

Marek stopped. Tamsin Rook, behind him, said nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said, because Tamsin was a person who filled silence professionally.

Nell stood up.

"Read it," she said. "Out loud. Exactly as it is written, and then tell me what it does, and then tell me why you came to my workroom at a quarter to seven in the evening instead of writing to me."

He read it. He read it correctly, which she had not expected, and then he explained it, and his explanation was better than the foreman's had been because he actually knew what a continuation was, and Nell listened to the explanation and understood every word of it and found that she was angrier, not less, because a thing you can explain is a thing that was done on purpose.

"And the guild schedule," she said. "Is that a forgery?"

"I don't know. I have found a discrepancy in a different instrument in a different district and I have not got as far as a forgery."

"Then don't tell me it's a forgery."

"I didn't."

"You told me to keep away from it."

"I told you not to sign anything tonight." He put the schedule down flat and squared it with two fingers, a habit from a desk in a city building, and Nell thought: *he cannot help it, he will tidy it, he is going to tidy a piece of paper on my workroom table.* "Nell, the reason is that there is a man in Mosswake—"

"I know about Mosswake. You told me a man in Mosswake four weeks ago and then you stopped telling me anything, and then this week you sent me a letter about my wages being safe, which was not true, was it."

"No. It was not true. It was late, and it was not true, and I wrote it because I thought—" He stopped. "I thought that if you were not frightened of the contract you would have room to be frightened of the clause."

"And if I had room to be frightened of the clause, what would I have done with the room?"

"You would have told somebody you trust."

"Yes."

"Whom."

"That is not the point," Marek said, "and you know it isn't, and you are using it because you have been furious with me since Michaelmas and you have not had a good excuse."

The silence in the workroom had changed shape. The last of the crew were pretending very hard not to listen.

"Sit down, Marek," Nell said. "Then give me your satchel."

He did not move.

"Give me your satchel."

"Nell—"

"You came here tonight with a document in that bag." She had been looking at it since he walked in, because she had watched him check the strap twice in a way that meant he knew what was in it. "I have known for four weeks that you have had something. I have decided I did not want it. I have changed my mind and I want it now, in front of Tamsin, and I want to know what it is before I sign anything in this room tonight, and if it is nothing then I will say you were carrying a spare latch, and we will all go home."

Marek sat down on the bench and took the strap off his shoulder and put the satchel on the table between them, and Nell opened it.

There were three books in it. The first was a court file with a green cord on it. The second was a wax tablet, cheap, the kind you buy at a market. The third was a folded square of copying stuff with a Chancery watermark, and when she shook it out, four lines of printed text fell onto the workroom table, and she knew two of the lines off the first page of any inheritance file ever printed, and the third of them was a sentence she had read on the wage schedule, six inches away from her elbow, in different words.

Nell picked it up.

"*...the field-side upkeep and the winter-seed repayment named to him are recorded against the field and continue, the present holder being bound to the same until full review.*"

Nobody said anything.

"That," Nell said, "is not our clause. Our clause says *the next household worker of that house*. That says *the present holder*." She looked up. "What is it."

Marek told her. He told her properly, in order, without softening it, and it took him perhaps four minutes: the widow, the field, the seven-year instrument, the correction with no authority behind it, the third notch, the four-line summary, the daughter who has not signed anything, the date on the fourteenth.

He did not tell her about the Kest file. She noticed that he did not tell her about it.

Nell put the four lines down on the table, squared them against the edge of the bench, and looked at them for a while, and then said: "You had this since the seventh."

"Yes."

"And you came to my workroom tonight because I wrote to Tamsin and not to you."

"Yes."

"You let me stand here for three days reading a schedule with *the next household worker* in it, and you had a piece of paper in your bag that says the same thing with different words, and you decided that I would be safer not knowing."

"I decided—"

"I know what you decided." She was not shouting. She had gone quiet, and quiet was worse, and Marek could see in her face the exact expression he had been wearing at the gate at Reed Cut when the keeper said *a person who cannot end an obligation eventually cannot refuse one.* "Marek. You are not protecting me. You are protecting yourself from the version of this where I am frightened and you are the one who told me. And every single time you decide what I can safely know, you are the one holding it. You and nobody else. That's what you want. And you call it looking after your sister."

"Yes," Marek said.

Nell let that sit.

"Give me the draft."

"What?"

"The other one. There is another one. You have had that bag open on this table for two minutes and you have put out a file and a tablet and a summary, and you have the flap of the inside pocket turned back, and you have been careful with that pocket twice, and I have been reading paper since I was nine." She held out her hand. "Give me the draft."

Marek did not move for a moment, and then he took out a single sheet of Chancery copying stuff, folded twice, and put it on the table.

Tamsin Rook came and looked at it over Nell's shoulder.

It was headed, in Marek's own careful hand, *Exception, proposed, Mosswake Roll Court, in the matter of the continuation at the Greyfen dye-house.* It provided that the debt for tools in the charge of a departing worker should be answered by the departing worker herself, and not by any other worker, and it recited, in the third line, that the clerk was of the Inheritance Desk and had compared the record.

"He wrote this," Tamsin said. "In the alcove. On Tuesday, after I had gone, and I found it in the copying wastebasket under the bench and I read it and I put it back, because I could not think at the time whether I was allowed to say anything."

Nell read it. "What is wrong with it?"

"Nothing on the page," Marek said. "That's the trouble with it."

"Then say what is wrong with it."

"It is not valid." He said it to the table. "A clerk at this desk has no power to write an exception at all. An exception is a thing a Continuity Auditor writes, and a Continuity Auditor writes it in a hearing, and the party it moves the consequence onto has to be in the room and has to agree, and I have put down that the present worker answers the debt herself, and I have not asked Vare, and I have not asked the guild, and I have not asked whether the person whose wage I am protecting is prepared to be named in a public document and live with it for the rest of her life. And the third line is a lie, because I have compared nothing. I compared a summary to a witness copy in a lamp-lit gallery and I have not opened the cabinet."

"You wrote a court instrument that is not a court instrument," Tamsin said, "and it recites a comparison you did not make, and you were going to file it."

"No."

"You were going to give it to Nell to use."

"I was going to give it to her to have." He took it back. "I know the difference is not good enough. I am telling you it is not good enough, because Tamsin has made it clear that keeping things from her is not a legal move, and I have heard that, and I am not going to argue with it tonight."

Tamsin picked up the sheet, folded it once, and put it in her own case. Not the wastebasket. Her own case.

"I am going to keep this," she said, "and I am going to keep the second book as well, and neither of them is going into your satchel again. And Marek, I want to say the rest of it, because you have just spent ten minutes being honest and I would rather give you the honesty back than let you have the ten minutes on their own."

"Then say it."

"I have worked beside you for three weeks and I have never once seen you wrong about a page." She had her arms folded, and this time it was not reserving an opinion. "Tonight I have seen you write a document to a court you cannot reach, on a comparison you have not made, about a person who is not in the room, so that a person who loves you would be protected from a decision she would have made differently. And I understand exactly why. I want that on the record, because I understand it and I am still not going to work beside you."

"Tamsin—"

"Not tonight. Tonight I will read the exit clause aloud for both of you, because Nell asked for it and I promised, and reading it aloud is work. After that you will take your satchel and you will go back to a district where a woman is going to be told on the fourteenth that her daughter is a debtor, and you will decide what to do about that, and you will tell Nell before you decide it, not after."

Nell had been standing quite still. Now she put her hand flat on the wage schedule, which she had been smoothing and smoothing and smoothing.

"Read it," she said. "The whole clause, and then the exit. Read it in this room, in front of the foreman and the witness and the crew, and let it be heard. And Marek—" she looked up, and her face was her mother's face with a colder mouth, "if you write me anything again, you show it to the person it is about before you write it. That is the whole of my terms. I do not care how good the handwriting is."

Marek got up. He did not say the thing he wanted to say, which was that he had been afraid of exactly this, and that being afraid of it had made it certain.

At the door he stopped and said, to Tamsin, low enough that only she heard it: "The eleventh is the inspection and the fourteenth is the notice. There is a hold on the field now that I did not ask for and cannot lift, and it is the only thing standing between that girl and a notice. If I take it to a hearing, the hold comes off and the notice issues. If I leave it, the field freezes and the Venns lose the harvest while everybody waits to see what I can prove."

Tamsin did not look at him. "Then that is a decision with a name and a date on it, and not a piece of paper in a bag," she said. "Say it where Nell can hear it. She has just told you she is not a substitute for a family secret, and she meant it about the dye-house, and she will mean it about the field."

Marek stood in the doorway of his sister's workroom with the lamp behind him and understood, with the clarity that comes at the end of a very long day, that he had lost the only advantage he had ever had, which was the ability to be the one person in the room who knew.

The vat crews had gone quiet. In the yard, the drying racks stood in rows like a fence, and above them the slot windows had gone black, and in Auremar, eight miles off across the flat, a hold with a sealed reference and no office name on it was sitting in a register waiting for the morning.
