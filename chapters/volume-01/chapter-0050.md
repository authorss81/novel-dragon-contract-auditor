# Chapter 50: A Bundle With a Thread In It

The file could not be closed, and Marek Kest had known that since the fifth of the ninth month of last year, and had spent the four months since the review of the fourth of the fourth month working out what a man does at the end of a thing that is not an ending.

Three things stood in the way. The request of the eighteenth of the ninth month, and its supplement of the eighth of the eleventh, are alive in the custody of Examinations and have never been withdrawn. What came back on the ninth of the fourth month was a decision on the *scope* of the request, and it is true, and it answers two of the five questions and refers three to the standing instructions of a district, and it is sealed and there is no name at the foot of it; that is not an answer and it is not a refusal and there is no word for it that can be asked about twice. The second sealed page is shut, and there is a refusal against Marek Kest's name in a form in that same office, and the refusal was correct and there is no second refusal to be had. And the Kest property-and-debt record is neither enforceable nor free, and a clerk cannot end his own time at a desk with a decision, because a decision would be a thing with his name at the top of it and there is not going to be one.

So the correct end to his time at the Inheritance Desk was a transfer, with a date on it, and a list of what was in the file and what was shut in it.

He did not choose the successor. The Registrar chose him, in about four minutes on the twelfth of the eighth month, and said why: Halvard Riet had been at the Inheritance Desk nine years and had asked for the Venn box and not the Kest one and had been given the Kest one as well, because a man who will take the harder box is the only man who will be there in nine years, and because Marek Kest had no standing to object and had not tried to.

The transfer was dated the first of the ninth month and made out in three copies, and the third is the one that matters: a list in Marek Kest's own hand, with no heading on it.

> *Filed in this gallery, and handed over on the first of the ninth month, and any person may come to the counter and ask for every item on this list and may be refused nothing on it that is not shut.*
>
> *One: the Venn packet — the source leaf in the cabinet of the Notaries' Table on the north wall under the chain key, the witness copy, the correction of the ninth of the eighth month, the Reed Cut memorandum of the eighth, the certificate impression on the back of the correction, the comparison copy in the hand of a roll-keeper, and the exhibit sleeve with its chain and two folded pages behind the memorandum.*
>
> *Two: the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth month, signed by the head of this Table, and the four refusals with a name at the foot of them.*
>
> *Three: the request of the eighteenth of the ninth month and the supplement of the eighth of the eleventh month, both in the custody of Examinations. Neither answered. The scope decision of the ninth of the fourth month is not an answer to either of them, and neither has been withdrawn. Ask Examinations for them by the number the intake clerk wrote in ink in the ninth month and initialled. He was twenty-five and nobody thanked him and he did it three times for the same man.*
>
> *Four: the request of a roll-keeper of this gallery, filed on the ninth of the fourth month over her own signature, naming the district of Glasswater, and unanswered. It is not mine. It is hers. Do not chase it for me and do not tell her it was chased.*
>
> *Five: the Kest property-and-debt record, reopened by the Registrar on the twelfth of the eighth month and not cleared. The name of Ilyra Kest remains a redaction in the public roll. A copy of the Chancery roll of the year of the fire is in a tin in Greyfen in the house of a locksmith and he is not to be written to about it by anybody in this building without his saying so first.*
>
> *Six: shut. Two pages behind the memorandum in the chain. One of them is a quarterly return of a dragon seat and it has been read. The other has not been read and I have refused to read it, twice, on the record, in two rooms, with two different witnesses, and the second refusal is this line. I will not read it. If you read it, you will be a second person in this empire who has, and the woman who swore a statement in a form made once would be examinable about it in a way I have spent eight months making sure she is not. Leave the seal on it, and if anybody asks you why, say that the person who had the file before you made two decisions about it in a room and signed one of them, and that is the whole of the authority you have.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Inheritance Desk, the twelfth of the eighth month. Signed without being asked, which is a thing a clerk of Examinations taught me in a warm room in the eleventh month of last year and which is the single most useful thing anybody taught me in eight months.*

---

The Registrar reopened the Kest file himself, on the twelfth of the eighth, at about the second hour, at the counter, with a young clerk who wrote the time in the margin in ink and initialled it because nobody had asked him to and this was the fourth time in a year and he had still never been thanked.

Reopening it means one thing and it is a hard thing to be told in a room with a person in it: the file is no longer closed to anybody. Any person at the counter of the third gallery may now ask for it, may pay a shilling for a copy of anything in it that is not shut, may read the entry of the property and the entry of the debt and the four openings and closings since the fire, and may go away and think whatever they like about the woman whose name is a redaction in the public roll of the year of the fire and a blank in a Chancery roll in Greyfen, and has been nothing at all, in three buildings, for twelve years. It is not cleared. Being reopened is the opposite of being cleared, and the Registrar said so out loud and entered it, and the young clerk wrote the time.

And Marek Kest stood at a counter in a building he has walked into every working morning for four years and understood that he has just agreed, in a room, with no witness but a clerk of the intake desk, that from that day forward every stranger who wants to know about his mother is a person he did not decide to tell.

That is the price of the second refusal, and he had thought it was the price of a third one, and there is no third one. There is one refusal in a form in Examinations and one line in a list in his own hand, and both cost the same thing, which is that a person who has not been read cannot be made to answer for not having been read.

---

He posted the ruling in two places on the fifteenth of the eighth month, both as one sheet with all five terms on it and the date, because four of the five are the ones the lane says by number, and there is no version of this that anybody can hear four out of and infer the fifth. They went on in the order they were ruled, in the magistrate's words, and only the second has anything new in it.

> *First: the personal debt of Tavi Venn is void.*
> *Second: the channel and the lower field are preserved to the household of Aldren Venn until the second month, when seven years from the signing are up, against any creditor, tenant, surety or assignee.*
> *Third: the gate and navigation duty of the water keeper is unaffected.*
> *Fourth: any person who thereafter assumes a continuing field-side duty at that place must sign a new obligation for it, the original instrument requiring it.*
> *Fifth: the district water office shall perform the bank works and the gate reset under its own flood authority and not under the instrument, and the household is not required to perform the field-side upkeep for the purposes of this order.*

One went on the reading steps of the Roll Hall in Auremar, where a ruling read out loud is a thing that happens and has no clerk and no number. The other went four hundred and thirty miles down the river to a board at a window, into a space under nine feet of a trial's own accounting, under a line ruled off in a clerk's hand on the fourth of the sixth month before anybody had thought of asking her to, and a copy of it is a shilling, because everything on that board is a shilling a copy and the day it stops being one is the day nobody in a flood district can prove a thing.

And under the five terms, in Sarn's own hand and not in his, because it is her board and she wrote it and he asked her to write it, is one line.

> *The second of these five terms preserved the channel and the lower field to the household of Aldren Venn until the second month, when seven years from the signing are up. That day was the nineteenth of the second month of this year. It passed with nothing decided about it. The file of Aldren Venn cannot be made complete and is not going to be, because the only person who could have given the court a word about the winter seed swore a statement in a form made once with a box in it for the other party, and the other party is in a ground on the upper lane. That is a fact and not a grievance. — I. Sarn.*

---

The Received Room is on the second floor of the Roll Hall and it has a barred door, a table, a stove, and a shelf, and it is where things go when nobody wants to be the one who has them, which the intake clerk of the Chancery said out loud in the ninth month of last year to a clerk who did not yet know that it was a description of himself. The shelf holds documents that have been received and have not been filed, and there are eleven feet of it, and the oldest thing on it is twelve years old. Marek Kest had been moved into the room on the first of the ninth month, which is the day he handed the file over, and on the first morning he came up the stairs he did what every person does who is put in a room like that, which is to look at the shelf.

There is a bundle at the back of the second shelf, on its end, in the kind of wrapper a branch archive wraps a thing in when it is going somewhere and the somewhere is a different building. The wrapper is scorched along one edge, the scorch has gone brown, and it has been gone over with a hand at some point, badly, by somebody who was not thinking about it.

*Ashfall branch, fourth shelf, uncollated. Received the ninth month of the year twelve years back. Damaged, four items, uncollated.*

That is the whole of what is written on it, and it is written twice, once in the hand of whoever packed it and once in the hand of whoever received it, and the two hands disagree about the number in one of them, and the receiving clerk wrote *four* and underlined it.

Nobody has opened it, and that is not because anybody decided not to. It came in on a day in the ninth month twelve years ago with a note saying the branch archive had burned and this was what came out of it, and it was put on a shelf, and a shelf is a shelf, and every year since there has been a first clerk of the year in the third gallery who has looked at it and gone to do something else. There have been eleven of those clerks, and one of them is him.

And on the collated edge of the wrapper — the edge where the leaves of a bundle are pressed flat against the wrapper and the thread goes through — there is a red thread. It goes through two places. It goes through the wrapper, and it goes through the leaf beneath the wrapper, and the leaf beneath the wrapper is not from this year, and the thread is old, and the way it is put is a way anybody can learn in a week.

It is a stitch. It is the thing a copyist does when she is working through a bundle of thirty pages looking for one passage and wants to come back to it: she puts the thread through two leaves at once, or through a leaf and the wrapper, so that when the bundle is picked up again the two leaves are together and the passage is where she left it. That is all it is. It is a way of marking a page, and a way of marking a page is a person who was working and not a person who was plotting, and it is a smaller and more human thing than what he has been looking for, and it does not tell him who was right.

He did not open the bundle. He wrote down what he could see, with the date on it, and put it on the file.

> *A red thread passes through the collated edge of a wrapper of the Ashfall branch, received the ninth month of the year twelve years back, marked damaged, four items, uncollated, not opened, and through the leaf beneath it. It is a working stitch, used to mark a passage in a bundle. It is not a message and I have not treated it as one. There is a rule on this matter from the eighteenth of the ninth month of last year about a red thread in a tin under an old man's bed, and the rule is that two facts of the same colour are not joined in any sentence, and the rule does not expire because I have found a third.*
>
> *I have not written to Nevin Trost, and the rule I made with a copyist in a copying alcove in the twelfth month of last year was designed to protect me and is now protecting him, and a rule that only ever works in one direction is not a rule and is a preference.*

---

He wrote to Nell on the sixteenth of the eighth month and it is four lines and it took him about eleven minutes, and he has not written to her since the twenty-ninth of the first month, and he did not put a single line of it into a book first.

> *Nell —*
>
> *The guild's finding on the levy came out on the ninth of the sixth. It is against the four houses, three and a quarter pages, and the Caudle's is two pounds five and a quarter and a farthing because they signed first, and it goes on, and you were right about the farthing being a thing a house says about itself and you were right about the other half too, which is the sentence Roke said out loud in a lane in front of ninety people and which is now written down and which is the reason anybody in the next lane will get it right.*
>
> *Mike Caudle has four words and they are: read the line out before he puts his thumb on it. It is not enough and it is what I have got.*
>
> *M.*

He did not write about the ninth month of last year. He did not write about the closed orders and the assessment and the four hours after the fact and the other three times, and the not writing was a decision and not a mood, and he wrote the decision down afterwards with the date on it, in his own book, in eleven words.

*I was frightened of the letter and I have found out which part of it. It is not the ninth month. It is that she is the only person alive who saw me decide the thing and she did not stop me, and if I tell her about the ninth month I am asking her to do something about it, and I am not entitled to that.*

He received no answer inside the volume, and that is not a cliffhanger. It is a man who has spent eight months learning to say out loud what he wants, in four lines, to a person entitled to answer in her own time or not at all, and who has then sat with the not answering for a week and a half and has not written a second letter.

---

The last thing he did in the eighth month was write a list, in his own hand, at about the eleventh hour of the twenty-second of the eighth month, on the back of a sheet that had an old docket on the other side. It has no office on it and it is not a summary of anything, and the only thing that has grown across eight months is other people's willingness to be named.

> *Who will stand in a room under their own name, as at the twenty-second of the eighth month, and what it costs each of them, in no order of importance:*
>
> *Wendt Sayer, foreman, nine men, a boy and a wet rope — two shillings and sixpence out of a works fund under a head a stranger will read in a year.*
> *Ivet Sarn, thirty-eight, clerk of a river court — four days in the fifth year, a wall, and a drawer in her own room with a copy of a line she has never charged a man for.*
> *Nery Caudle, thirty-nine, last house on a lane, one wage — two pounds five and a quarter a quarter, taken in the same coin, in a cloth, with a farthing over.*
> *Bettany Skail, neighbour of forty-one years' standing — four pounds of tilling seed, half a mile in the rain, and a granddaughter of seven sent with a message.*
> *Ilsabet Kell, sixty-one — a note in her own words, three months in a box, a son who did not ask her to stop.*
> *Orra Dunn, thirty-six — forty quarters of rye, thirty-one miles of canal, and a request to a court to hear a creditor's paper instead of to stop him.*
> *Nessa Dunn, fifteen — two of the four questions rewritten, and a paper with her mother's hand on it and her own name at the top.*
> *Iselle Oster, forty-one, three hundred miles down the water — a book that says on its first page that nothing in it is evidence, and a quarter of an inch of iron missing from a shop bench.*
> *Sabra Ollett, forty-one, nine years at a landing office — four seconds, a sentence said in front of two witnesses, and no way to be given anything to feel better about.*
> *Hallis Dren, thirty — a page number written on a copy nobody asked him to write, and a quarterly return sent four hundred miles in a sealed sleeve that nobody in this file knows about, and which I have not mentioned to one human being in eleven months.*
> *Bryn Mawer, seventeen — two years behind a copying frame and a line in a file and no wages, a penny a sheet at a chandler's counter, and the sentence that a copy of a decision is a weapon and it is a target.*
> *Nell Kest, twenty, dye-house worker — a board, four questions, twenty-three doors, a coach fare that was two days of work and six months of saving, and a practice in four buildings in three districts that is not hers.*
> *Tamsin Rook, twenty-four, roll-keeper, having no office — a register that says on its first page that nothing in it is evidence, a request naming a district that is unanswered, four pages of a list in four hands that disagree, and a name she got from a shop window and will never know how she got.*
> *Leda Rell, magistrate, Mosswake Roll Court — sixty households in the sixth year, not named, not represented, not asked, entered against her own court's practice with her name at the foot of the third paragraph.*
> *Marl Crenn, fifty-one — a decision in her own association's favour that costs Greyfen's four houses thirty-six pounds a year, and a paragraph in a book of her own.*
> *The professional copyist on the Greyfen road — six free copies that were anger, four hundred and eleven at four pence, and a waiting list inside a window.*
> *The young clerk of the intake desk, twenty-five or thereabouts — the time in the margin, in ink, initialled, four times in a year, for the same man, and never once thanked.*
> *And four women in a furnace town, four hundred and forty miles off, with a surname and a landmark and no first names in my own hand, four first names in four other hands, and a rule thirty-one years old at the second leaf of their trade's own book with four new lines under it in a clerk's hand, the last of which is a question with no answer under it — and a woman of twenty-two in the same town who put her own name in a box on her own house's amendment and has said so out loud in a room with somebody in it, which is the only time in eight months that a person told me the thing they wanted most was a room.*

And at the foot of the page, in the same ink, in the same hand, and dated, because the rule about the date came from Nevin Trost in the eleventh month of last year and has been the only free thing anybody ever gave him:

> *There is not one line of it that says I was right.*
