# Chapter 25: The Reply of the Thirteenth

There are two ways to lose an argument in a row of offices and Marek Kest had spent two months learning the difference.

One is to say nothing, in which case the office writes the answer and the answer is about you and is in the office's words, and nobody can later say you were asked.

The other is to write a reply, in a form, in a time, and to make it carry the fact instead of the absence of one. A refusal carried as a fact has a date, an offerer, a set of terms, a person who made it, and a person who declined it. A refusal carried as an absence has a clerk who did not answer, which is what a clerk who declined looks like to anybody who was not in the room.

The first version he wrote in the alcove on the sixth of the twelfth month was the second kind, and he knew it inside four minutes of having finished it, and threw it in the stove.

> *The applicant does not accept the certification offered by this office. The applicant has no further observation.*

Three sentences, and a man sulking, and in a fortnight somebody looking for a reason to close a file would have read it as exactly that.

He wrote the second version on the eighth, and it took four days, because every one of the four facts in it was somebody else's decision being reported by a man with no standing, and a man with no standing who reports a decision has to say whose decision it was every single time or a reader will assume it was his.

---

The second version, in the end, was this, and he read it out to Tamsin Rook in the alcove on the tenth before he sent it anywhere, because it is the rule they made in the ninth month and neither of them has ever once suggested doing it any other way.

> *Received in this file on the eighteenth of the ninth month: a request of nine paragraphs. On the eighth of the eleventh month a supplementary request of two pages was added, naming the series MC/FS, the base issue of the second month of this year, the amendment sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month, both print runs, the distribution line, and the signer.*
>
> *This office, on the ninth of the eleventh month, offered to the applicant a bounded certification of the practice stated by the Mosswake Roll Court on the twelfth of the ninth month, for six months, for every district in the empire holding a flood docket, in exchange for the closure of the request. The terms of the offer are retained in the file, unsigned. It was not made by the head of this office. The applicant declined it on the twentieth of the eleventh month, in a room with a witness, and the record of the refusal is on the file at the place marked. The applicant has no objection to the terms of the offer being stated to any district, and understands that this office is under an obligation to state them.*
>
> *The applicant has not asked this office to certify anything and does not now ask it to certify anything. He asks five things, in this order, and none of them is a preference: that the Chancery produce every sheet of the series in its custody, from the earliest issue, with the offices the series has gone to; that the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities state in writing on what authority a sentence stated by one district court on the twelfth of the ninth month became approved model wording for all of them by the twenty-second; that it name the person who signed the sheet; that it preserve pending that answer every endorsement entered in the model form in every district since the sheet was issued, itemised or not itemised; and that he be told what the process described below costs, in hours and in money, and by whom, and what happens to it when the cost is spent.*
>
> *In place of the certification the applicant has asked the Mosswake Roll Court, the district water office at Reed Cut, and a roll-keeper of this building to try a named process for the flood-season continuity filings of this district for six weeks, and to say in writing what it costs. The court has agreed to try it. Its cash cost is eleven shillings for six weeks, to be found by the district works. Its cost in the hours of a clerk at one window is a hundred and fifty-six hours, which is twenty-six days of a six-hour window; of the forty-two days in that window at eleven relief rolls a day, sixteen remain, which is a hundred and seventy-six certified against four hundred and sixty needed, and two hundred and eighty-four households in that district whose seed parcels come off the rate late. Neither figure is small, and both are stated here because the applicant has been offered a cheaper thing and has not taken it, and a person who refuses the cheap thing is entitled to be asked what he is offering instead, and has four days left to answer the same way.*
>
> *This reply is not an answer to the nine paragraphs and does not withdraw them. The request is alive and unanswered, and the applicant asks that it be answered by a person whose name is written at the foot of the answer, before the fourth month, and not by a date.*

He put the reed down and read the last line back twice and Tamsin read it over his shoulder and said, "That last line is the only sentence in the thing that frightens anybody."

"It is the only one that is true," Marek said. "A date is how they keep it. A name is how somebody has to keep it."

---

The question that took four days was the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities, and it was not a hard question to answer. It was hard in the way that a floorboard is hard.

Marek went down the eleven-foot passage behind the third gallery on the ninth of the twelfth and pulled the index card for the office, which is a card the size of a playing card with a shelf number on it, because the rack is indexed by the office that sent the thing and the office is the only key anybody uses.

The card said: *Standing Office of Provincial Continuities. Series MC/FS and eleven others. Standing instructions, district flood, current. Third shelf, second bay, behind the ninth year's memoranda.*

He got a step-ladder, because the third shelf is above head height, and he wanted to be standing exactly in front of it and not reaching, because a man who has spent two months being frightened of what is in a drawer should not also be a man who cannot be honest about how he read it.

The second bay held standing instructions. They are a bound pad of about sixty leaves with a district's floods in them, one leaf per year, kept because a district office is required by nobody in particular to be able to show what it was told. He found the ninth year, turned to the flood leaf, and read the head of it.

> *Issued under the standing authority of the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities. To be filed with the district's standing instructions and not with its papers. Printed forms of this series are issued under the same authority and no further authority is required for their use in a district holding a flood docket.*

He read it a second time, standing on a ladder in a corridor, and then came down and worked out what he had just proved. It was not a discovery so much as the discovery that a thing he had known for nine years and had never once looked at was the entire answer to a question that had cost him a fortnight.

There was no man in it. There was no office with a person at the top of it. There was a seal, and a series, and a run, and a line at the head of a pad of standing instructions in a third bay of a shelf, and the line said that no further authority was required.

The authority for the circular was the drawer the circular was in.

---

He asked Pell Vey at the seventh hour on the tenth, in the third gallery, and he asked it badly, because he had spent nine days rehearsing a version in which Pell had to answer and Pell could choose to answer, and when he actually asked the question the rehearsing went out of the window.

"Who is at the top of the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities."

Pell Vey stopped in the aisle with a file under his arm and looked at him, and for a moment Marek thought he was going to say the name of a person, and then understood that the not-being-given was going to be the answer.

"Nobody," said Pell.

"That is not an office."

"It is an office in the way that the Crown Kiln is a clerk," said Pell. "It has a seal, a series, and a run. It has no head, no named officer, and no person who signs the sheets, because the sheets are not signed by a person. They are issued. That is the whole design of it and it is a good design, and I want you to hear me say that before you go and write it down, because the fact that it is a good design is the reason it worked."

"You have signed for it."

"I have signed district standing instructions in nine years. I signed the ninth year's flood leaf for the district of this city in the third year of my time, and I put my name under a line that said *issued under the standing authority of the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities*, and I did not read the rest of it, and I did not ask who the authority was, because a supervisor who asks who authorises a standing instruction is not a supervisor." He shifted the file. "I have taught more than half the clerks in three galleries how to keep a minute book and I have never once, in nineteen years, in any corridor in this building, asked a person where a printed form came from."

"And the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month."

"Is in the drawer with the instructions, under a number, in a run of nine hundred and forty, and there is nobody in this empire who can tell you what office within that office decided in ten days to take a sentence out of a lane and print it, and I would put money on there being no office within it at all. I looked on the sixth, after you asked me to. There is a box for the series and the box has a card in it and the card has a run on it, and there is nothing else in the box, because a box with something else in it would be a record of a decision, and a series is not a record of a decision."

"Then the answer to my third question is that it cannot be answered."

"The answer to your third question," said Pell Vey, "is that there is nobody to name, and you must not write it down as *refused* or *unknown* or *not held*, because every one of those is a thing an office can be asked again about. Write: *the office states that its sheets are issued under standing authority and are not signed by a person, and the applicant is referred to the standing instructions of his own district, which are the authority.* Then he will have a thing that is true, that he cannot easily unask, and that will be read by every clerk who ever opens that bay."

Marek wrote it down exactly.

"That is two things you have just done for me."

"I have done one thing for you and one for this building," said Pell Vey, "and they are not the same, and you may notice the difference, and I would rather you did not make a speech about it in a gallery."

---

He handed the reply up on the ninth, and it took four days to get signed, and each of the four was a different person in the building not being the man who could sign it.

The reply was about a district court's docket and about a form series in a corridor in this building, and a junior clerk who signed a reply about whether a sentence was law would be signing a legal opinion, and Marek had a line in his own notes from his first week in the gallery that said, in his own hand, *a junior clerk may record, summarise, and send, and may not decide.*

So it went to Pell, because the office that holds a series in a corridor is the office that should answer for a reply about it, and if the office did not want the reply then the office could say so in writing and would have to say it in writing, which was a thing that had never once been asked for in nineteen years and was, Marek thought, the entire content of the last two months.

Pell read it twice on the twelfth, made four small corrections, all of them in the direction of taking things out, and signed it on the thirteenth of the twelfth month with *Pell Vey, Notaries' Table* under his own hand, which was the first time in two months that his name had been on anything in this matter at all.

"You understand what you have done," Pell said, giving it back. "There is my name. Not yours. It is a reply that says a young clerk refused a thing an office offered, and an office put him in a position where he had to. In four years somebody will read this file and ask me why I let a junior clerk answer for my office, and I will have to say that he wrote a better letter than I would have written."

"That is not a bad reason to be on it."

"It is a spectacular one," said Pell Vey. "That is the difference, and I have been counting on somebody noticing it for two months and being annoyed."

---

On the morning of the thirteenth he posted the letter to Magistrate Rell, which he had carried in the inside of his coat since the eighth of the eleventh month.

It went into the district bag with the morning post, and it was four paragraphs and it was accurate and it said that a sentence stated in open court in Mosswake on the twelfth of the ninth month had been set in type as approved model wording in a printed sheet of another series, with a run of nine hundred and forty, on the authority of a standing office, distributed to every district court and landing office holding a flood docket, and that the magistrate was entitled to know that a sentence of her court was in print in nine hundred and forty copies, four of which were in a lane in her own province.

He put it in the bag and tied it and did not tell Tamsin, and then he went up to the alcove and sat down on the end of her bench, because he had a thing to tell her and he had wanted to write it down first.

Pell Vey had asked him for the corridor conversation.

---

He did not tell her. That is the thing that has to be put in the record, because everything else in two months has been about a man who could not get a sentence out of his mouth and a man who put a draft in a drawer, and this is the last of that and it is the smallest and it was chosen.

Pell had asked at the fourth hour on the twelfth: the offer had been handed to Trost in a corridor on the ninth of the eleventh, in about ninety words, by a person who is not the head of the office, with no witness, and no minute of it was anywhere, and Trost had written his own note on the back of a sheet and had not filed it.

Pell's name was on the reply now. And a supervisor who has just signed a reply saying his office is trying to protect forty-six districts ought to know what his office said, and one person in Auremar had it, and it was an examiner with a cold who had handed it over in a room and had asked to have it noted that he was not the man who offers it.

And Marek sat on the end of a bench and worked out, in about nine seconds, that if Tamsin Rook knew there was an unsigned instruction in this file she would do something about it, and that what she would do would be right, and that a right thing done by a copyist with no standing in the fourth month about a document no office has admitted exists would end everything he had spent two days building, because the whole value of the refusal he has just signed is that it is in the open, in writing, with a name at the foot of it.

So he decided to look into it himself, and he had got as far as deciding when Tamsin Rook came back into the alcove with her sleeves pushed up, put her book down on the frame, and said:

"I have had a question about your supervisor since the fourth of the tenth month and I have not asked it, and I am not going to let you be the one who decides when I ask it, and you can be angry with me in about an hour, but you are going to hear it now, and the reason I can say it now is that a letter went out of this building to a magistrate this morning and there is no version of this where either of us is standing outside it any more."

Marek said nothing.

"He came and asked me in the yard how many of thirteen protected people I had the form with, and I gave him a counting answer, and then he said a true thing and a frightening thing. He said he did not know the form existed, found out three weeks late from a boy with a tray, and would not have acted on it in the eighth month. And then he said that a form with a run on it is a form about a business, and that a business which prints four hundred things for four hundred courts has somebody in it whose job it is to know how many of the four hundred have complied, and that that person will one day have to answer in a room like the one thirteen people are sitting in, and will want to know which thirteen names are with a copyist."

"And you wrote it down."

"I wrote all of it down, in my book, on the fourth of the tenth, in full, with the hour and the place. And I put a line under it." She turned the book round, and there it was, in a fair hand, dated four days after a conversation in a yard: *He says he did not know and did not act. That is one sentence too many for a man who had not read it. Ask him one day, not today.*

"I did not tell you," said Tamsin Rook, "not because I was keeping something from you, which is what I have been telling myself for a month and which is not true, and because I wrote the sentence under it and I have not asked the question. A man who has not read a document says *I did not know and I did not act*, and he does not say it about a document nobody knew about. He says it about a document somebody in his own building had a copy of. Ask him one day. I have had a month. I have not asked him, because I could not work out what I would do with the answer, and because I did not want to hand the thirteen names over, and because I did not want to be the person who made a supervisor frightened of a lane."

Marek Kest sat on the end of a bench in the copying alcove of the third gallery of the Roll Hall and discovered, with the clarity of a man stepping onto a floor in the dark, that he had been doing exactly the same thing in a different direction for nine days, and that he was about to do it for about four more minutes.

"I have to tell you something," he said, "and I have to tell you that I have been not telling you for nine days, and I would like you to have the first fact before the second one."

She put her hand flat on the book.

"Pell asked me on the twelfth for the corridor conversation," he said. "The ninety words in the corridor on the ninth, the ones that came from this building and are not in the file. He has signed the reply this morning. He is entitled to know what his own office said. I have decided not to bring it to you."

"Because you have started a procedure this week that is the opposite of a procedure with two people in it."

"Because the day you know there is an unsigned instruction in that file you will do something about it, and what you will do about it will be right, and it will be done by a roll-keeper of twenty-four in the fourth month about a document that no office in this building has admitted exists, and it will be the end of the refusal that is on the public record with Pell Vey's name at the foot of it."

"Then we are agreed," said Tamsin Rook.

"We are not agreed about anything. We are agreed on a rule."

"Say the rule, and then say your rule for it, because I have had two months of your rules and I would like to know which of them is a rule and which is a man."

"Neither of us moves it," Marek said. "Not you, not me, not down a corridor. If it comes out of that building it comes out of Examinations, in writing, with a name on it, or it does not come out at all. And I am not writing to Trost this week, because if I ask a man with a cold for an unsigned note I have stopped being a person who answers things in the open and become a man collecting pieces of paper in a drawer, and there are enough of those in this case already."

"That is a good rule."

"It is my rule, and I have made it, and I have kept something from you for nine days, and you are allowed to be angry with me now and not in an hour, and I am going to ask you for one thing in exchange and it is a small one. Do not put the second document in the reply."

"The First Seat's quarterly return."

"It is not connected to the circular by anything on this earth, and if it goes out in the same packet it will be read as one thing, and then the circular looks like a dragon's paperwork, and the circular is a Chancery standing office's, and the whole answer we have found about it in two months comes apart because we put two half-truths in one envelope to be clever on a Tuesday."

"It went to forty-six districts," said Tamsin.

"It did. And one of the forty-six is a place we do not know the name of, and the only two facts in this empire that anybody in nine provinces does not know are on the same piece of paper and cannot be separated by a reader." He put his hand flat on the frame. "I know that is the worst reason in the world. I have written it in my book and read it back and it is still the worst reason in the world. But the second document answers nothing in the reply, and the moment it looks like an answer it stops being a fact and becomes a claim, and nobody can check a claim in a month."

Tamsin Rook wrote for about a minute.

Then she tore the leaf out, folded it once, and put it into his coat's inside pocket, on top of the empty place where a letter to a magistrate had been lying for a month, and said, "I am not agreeing with that. I am putting it where I put things I want to be able to find again, and you will have to look at it before you write anything else about a dragon seat's paperwork for the rest of this year, and on the day you do not agree with me you will owe me the argument in a room, in words, with a date on it, and not a letter in a coat."

"I agreed," said Marek Kest.

"You *agreed*. I am writing that down and I am going to check it in a year."

---

The packet left Auremar on the fourteenth of the twelfth on the ordinary distribution, and the ordinary distribution is the list of district courts and landing offices holding a flood docket, which is the same list the circular went to, and it went because of a paragraph Nevin Trost of Examinations had written in his own hand and signed, of which the whole was this:

> *A certification was offered by this office on the ninth of the eleventh month and was declined by the applicant on the twentieth. The terms of the offer are as set out in the enclosed reply. This office is under an obligation to state them to every district within the distribution, and does so. Districts are not asked to accept the offer, which is withdrawn. Districts are asked to note what is enclosed and to preserve endorsements in the model form pending an answer to the questions in it.*

Forty-six packets, and the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth inside each of them, and the first of them went down the river on the packet boat and the rest went by the coach road and two of them went out of the city on the night mail with a clerk who did not know what he was carrying.

One of the forty-six was a landing office at a place called Millbrook Reach, three hundred miles down the water, where a woman called Iselle Oster had written to a trestle table in a lane to ask for a copy of a form, and had said in the same letter that a gauge had gone on a bond in the first week of the month, which was nine days before the packet that told her district that anybody in nine provinces had refused anything at all arrived on a barrow behind a boy.
