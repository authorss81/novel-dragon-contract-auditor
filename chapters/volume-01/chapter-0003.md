# Chapter 3: The Clerk's Window

The Mosswake Roll Court is a low building with a stone roof and a clerk's window that faces the flood terrace, so that anyone waiting to be heard can stand in the open air and be seen waiting, which was either an accident of architecture or the most deliberate piece of civic engineering Marek had ever encountered. Tamsin Rook had been in the copying room for forty minutes that morning and had already formed the view that it was deliberate, and that the court had chosen it for the same reason a good lockmaker chooses a key: not to keep people out, but to make being kept out expensive.

The copying room smelled of wet stone, lamp oil, and the particular scorched sweetness of seal-wax from the magistrate's room next door. There were six copyists in it, all of them doing flood-season work, all of them copying lists of households. The lists went to the district for winter relief. Nobody enjoyed the work and everybody did it fast.

"Copyist Rook." Ivet Sarn came through the doorway without hurry. "Your clerk is on the field. You have the copying pass and no business in the register, and I would like to keep it that way until he comes back and asks in person."

"He asked in person. Last night, at the office, and I was there."

"Last night he asked for a Venn comparison. A Venn comparison is not a Dunn comparison."

"No. It isn't."

Sarn was thirty-eight and had been the clerk of this court for nine years, and she had the specific tiredness of a person who has spent nine years being the person who says no while a river rises. She set a cup down on the ledger stack, which was either a habit or a message.

"Tell me what you want and I will tell you what you will get, and then you can go and be useful somewhere else, because I have four hundred and sixty relief rolls to certify before the fourteenth and the court sits on the fifteenth."

"The Venn correction, as it was entered on your docket. The authority it was filed under, and the date it was received."

Sarn looked at her for a moment and then went to a shelf and came back with a flat wooden case and opened it.

"The eighth month," she said. "Received on the ninth of the eighth, from the district water office at Reed Cut, under flood authority. It went onto the docket the same day. It was not argued. Corrections under flood authority are not argued, they are entered, because the whole point of a flood authority is that there is no season in which to argue."

"Who entered it?"

"A clerk from the water office brought it in and a clerk here took it in and I initialled the docket, which I have done nine thousand times." She turned the case so Tamsin could see. "There is your authority. It is a memorandum. The district issues those when the season's channels are declared. It does not say anything about the Venn field. It says that continued works on the Reed Cut banks are authorized pending inspection. It is the kind of paper that a hundred corrections are filed under in a year, and it is the kind of paper that has no business altering a private instrument, and I am aware of that, and I entered it anyway, because my job is the docket and the docket was full and the season was in my face."

She put a finger on the memorandum.

"I entered it. If you want to know who wanted it, it was not me."

Tamsin copied the memorandum, the docket line, and the date. She copied them slowly and in full, corners included, because the corner of a page often carries a filing clerk's initial and an initial is a person.

"That," Sarn said, "is all you are getting, and you are getting it because the boy will ask me again and I would rather give him once."

"He will want the file reopened."

"No."

"Ivet—"

"Copyist." Sarn's voice did not rise either. Tamsin had noticed that people in the river country all had the same trick. "In the eighth month of the fifth year, a man in the upper district disputed a precedent from a winter two years before. I reopened the file because the man had a copy of the original and the original said what he said it said. That file sat open from the eighth month to the fourth month of the next year, because every time I moved it someone found a reason it needed to sit longer, and the relief rolls for that winter could not be certified while it sat, because the certification oath requires the docket to be closed and settled, and if the docket is open then the court cannot swear that the district's list of need is the district's list of need." She put her thumb and forefinger about two inches apart. "Eleven hundred households ate four months late because a precedent was open. Not because it was wrong. Because it was open. I have a piece of paper about that winter that somebody keeps in a drawer as a caution, and I am the somebody."

"So you don't reopen."

"So I reopen when I can afford to and not otherwise, and I can never afford to, because the district has a flood docket every single year of my life." She took her cup off the ledger stack. "That is my reason. Write it down with the date. I will not initial it, because an initialled refusal is a thing that can be appealed, and I have thought about that longer than you have been in this room."

Tamsin wrote it down. She wrote the date, the time, the docket number, the name of the court, the two other people in the room — a lamp-trimmer and a man asleep over the relief rolls — and the words as Sarn had said them, including the two inches. Then she put the page face down on the board and left it where Sarn could see it and did not push it.

"Clerk."

Tamsin looked up.

"I am going to tell you a number, because you will find it out inside a week and I would rather you had it from a person." Sarn had not moved away from the window. "It took me four days to open that file. He asked me and I said I would look at it, and I looked at it for four days looking for a way not to, and on the fourth day the docket was still shut and the relief rolls could not be certified, and by the time the file was open it was the wrong month to be sorry in."

"Whose file was it?"

"That is not the question you came in with."

Tamsin wrote four days on the board under the date, and did not ask again, and understood while writing it that a refusal nobody could find was only a thing Sarn had done to herself.

"You have not asked the one question you came in with," Sarn said.

"I've got two left. The register of what was received today, and the Dunn file."

"The register of what was received today is public, because anybody may be served out of it and I have to be able to say who has been served. It is over there. Take what you like; the tray is open until the three o'clock bell." Sarn paused. "The Dunn file is closed. It was certified in the spring and it is a good file with a clean instrument in it and there is nothing in it I have any reason to be ashamed of. Which is exactly why I'm not going to open it for a clerk who has a bad morning and a worse theory."

Tamsin went to the received tray.

It was a shallow oak tray, and it held about forty leaves, and it was the most useful object in the building. Anything a party brought in landed there, and everything a party brought in wanted very much to be filed, and a copyist's daily index of that tray was one of the standard afternoon jobs, done four times a week by whoever was nearest and lowest in the room. Tamsin had done it on her first day. She took the tray to the board and began to write the index, because that was what she was there for, and because it was the fastest legitimate route to the answer she already suspected.

She got to the eleventh leaf and stopped writing for a moment, and then went on writing, and finished the index, because an index that stops in the middle is an index that announces itself.

*Received, fourth month, eleventh day: Dunn seed-barn, upper lane. Barn term, summary form, four lines. Unfiled.*

*Received, eighth month, ninth day: Venn, lower field, Reed Cut. Correction, flood authority. Filed.*

Same four words. Not a resemblance. The same four words, in the same order, in the same third line of a four-line summary, in two packets from two different families in two different months of the same year.

And the Venn summary that was riding in Marek's inside coat instead of in the packet was a third instance, and it was the one that had a third witness notch on it, and it was the one that would go down the river in a courier's satchel in the morning if a supervisor at the Notaries' Table ever put his hand to the slip that was at this moment folded in the same coat, unsigned, with a good clerk's signature at the foot of it and nobody else's.

Tamsin finished the index, and entered the tray, and then, because the tray was open and the index was public and the leaf was in her hands and no rule on the wall forbade it, she copied the Dunn summary in full into her own book, in her own hand, with its date and its receipt number and the name of the clerk who had taken it in, and then she put the leaf back in the tray in the position it had been in, and squared the edges.

The Dunn summary read:

> *Orra Dunn, of the upper seed barn, holds the barn term for the term, the term being the drying floor, the racks, and the winter seed. Barn term confirmed at the spring renewal. It is recorded that where the present holder is unable of the winter seed, the present holder's surety answers, the surety being named below as Nessa Dunn, minor, of the same house, who signs under hand. Certified to Mosswake Roll Court upon renewal.*

The clerk's window was at the far end of the room, and it was open, and there were two people standing at it.

The older one was thirty-six and had a seed barn's hands and was holding a paper she did not appear to have wanted to be holding. The younger one was fifteen and had a shawl over her shoulders in a season that did not need one, and was looking into the window with the fixed attention of somebody waiting to be told something they already know.

Tamsin put the index on the board and went over to the window.

"I'm the copyist assigned to the Venn file," she said. "I'm not from the court and I'm not here about your barn. My name is Tamsin Rook and I'm the person who made the index of what's been received today, and your summary is in it, so you may as well have the number of it from me rather than from the tray."

The older woman looked at her. "Orra Dunn."

"I wanted to ask your daughter something, and I'll ask it in front of you and in front of nobody else." Tamsin did not look at the paper. "Nessa. What have you signed?"

Nessa Dunn's chin came up about a quarter of an inch, which is a very great deal for a person of fifteen who has been told something terrible and is holding it in place.

"Nothing."

"Anything at all. A mark at the bottom of a paper. Your name written in a book. Your name said out loud and written down by somebody else."

"Nothing. I was at school until the fifth month. I'm not even a party to the barn, I'm in the house, my mother does the seed and I do the sacks."

"Nessa," Orra said.

"I know what I'm being told. The man at the water gate said it like a fact. He said the barn term has to have somebody on it past the season and I'm the only one in the house, and I said I hadn't signed anything, and he said that's how it works now."

Tamsin felt something go cold and tidy in the middle of her chest, the way a line goes straight when a clerk has ruled it. It was the most familiar feeling she had. It was the feeling of a page that is exactly what it was copied from and exactly as wrong as its source.

"Nessa," she said. "Your mother is the only signer on the original instrument. I've read it. The renewal form came to this court in the fourth month and it was never filed, and it names you as surety, and no one has come to this window and asked you anything. When did you first hear the word *debt*?"

"Michaelmas. At the gate."

"Michaelmas was in the ninth month."

"Then before. Before Michaelmas." The girl pulled the shawl tighter. "All through the summer. My aunt said it would come to me the way it came to her brother, and I said I'd heard the Venn girl had it too, and my aunt said, well, there's the two of you then."

Tamsin did not look at Orra Dunn. She looked at the tray, at the forty leaves, and thought about the fact that somewhere in this district there were probably eight more households where a man at a gate had said *well, there's the two of you then*, and that nobody had written any of it down, and that the reason nobody had written any of it down was that the children had nothing to hold and the mothers had everything to lose by holding it.

"Mrs Dunn," she said, "I'm going to tell you two things and you can do as you like with them. First: the paper naming your daughter has not been filed, and I am not going to pretend that makes it safe, and I know better than to tell a woman with a barn that a thing in a tray is not a thing. Second: the day that paper is filed, the only way it touches your daughter is through a hearing in a room she is in. That is a rule and not a kindness, and rules can be broken by people who are paid, which is why I have copied it and it is in my book and not in the court's."

"Copied it," Orra Dunn said. "In a book."

"Under the date and the receipt number and the name of the clerk who took it in. So that whoever wants it later has to say they took it off a copy that already existed."

"Which is you."

"Which is me." Tamsin squared the board. "I am not a court and I cannot hear anything. If you want a hearing before that paper is used, you have to ask this court for it, and this court will say what it will say."

"I know what this court will say." Orra Dunn folded the paper in half, which told Tamsin she had brought it to be shown to somebody and had not expected to be. "Nine years I've been bringing things to this window. Four of them I've been asked to take away again."

"Ivet Sarn is honest," Tamsin said. "You will not get a kind answer out of her. You might get an early one."

That was the whole conversation. It lasted perhaps nine minutes. When it was over Tamsin went back to the copying room and finished the index and took her copy of the Sarn refusal to the board and pinned it up on the peg where the day's tallies went, which is a public thing and is meant to be a public thing, and then she wrote at the foot of it, in the space left, the date and the time and the two inches and the eleven hundred households, because a refusal is only a refusal if somebody has written down what it was refusing.

Then she sat down and looked at her own hand for a while.

Three weeks in the alcove, copying other people's evidence, and she had thought the work was a kind of honest drudgery. It was not drudgery. It was the whole of it. Everything that had ever been proved in the Empire had been proved because somebody in a dull room wrote down the date on a leaf, in their own hand, with the corner initials, and then could say, when the year turned and the memory turned and the House came, *that is not what I was told, and here is when I was told it, and here is who else heard.*

She had thought the Venn file was an assignment. A junior clerk with a theory and a boat, going north to look at a field. She had come to the court to get an index of a tray.

Marek came up the terrace at dusk with mud to the knee and a strip of paper in his inside coat, and found her in the doorway with two books under her arm and a face that made him stop walking.

"Sarn won't reopen it," Tamsin said. "You have that. Written, dated, witnessed, and pinned up where the tallies go, so she cannot now say I asked her a casual question."

"Thank you."

"That isn't the half of it." She held up the second book. "The half of it is in here, and it is dated the fourth month, and your Venn correction was filed in the eighth, and the four lines are the same four lines, and the Dunn one has a child's name in the surety line, and the child is fifteen, and she has signed nothing, and by Michaelmas half the lane had told her she was going to be ruined by it."

Marek took the book. He did not open it. He stood on the flood terrace with the river going brown below the wall and the wind coming up off it, and he looked at Tamsin for slightly too long.

"They'll close the Venn packet tomorrow," he said. "I have sent the packet back down the river with the morning courier, because a packet that sits in a district tray over a flood weekend is a packet the Table will certify on Monday out of habit, and a summary with a copied notch in it is exactly the sort of thing that a busy man calls routine. The slip I wrote Pell about the summary is in my coat, unsigned, and it will stay in my coat until he puts his hand to it. We cannot get to Auremar tonight and open the cabinet and compare both files and still have anything to file before the board sits tomorrow morning."

"Then we go tonight," Tamsin said. "And you will tell me the rest of it, in the boat, in order, including the parts you have been keeping in your coat."
