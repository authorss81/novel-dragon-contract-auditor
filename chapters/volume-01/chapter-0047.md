# Chapter 47: One Nail and a Shilling a Copy

The record came down the river on the twenty-ninth of the sixth month at about the fourth hour, in a packet boat's leather bag with a Crown Kiln label on it, and it was forty-one pages, and it came in eleven days, which was twice as fast as anything from Auremar had ever come to that window, and Ivet Sarn did not know why and wrote the reason down as *not known* rather than guessing at it in a column.

The six weeks is the ordinary distribution. That is the thing that goes to the forty-six district courts and landing offices that hold a flood docket, and it is the same list the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth went to, and it carries a form of words on the front that says what a record of a decision is, and that it is sent to nobody in particular, and that no court or office receiving this is required to take any step upon it. It has no addressee. It has forty-six destinations and an instruction to nobody, and it will arrive in the last week of the seventh month, and the man in a good coat who came up the quay past the Crown Kiln stair on the seventeenth and asked, in nine minutes, at a counter, will have a copy of it in about a year and will not remember asking.

The forty-one pages were a different thing. The Mosswake Roll Court is a party. A party gets the record.

Sarn took it to the board at about the fifth hour, which is a thing that does not usually happen and which she had decided on the fourth of the sixth month, standing in front of a bare wall with a piece of chalk, when it was four days old and still two hundred and some miles away, and which she had ruled a line for in her own hand and which was therefore already hers before anybody thought of asking her to have it.

The board is three feet high and four feet wide and it holds the register of pending duties at the top and the hearing slate under it, and between the two of them there is nine feet of the trial's accounting, in her own hand, with a magistrate's name at the foot of the third paragraph. There was one space left at the bottom, about eleven inches, and it was under everything, and she put the Cinder Court's record there and wrote a line at the head of it in her own hand.

> *THE CINDER COURT AT AUREMAR. ROOM FOUR. THE SEVENTEENTH OF THE SIXTH MONTH.*
> *By order, and in my own hand, and not because anybody told me to. A copy is a shilling. There are forty-one pages and it is the record of a decision of a high court and it is not a copy of an argument.*
> *Read the first order, the second order and the fourth. The third is about a man's certificates and you will not understand it and it is the shortest.*
> *I. Sarn, clerk of this court.*

Nobody told her to write that and nobody in Auremar will ever read it, because it is a wall at a window in a flood district four hundred and thirty miles from anything, and that is the entire distribution mechanism of the law in this case, and it is one clerk and one nail and a shilling.

A man in a good coat came down the steps at about the sixth hour, having come off the morning packet, and read all three things on that wall before he got to the sill. All three. The register of pending duties, the hearing slate, and the nine feet of accounting, and then forty-one pages of the Cinder Court, in that order, standing up, in about eleven minutes, and then he asked for a copy of the page with the first three orders on it, and paid a shilling for it, and asked whether the district intended to take any step, and was told by a clerk of thirty-eight that the district had not decided whether to run a process next season and that there were forty-one names on the hearing slate and that four of them had been asked and not granted, and that a man in Greyfen might or might not have a lawyer, and that all of that was on the wall.

Then he bought the wall. Which was a shilling.

---

What the district has to do about it is almost nothing, and she wrote that on the wall as well, on the second of the seventh month, because a wall that only says the good news is a notice and this thing is a record.

> *WHAT THIS DISTRICT HAS TO DO ABOUT IT, WHICH IS ALMOST NOTHING.*
>
> *One: the first order of the Cinder Court says that no guarantor duty may attach to any person who has not been heard and has not signed. That is now true here, and it was already true here, because this court said it in a room on the twenty-first of the fifth month, and what changed on the seventeenth of the sixth is that it is true in forty-five other places as well, and this court is glad of it and claims none of it.*
>
> *Two: the second order says the sentence is model wording and is not a decision of any court. It is not withdrawn in this district, because this court is the only body that can withdraw its own practice, and it has not been asked to and has not offered to, and I am not going to write on this wall that a district is bound by a high court it has never been heard by.*
>
> *Three: the process may be run again in this district next season. This district has not decided. It is not decided either way and I have not asked the magistrate and I will not be asked and I will not put my own view on a wall.*
>
> *Four: forty-one names are on the hearing slate. Twenty-two households are on the register of pending duties. Four people have been asked for a hearing and not granted one, and a request that is not granted is on the slate anyway, with a date on it, and that is the whole of what a slate is for.*
>
> *Five: a creditor in Greyfen has a lawyer and has had a paper served on a household in the first month, and the sale of the thing that paper was against is in a book at Coldsfoot and the book is right, and this court has no power over him and did not promise anybody that it had.*

And then the last line, which she wrote at about the fourth hour on the third of the seventh, with the nail still in the board and the hammer on the sill, and which is the only sentence in nine years of that wall she has written in the first person about herself.

> *Six: I have not run a window in eleven weeks and I have not opened one, and there is nobody in this building whose job it is to open one, and if you have a paper with a duty on it and you come to this window and ask me, I will read it to you and I will not tell you what to do about it, and I will enter that you came, because the register above my head is a register of duties and does not do that and never will.*

---

Then the copies went out, and the copies are the part of it that nobody designs and everybody is surprised by.

The first sheet out of the building went to the professional copyist on the Greyfen road, who had charged four pence a sheet in the ninth month of last year and had a waiting list in the fifth month of this year, and who took eleven sheets at four pence and charged none of them for the twelfth, and who said, and this is on the sheet because she wrote it herself on the back of it, that she had made six of the twenty-six public copies for nothing in the ninth month of last year and that the free ones were anger and that there is a price.

The next four went to the two men off the Reed Cut bank, who had made four of the twenty-six on the back of a seed tally while arguing about the fourth term, and who still would not give their names, and who made theirs together, and who have now made nine of this, and who still argue.

The next went to a woman in a seed barn who will not give her name and who made five of the twenty-six public copies in the ninth month and the twelfth, and who has never once been written to by post.

The next went to a lamp-trimmer, who can write, and who did not want it, and who took it because a clerk of the Mosswake Roll Court asked him in a room with a witness and did not order him.

The next went three hundred miles down the water to a cooper's shop with the *and Son* painted over and not painted out, and the woman there has a book that says on its first page that nothing in it is evidence and that anybody may read it or write in it, and she put forty-one pages into that book and wrote underneath the heading what the order was in her own words, and her words were *no person may be put in for something they did not hear and did not sign*, which is not what the court said and is the sentence she wanted, and the sentence in her book is the one anybody in that town will read in nine years.

And two of them went four hundred and forty miles to a furnace town, in the same bag as a letter going to a lane with no numbers, because Tamsin Rook in Auremar had by then heard from a lane that a woman off the vats in Greyfen was going up the road, and had written to a copyist to pass on anything that arrived for anybody in a trade, and had not told Marek Kest, and had not been going to.

And a woman in a dyers' lane took the first ten free and charged nothing for them, because that was what she had been doing since the ninth month, and then a man with a tray of forms came down the lane on the fourth of the seventh month and stood at the trestle and said, in a voice that was almost kind, that the dyers' lane was on the ordinary distribution of nothing at all, and that eleven houses had already signed a second instrument, and that copies of a decision by a high court about guarantors were not what anybody in this trade needed, and that she would be making a great many people without the work a good name for herself.

She gave him the eleventh sheet.

"I am not going to pretend to you that I know what I am doing," she said. "I have been on a board for six months and I have never once been asked to stop. And there is a woman in this lane who put a number about my neighbours on that board in chalk with her own name under it, and I have not agreed with her about one word of it, and I copied her arithmetic out in a fair hand when a man in a good coat came for a schedule, and if I stop now then in a year somebody will say the four questions came from a woman who would only use them while they were useful."

"Take the tray," said the man with the tray.

"I have taken the tray," said the woman in the dyers' lane, "and I have read it out loud to a man in a good coat, and I have given him a copy, and it is the twelfth one I have given away and it is not the first, and you may put that in the road behind you."

---

On the evening of the ninth of the seventh month Ivet Sarn went home up the embankment with the shillings in a cloth, eleven of them, being one pound and one shilling, and she did not spend a penny of it, and she went into her own room and opened the drawer where the paper had been since the fourth of the tenth month.

It was a copy of the entry she had made in the register on the fourth of the tenth month, in her own hand, with the reason and the consequence in the same box: an upper-district barn term file of the fourth month of the fifth year, a household called Sallow, four days, and eleven pounds of seed bought over the price because a docket could not be closed and the relief rolls could not be certified.

She had told them all of it, on the thirtieth of the ninth month, at the top of the upper lane, in her own words, with Marek Kest standing at the bottom for five hours and not permitted up. It was entered. It was on the wall of no court, but it was in the register, and there is a copy of the register, and Rennie Sallow could have come to this window any day since the fourth of the tenth month and bought a shilling's worth of the line about his own house.

He had not, because nobody had told him that he could.

Ivet Sarn took the sheet out of the drawer and put it in the front of the desk, on top of a blotter, and then went to the door of her room and came back and picked it up and put it in her coat, and then took it out of the coat and put it back on the desk, and then did the thing she had been doing for nine years and had never once done on purpose, which was to decide, and be right.

She would take it to him. Not by post, because post to an upper-district household costs fourpence and he has paid a shilling for a line about his own house before and would pay it again and should not have to. And not on a working day, because a clerk who carries papers down a lane in working hours is doing two jobs. And not in front of his wife, because Rennie Sallow has been to the window about the seed twice and the second time he did not want her there, and that is his one piece of standing in this district and it would be taken off him in a second by a court clerk standing on his step.

On the fourteenth of the seventh month, at the seventh hour, on his way home from the works, she would walk up the upper lane and put it in his hand and say four words and go, and the four words would be *from the window, no charge*, and she would not go in, and if he asked her in she would say she had a shilling to take back to a man with a tray.

And in the meantime, on the wall, in the first person, with the day on it, she wrote the thing she had actually arrived at in nine years of being a clerk who is afraid of being the person who runs a thing she cannot hand on.

> *Seven: people ask me whether I would rather be a tidier clerk in a building where a decision stops at the building. I would not. I would rather be frightening and indispensable at a window with a nail in it, in a district of four hundred and sixty households where a man in a good coat has to stand and read everything before he reaches my sill. That is the whole of what I have got and it took nine years and a trial that cost nine shillings and fourpence and four people saying the words *I do not understand this* in a register, and I have nobody to hand it to, and there is a sentence on this wall about it, and the sentence is the only inheritance I have ever been left.*
