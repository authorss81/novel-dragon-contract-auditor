# Chapter 42: In the Name of the Court That Asked

The minute of the twenty-first came down the river on the morning of the twenty-second in a sleeve addressed to the applicant and to nobody else, and it took Marek Kest about four minutes to read and about a second to understand that the district had just paid for something, out loud, with a magistrate's name on it.

> *Mosswake Roll Court, in the matter of the household of the upper seed barn, the twenty-first of the fifth month, at the second hour. The household is heard. The girl is heard. Both are entered.*
>
> *First: this court will not join Nessa Dunn as guarantor. The sentence stated by this court in open court on the twelfth of the ninth month is not a reason in this court by which any person may be joined as guarantor, unless that person has been heard in the room and has signed.*
>
> *Second: the creditor's paper served on the forty quarters of winter rye on the twenty-ninth of the first month was read into this hearing in full, at the fifth hour, in front of the person it names, at the household's request and not at the court's own motion. It is entered as read. It is not affected by anything this court has said and this court has no power over it.*
>
> *Third: this court states, and states now for the first time in writing, that between the third month of the sixth year and the sixth year it applied that sentence in letters and without a hearing to about sixty households in this district, and that the sentence is the reason no household in this district was turned out of a house in the flood of that year, and that this court does not know the names of those households and cannot get them in time, and that the sixty households are not before it and are not represented and have not asked to be.*
>
> *Fourth: this court does not order the district to do anything in place of the sentence, and cannot, and the district will have to work out in the sixth month what it does in a flood when it cannot use it. That is a cost and it is named here so that nobody can say afterwards that it was not known.*
>
> *Fifth: the household asked on the twentieth of the fifth month, at the clerk's window, whether a magistrate may read a creditor's paper into a hearing. The question was written on the board with a line number and the answer is yes, and the answer was given by this court and not by its clerk.*
>
> *Leda Rell, magistrate, Mosswake Roll Court. Entered at the sixth hour of the twenty-first of the fifth month.*

Six pages, and five numbered things, and the third of them was a sentence about sixty people who had not been asked anything and were not in the room.

Marek Kest read the third of them four times and then put the sheet down on the frame of the copying alcove with his hand flat on it, and Tamsin Rook came and read it over his shoulder and read it four times herself and said nothing at all, which in four months he had learned was the thing she did when a document had cost somebody something.

---

"A court has just put a number on its own habit," he said, "and admitted the number, and nobody asked it to."

"The number is about sixty and the name of the magistrate is on the bottom." Tamsin did not move the sheet. "And in four months nobody has to guess what the sixth year was, and there is a person in this file who has just told the truth about her own court in a document that goes where documents go. Do you know what that buys her."

"It buys her being cross-examined about it in a year by somebody who is a cleverer lawyer than the one in that room today."

"It buys her being believed by whoever reads it in nine years," said Tamsin. "You have spent four months asking for a name at the foot of an answer. She has just put one under a sentence nobody made her put there. Those are not the same act and only one of them is free."

He wrote that down. Then he said, "I am going to the Cinder Court."

---

The problem was not the court. The problem was the shape.

A referral is not an audit and the Cinder Court will not hear one, and a request in the Chancery's own form is an application, and an application made by a clerk is a thing a court may grant, refuse, or hold, and every one of those three is a thing a court can do to a junior clerk of the Inheritance Desk without ever touching the series in the corridor behind the third gallery.

What a high court can be given is not a proof. What it can be given is a **record with named people in it who have already sworn things and cannot be got to change them.**

The Mosswake file had that now, and it had it in a way it had not had in the fourth month, and Marek sat in the alcove on the twenty-second with nine things written out in a list and counted them and found that he had six, not nine, and that the six were: a sworn statement in scale form with a hole in it; a review that compelled nothing and created no precedent and had a magistrate's name under the worst paragraph of it; a decision on the scope of his own request that is true and is signed by nobody; a paper read into a hearing in the fifth month because a household asked for it; a ruling of five terms that is still read aloud in a lane by number; and a district court's own written admission that it has done something sixty times without hearing anybody.

That is not a case. It is a set of facts that cannot be recalled.

He wrote the referral in a plain form with no series and no number, in his own hand, and it took him two days, and the eleventh thing he wrote in it was the eleventh thing he was not asking for.

> *A referral to the Cinder Court at Auremar, brought on the record of this court as stated at the third of the fourth month, and on the decision of this court of the twenty-first of the fifth month, and not on any application of the applicant.*
>
> *The applicant is a party in this referral and is not a claimant in it, and asks for no relief in it, and has no interest in it beyond the fact that he is a clerk of the Inheritance Desk and can be got at.*
>
> *The record contains: a source leaf, produced in open court on the twelfth of the ninth month, out of the cabinet of the Notaries' Table, on the direction of a magistrate and not on the request of any person, which is held in that cabinet and has not moved since and is not asked for; a filed correction received by the district on the ninth of the eighth month, which substitutes for a named person a word that any person may stand in; the district water office's memorandum of the eighth of the eighth month, which is the schedule and not the authority; a ruling of five terms; a limited sitting of the third of the fourth month at which a party to the instrument swore a statement in a form made once, of which the second box is blank and is entered as complete so, with the reason entered; a review of the fourth of the fourth month that created no precedent and compelled nothing; a decision on the scope of a request, sealed, true, and with no name at the foot of it; a paper read into a hearing on the twenty-first of the fifth month at the request of a household; and a sentence stated by a court on the twelfth of the ninth month and printed as approved model wording in the amendment sheet of the twenty-second of the same month, of the series MC/FS, issued by the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities, base issue of the second month of this year, run five hundred and sixty, amendment sheet run nine hundred and forty, distributed to all district courts and landing offices holding a flood docket and filed with a district's standing instructions and not with its papers.*
>
> *That sheet is named, and it is not produced, and it is not asked for, and it is not before the court this is referred to. The referring court asks what the recorded reason may be. It does not ask what the sheet is, and it does not ask the court to touch it.*
>
> *The referring court has asked, in writing, four offices and one district water office, over four months, who authorised the instruction sealed with the device which stands on the Venn file, and its own court has recorded on the third of the fourth month, with a magistrate's name at the foot of it, that it cannot find out. That sentence is in the record. This referral carries it and does not ask the court to supply the answer.*
>
> *The referral asks the court to say, on the record: whether a sentence stated in open court in one district, and issued as approved model wording by a standing office in a numbered run, may be the recorded reason for a decision in any other district without a hearing of the person it names.*
>
> *The nine paragraphs filed on the eighteenth of the ninth month and the two-page supplement of the eighth of the eleventh month are not before this court and are not relied on. A request filed at this court on the ninth of the fourth month over the signature of a roll-keeper, naming a district, is not before this court and is not relied on.*
>
> *The eleven things this referral does not ask for are these. It does not ask the court to strike, void, amend or suspend the correction. It does not ask the court to touch the series, or any standing instruction, or any return. It does not ask the court to say who signed the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth. It does not ask the court to disclose any document. It does not ask the court to reopen the Venn file, which has been heard. It does not ask the court to make the decision in the next matter turn on this one. It does not ask the court to hear a witness. It does not ask the court to say that anybody in any of the five districts did wrong. It does not ask for a penalty, and it does not ask for costs.*
>
> *And it does not ask this court to find a person.*

He read that last line back and did not soften it, and it was the only line in three pages he was actually afraid of, because a referral that does not ask for a name is a referral that cannot be refused for asking in the wrong place, and because a court that has been asked nothing about anybody cannot be accused of having found anything about anybody, and because a document that ends that way is a document that a man in a good coat four hundred miles from Auremar could file a different document against, and Marek had learned in the twentieth of the eleventh month, in a chamber above a chandler's shop in Candlewright Lane, exactly what a man in a good coat files.

---

Pell Vey read it in about eleven minutes, standing, with his hand flat on the corner of the desk, and he did not argue about a single word of the substance, which took Marek by surprise and did not entirely please him.

"I am not going to argue about the merits of a thing this court has not read," said Pell. "I am going to argue about the caption, and I am going to win, and then you are going to go and do what I tell you, and I would like you to notice that I have not pretended to want you to notice it."

"Say it."

"Read the first line again."

"*A referral to the Cinder Court at Auremar, brought on the record of this court.*"

"By whom." Pell put it down. "There is no by whom. That is the defect and it is fatal, and if you take it up the river as it stands the registrar will read the caption and find that a document has arrived in the building from the third gallery of a Chancery, written in the hand of a junior clerk, and it will be a document about the clerk. It will go into a file with a clerk's name at the top of it, and in four years a man reviewing it will say that a clerk had a view about a series, and the series will be a series and the clerk will be a man who was twenty-nine."

Marek Kest stood in the third gallery of the Roll Hall and found the whole of the seventh month in about nine seconds, and the whole of the seventh month was a man in a good coat offering four documents back out of an inventory free and unsigned, and a drawer in a corridor, and a yard behind the Chancery, and a girl of fifteen who had said *my name was the last one on it and the last one pays*.

"The Mosswake Roll Court made a decision on the twenty-first," he said. "Rell put her name under sixty households. The referral goes up on the record of that."

"Yes."

"And the applicant is a party."

"And the applicant is a party," said Pell Vey, "and not a claimant, and in the caption, and the caption will say so, and the court that brings the referral is the court that recorded the limits, and not the person who asked for them." He picked the sheet up and turned it round. "Here. *Upon the record of the Mosswake Roll Court as stated at the third of the fourth month, and upon its decision of the twenty-first of the fifth month, the Mosswake Roll Court refers to the Cinder Court at Auremar* — and then your name goes in, in the line under it, as a party, and not at the top, and not in the title, and not as an applicant, because an applicant is a man asking for something and this is not a man asking for something. This is a court telling a higher court that it has hit the edge of its own competence, which is a thing courts are supposed to do, and which this court has now done on the record with its magistrate's name on it."

"You want me to be a party's name in a caption instead of the document's author."

"I want your name to be the second line instead of the first, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a matter and a campaign, and a campaign is what a man in a good coat files against you with." Pell Vey put the pen down. "And I am going to sign it, which is the fourth document in five months with my name on it in a matter I do not control, and I have said the arithmetic out loud to myself in the last fortnight and I have not found a way to make it come out lower, and I would like you to notice that I have written down that you did not ask me to."

"I did not ask you to."

"You never ask me to. That is your whole method and it is why I have been able to use it." He turned the sheet back. "Write the caption I have given you and bring it to me and I will sign it, and then take it down to Mosswake and get the magistrate's clerk to certify the record it refers to, because a referral that comes up the river with a provincial court's record in it and no certification of that record is a referral with a hole in it that a registrar will find in about nine seconds and enjoy."

---

It went down on the twenty-fourth of the fifth month on the morning packet, certified at the Mosswake clerk's window at a shilling and twopence, which Orra Dunn did not pay and which nobody in that building ever mentioned, and the packet boat took it up and the man on the quay took it in his book and did not look up, because he had learned in the ninth month of the ninth year that a decision had reached a person he had never met and that the customs trade does not ask.

That left Tamsin Rook in a copying alcove at about the fifth hour of the twenty-fourth with a stove going and a ledger open, and with a thing she had been carrying since the twenty-sixth of the second month.

"You said three months."

"I did say three months, and it has been about three months, and I have been counting." She did not stop copying, and she had not stopped copying for four months. "There are four envelopes in a drawer eleven feet from where you are standing. They are addressed to the lane in Emberwatch, which has no numbers, and the fourth house past the weigh-house, and I wrote them in the fourth week of the first month and I have not sent one of them, and I told you on the twenty-sixth of the second that there was a thing in the drawer I had not told you about and that I was going to tell you in about three months, and I did, and I have not moved the envelopes, and I want to say both halves of that in the same breath so that you cannot use one of them to make the other one smaller."

"Both halves are in the same breath."

"I wrote them and I did not send them because the last person who wrote to that house about an instrument was a man from a factor's office and what came back was a form with a thumb on a line." Tamsin turned a page and did not look up, which was how she told him the things that mattered. "And a woman in a lane says a thing is hers, and a stranger comes to her door about it, and the stranger is a fact in her life for ever. I have that on a card in a book in a case off a Kiln road. I watched it happen to a woman in Glasswater and it cost her her husband's supper."

"You could go through the assessor."

"I can, and I have, and the assessor is a man of about sixty who has been in that room eleven years and who answered me one question in four lines and told me the cost of the next step before I asked him twice, and I have a name and an address and his hours are the second bell to the sixth." She squared the ledger. "The reason the envelopes are in a drawer is not that I could not get them there. The reason is that in the fourth week of the first month, before the rider, before the name, before any of it, I had four envelopes in my hand and I was frightened, and I have spent four months not being a person who is frightened of a letter."

Marek Kest sat down on the end of her bench, which was a thing he had done in this alcove about forty times and had never once done without a piece of paper in his hand.

"You have told me now."

"I have told you now, and I have not asked you for anything, and I want that in the room." Tamsin Rook put the pen in the stand. "The rule we made in the twelfth month says that either of us may put a choice in writing with a date on it and the other must answer in a room, in words, with a date. I am going to write one and I am going to hand it to you and you are going to answer it, and it is not about the drawer. It is about what I do with four letters in the next ten days, and I will tell you what I have decided and you will tell me what you decided, and if you disagree with me you will say so in words tonight, because I am not going to spend the summer finding out in a corridor in the eighth month."

"Send them."

"That is my decision and it is not a good one, and I will tell you why, and then you may disagree." She took a leaf out of the pile. "Of the four envelopes, three are addressed to the lane in Emberwatch, which has no numbers, and the fourth is addressed to the standing assessor by name and office. The three go. The fourth does not, and the fourth is the reason, and it is a reason about you and not about me. I will get one answer off that lane or three and I will not get four, and four months from now this book will have a column with a surname and a landmark in it and nothing else, exactly as it has now, and that is worth having. The fourth is the one with a district office on the head of it, and if it goes in the fifth month, in my hand, in a file that has your name on it, then in nine years a person in a room can hold up two sheets and say the roll-keeper wrote to a district about a Chancery clerk, and both halves of that sentence are true and neither of them is a thing I am willing to hand anybody. He answered me one question in four lines in the second month and I have not owed him a second question since. That letter is not a question. It is a fifth thing in a hand, and a fifth thing in a hand in a district is an office, and I have spent eight months learning that the only value my book has is that it has no office on it."

Marek Kest looked at the leaf for a while.

"Three and not four," he said, "and the answer goes in the book and the book does not go to a man in a good coat."

"Three and not four. And you may say the thing you are thinking, which is that this is me deciding what I can be trusted with again, one letter at a time, over six months, and that it is slow."

"It is very slow," he said.

"It is extremely slow," said Tamsin Rook, "and I have been extremely slow for four months, and I would rather be extremely slow with a rule and a date on it than fast in a coat pocket. The fifth of the sixth. I will tell you the day I put them in and I will not tell you what they say."
