# Chapter 22: Nobody Under It

The letter from Reed Cut reached the Roll Hall on the fifteenth of the eleventh month, on the morning post, in a district envelope with a water office's card on it, and it was put on the table in the Received Room on the second floor because the clerk of the intake desk could not find the file it belonged to, and the file it belonged to was the one the young clerk of about twenty-five had marked *Custody: Examinations* a week earlier and had not been able to put anywhere, because the file had left the building and the boy's initials were on the copy.

So the young clerk put the district's arithmetic face up on the table beside a folded sheet of good paper with nothing written on the top of it, and the folded sheet was the certification offer, unsigned, which had been sitting in the room since the ninth of the eleventh because nobody had a drawer for a thing nobody was meant to sign, and then he went out and shut the door, which he had no authority to do and did anyway, and which Marek noticed and did not mention.

Two documents on one table. Forty lines of a district water office's arithmetic and a sentence with no signature on it, put there by a boy with a tray and no sense in him and no reason at all.

Marek read the water office's four columns first, because he was a man who read the paper that was actually in his hands before the paper he was frightened of, and he had got about a page into the second column when he had to put it down and stand at the window for a minute.

*The officer signing this has a certification, and his mother's canal house is the security on the works schedule.*

He had read that sentence four times, in an empty room, over the course of about eleven minutes.

It was not an argument. He went back and checked, twice, the way he checked everything, looking for the hand behind it, and there was no hand behind it. A clerk of a district water office had been asked by nobody to write to the capital, had done his own arithmetic, had put his own house on the paper, had not said what ought to be done, and had written down in the fourth column the cost of the thing that would be done, and had written it in the same column as the money so that the money and the hours were one figure and could not be taken apart by somebody in a warm room who only wanted the money.

And the second column was worse. The second column was a man in a wet district saying out loud, in his own hand, that a single answer got the rolls out on time.

*One answer gets the rolls out on time. This is not an argument about the sentence.*

---

He took it down to the alcove at the eighth hour and put it on the board in front of Tamsin Rook, and she read the whole of it, twice, the second time with her finger on the four columns the way she read everything that came from a court.

"How long have you had it."

"Since the seventh."

"You have had it two hours and you have come down here and not said."

"I have been trying to work out how to say it and I have not managed," Marek said, "which is new, so I would rather you had the fact than the version of me that managed it."

She put her hand flat on the frame.

"This is the first honest document anybody has sent us since the ninth of the ninth," she said. "Read the second column out loud. Exactly as it is written."

He did.

"And that," said Tamsin, "is a district water clerk in a wet room telling you that the thing you have been offered is a convenience, and he has put it in his own handwriting, with his house on it, and he has not said what should be done, and he has cost himself more by writing it than he has cost you."

"Yes."

"Do you know what the hardest sentence in that letter is."

"Which one."

"The last line of the third column," she said. "*That is the whole of the difference between the second column and the first, and it is not a large sum.* He has just said that whether a person gets to speak in a flood season depends on a clerk's afternoon, and he has called it a small sum, and he is right, and that is the sentence I would put on the front of the reply if anybody is going to write a reply."

Marek looked at the board for a while.

"He is not a lawyer," he said. "He cannot say what the sentence is. He cannot say whether it is lawful. He can only say that interrupting a clerk costs a clerk an afternoon, and he is right about that, and it is the only thing anybody in nine provinces has put in writing that a rule like this actually does."

---

He worked out the rest of it over the following three days, and he did it badly, and he did it in the alcove with Tamsin's book open on the frame and the reed in his hand, and what he got at the end of it was not an argument at all.

"What is wrong with the offer," he said, on the Wednesday, "is not the sentence."

"No," said Tamsin. "Say the rest of it."

"The sentence is bad and it is in nine hundred and forty sheets and it has been in a furnace town since the twenty-second of the ninth month and I cannot get any of that undone by the fourth month, and I have not got a single hour that I am going to spend pretending that if I do the right thing in a room in Auremar the sentence will stop travelling."

"Then what is it."

"The review," Marek said.

He got up and put both hands on the frame in front of him, the way he did when he was going to say a thing he had not finished working out and wanted somebody to hold the other end of.

"The offer has a date on it. Six months, and a review in the fourth month. It is the only thing in it that I would keep if I could keep one thing. And the review is not decoration — a review date is the whole difference between a rule and a sentence, and a review date is worth nothing without a named person who is required to do the reviewing, and who is accountable if it does not happen. And the offer has neither. It has a date and no name. In six months somebody will look at this file again and the sentence will still be in nine hundred and forty sheets, and there will be no one in the empire who can be asked why."

"You have just described a better version of the same thing."

"I have described the *review*," Marek said. "Which is a small part of it, and it is the part I can get, and it is the part that answers a question the certification answers and a refusal does not."

Tamsin sat down on the end of his bench.

"Then say the question, because I have been in this building for two hours and I have not heard you say it once, and we have been talking about a document for three days."

Marek looked at the water office's four columns on the board behind her.

"The question the certification answers," he said, "is: who is responsible for what this sentence does to a household in the fourth month? A certified rule says a person. An uncertified sentence says nobody. And a refusal on its own is a sentence with nobody under it, and forty-six clerks in nine provinces will each decide on their own in nine hundred and forty copies' worth of districts, and they are not bad men, and every one of them is going to be right in the way their own district is right, and a woman in one of them is going to be joined as a guarantor by a good man doing his honest best in a hurry."

"So the thing you want," said Tamsin Rook, "is not a rule and not a silence. It is somebody's name."

"It is somebody's name and a date and a form and a witness and a place where the hearings go that anybody can read, and a number on the cost so that when it runs out the thing stops out loud instead of going on being done badly for free."

---

They went through the list of households on the seventeenth, at the seventh hour, with the register of the Mosswake window in front of them because it was the only list either of them had, and it took forty minutes, and by the end of it they were both very quiet.

Marek laid it out in his own hand, four names, and the four faults, and he read them out, and he did it in a flat voice because the alternative was not a voice he wanted to be using in a copying alcove.

"Venns. The term ends in the second month and there is nothing after it decided, and the thing you are proposing covers flood-season continuity filings, and a seven-year water-and-field term that expires is not a flood-season filing. So the process will not touch the only thing that is going to happen to them in the next three months."

"Correct."

"Dunns. Orra's file is a barn term of the third month. It was made in the third month. She has been waiting since the twenty-third of the ninth for a review in the fourth month that a process of this kind would accelerate or ignore, and it will not do either, because the process is for flood-season filings and hers is a barn term and there is no flood season in it."

"Correct."

"Sallows. Rennie Sallow paid eleven pounds over the price for seed that a clerk did not certify in the fifth year, and his file is four years old and shut, and no process with a date in the fourth month in it will ever look at it again."

"Correct."

"Greyfen. Four houses and a levy and an assessor whose own seat is exposed, and none of it is a flood-season filing anywhere, and the moment a process exists that somebody can point at, every person with a guild instrument in their hand is going to try to get it applied to their instrument, and the second will fall over in a week."

Marek put the reed down.

"That is four households, and every one of them is a household we have spent two months on, and the process I have been offered in place of a rule will help precisely none of them, and it will cost them the thing they have been told to rely on, which is that somebody in a room is going to look at it."

Tamsin did not answer for about a minute.

"And you are going to take it," she said.

"I am going to take it."

"Knowing that."

"Knowing that," Marek said, "and knowing that the alternative is the four hundred and sixty relief rolls going out late in the worst month in nine years, which is also true, and is a number I have not got a family attached to, and that is exactly why I am frightened of how easy it is to say."

---

He went to the Received Room on the twentieth of the eleventh month at the second hour of the afternoon, and Nevin Trost was already in it with his coat still on, and the young clerk of the intake desk was there because a refusal could not be taken in a room with one man in it.

Marek put the water office's four columns on the table face up, and then put the unsigned offer on top of them, and did not sit down.

"Mr Trost. I am refusing the certification."

Trost put his pen down and then did not pick it up, which was the first thing he had ever done in Marek's presence that was not procedural.

"Say the reason and I will write it as you say it."

"Two, and I am not going to argue with you about either of them, because you have already written the argument in the file and I have read it. One: a certified rule makes the public-necessity sentence the practice of every district holding a flood docket, including the sixteen that have never heard of it, and I do not have the standing to do that and neither does your office and neither does anybody in this building. Two, and this is the one I have thought about for nine days: the offer has a review date and no name, and a review with nobody in charge of it is not a review, it is a date, and in the fourth month this office will be able to say that a thing was looked at on a day, and it will have been looked at by nobody in particular and it will not have cost anybody their afternoon."

Trost wrote. He wrote for a while, in the small firm hand of a man who has six years of files in him, and then he read it back, and then he did the thing that Dunnet had done in a corridor in Candlewright Lane, which was to make the mouth and the record match.

"Say it again, and this time address it to me, because a refusal in a room has to have a person in it and not an office."

Marek said it again, word for word as it had been read back, and the young clerk of the intake desk wrote the time in the margin in ink and initialled it, because nobody had asked him to and because he was the only other person in the room.

Then Trost did something Marek had not anticipated, which was to take the sheet of good paper, the one with nothing on the top of it, and turn it over, and write at the foot of the back, in the same hand he had written the offer in:

*Offered by this office on the ninth of the eleventh month, in the terms set out on the face, unsigned. Declined by the applicant on the twentieth of the eleventh month, in a room with a witness, at the second hour of the afternoon. Terms of the offer retained in the file.*

He read it back and then he said, "Sign that one. Not the offer. The record of the refusal."

"That is not a thing I know how to do."

"It is a thing you know how to do," said Trost. "You are signing statements that are worse than that one four times a week. The point of it is that in the fourth month nobody in this building can be able to say that you declined to be asked."

And Marek Kest signed the back of a piece of good paper in a warm room with a stove in it, and the offer went back into the folder, and it was unsigned for the rest of its life, and it was in a file in a building in Auremar with a date on it.

---

"Read the four columns," Trost said, when the reed had stopped moving. "I have not read them and I am going to read them, because a man who is refusing an offer on the ground of a review with nobody in it ought to be quite certain that the man he is refusing it from is a fool."

Marek pushed the sheets across and Trost read them, and the reading took four minutes, and the only thing Trost said while he was doing it was "Two hours. He has cost a clerk two hours," and the only thing he said afterwards was:

"He has put the answer to my offer in the fourth column of his own arithmetic, and the fourth column is eleven shillings, and he has told you that the whole of the cash cost of a thing is eleven shillings and the whole of the other cost is a clerk's afternoon, and he has asked you to believe him, and he is right."

"Yes."

"So now you have refused," said Trost, "and I want to tell you what you have done, and then I want you to go and do the other half, and I am not going to help you with it, because the moment I help you with it, this file stops being a request and becomes a project of this office, and then I am a man who has an interest in it, and in six years I have never once wanted to be a man with an interest in a file I have to answer for."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"You have left forty-six district offices with a sentence and no name on it. Not this district. Not Mosswake. All of them. And every one of them will now hear about it, because they cannot help it: my office offered a certification to every district court and landing office holding a flood docket, and the offer has been declined, and it is impossible for this office to say that to forty-five places and not to the forty-sixth, and I have thought about that for two hours and I have not found a way round it and I have not tried very hard, and that is a thing I have put in the file in my own words and signed, and you can read it whenever you want it."

Marek had not thought of that at all, and it sat in him for a moment with the particular weight of a thing that helps him.

"What do you want me to do."

"The other half," said Nevin Trost. "The thing with the name and the date and the form and the witness and the register. If you do that, the refusal is not a vacuum. If you do not do that, then in about five weeks' time a man in Salt Teeth who has never heard of you is going to be alone with a piece of paper in nine hundred and forty copies and he is going to do the best he can, and the best he can will be wrong in a different direction from the best anybody else can, and nobody will ever be able to tell anybody that the thing was refused on purpose."

---

Marek did not write the process. That was the part he had decided on the seventeenth and held to on the twentieth, and it was the hardest thing he had decided in two months, and he wrote down afterwards, in his own book, with the hour, the single sentence he had written about it: *A procedure I write is a procedure that reports to me.*

What he wrote instead was a sheet and a half, in his own hand, headed with the date and the place and the fact that it was a list of things and not a rule, and it went to the Mosswake Roll Court, to the Reed Cut district water office, and to a copyist of the Chancery, and it said that a person in a flood district must be able to ask for a flood-season continuity filing to be heard on its own, and that the ask must be written down, and that the filing must not be put in a column that makes it unreadable, and that the hearing must be entered in a book that anybody may come and read, and that the person who brings the ask must not be the person who witnesses it, and that the cost of running the window must be stated as a sum in advance and that when the sum is spent the window stops and the book says on its face the day it stopped and why, and that the whole thing must be looked at again in the fourth month by a person whose name is written at the top of it.

There was no seal on the sheet and no series and no number and no office at the head of it, because it was not a rule and could not be made into one, and Marek had understood that about a form with a run on it for two months now and had said so out loud in a workroom in Greyfen with a woman in a vat in the next alcove.

He read the last line of it aloud to Nevin Trost before he sealed it, and it was this:

*A list of what a process must contain, made by a junior clerk of the Inheritance Desk with no standing, sent to three people who have some, in the knowledge that the making of the form is not his and the answering for it will be.*

Trost read it back to him twice, and then initialled the second copy, because an examiner's initials on a piece of paper do not cost an examiner anything and are worth a good deal to a copyist in two months' time.

And then Marek went out of the Received Room with an unsigned offer gone back into a folder and a list of things in his coat, and he stood on the stairs with his hand on the rail for longer than he meant to.

There were four households on the list in his own book, and the process he had just agreed to would not help one of them. That was the price and he had named it out loud in a room on the seventeenth and it was going to be the price in the fourth month as well, in front of people, on a docket, with a date.

What he had, instead of the thing he wanted, was a sheet with a date and three recipients, and the truth about it, which he understood in the doorway of the alcove and would not have been able to put in a sentence at the time, was that the only part of it he would be allowed to keep was the part that some person he had never met, in a room he had never been in, at a window he had never stood at, could read and use without ever once knowing his name.
