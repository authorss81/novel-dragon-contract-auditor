# Chapter 11: The Schedule

The packet boat up from Auremar left in the dark and was eleven hours late getting in, and Marek spent most of it on the afterdeck with his back against a cold bollard, sorting nothing, because his hands had started doing the thing they did when a file was wrong — going through the loose papers in his coat by the date they were written rather than the date they were filed — and there was nothing in his coat to sort. He had the court's copy of the ruling in a oilskin, a stamped duplicate of his own nine paragraphs with a young man's initials in the middle of the last page, and four sheets of Tamsin's hand she had given him at the quay with the instruction *for the lane, not for the court.*

His teeth still hurt. The ache had settled behind his left eye and had made a home there, and the taste behind his teeth had gone from lamp-smoke to something flatter and older, like a coin kept in a pocket.

He got down at the Mosswake landing at the fourth hour of the afternoon with the whole town going about behind him in the wind, and walked up through the flats to Reed Cut instead of taking the bank, because the bank was the long way and he wanted to arrive at the gate looking at the water before he looked at anybody.

The water was the problem. It had been coming up the ditch since the eighteenth and by the twentieth it stood a hand's breadth under the lower field's furrows, and the Reed Cut gate was two fingers out of true, which was a thing Tavi Venn had said in a courtroom nine days ago in front of forty people and had been believed by about half of them, because a gate being two fingers out of true is not the sort of thing a room can see.

He could see it. The linkage had dropped its rear stay and the whole gate was riding on the pivot, and when a gust came up the channel the top board swung a full inch on a hinge that ought to have had a keeper on it since the third month.

He stood on the platform a while and looked at it and did not go and tell anybody. He had learned in nine days that looking at a thing carefully and saying it out loud are two different acts and only one of them is free.

---

The district water office was a low building with a stove in the middle of the room and a bench round three sides of it and a board on the wall by the door, and on the board, in Hallis Dren's upright hand, was the season's work as it had stood for eleven weeks: forty lines, no dates, no names.

"You've come up for the works," Dren said. He had not taken his coat off. There was no fire in the stove and the stove was not for the office, it was for the men who came in out of the cold, and it was not lit, and Marek understood that as a fact about the office's week rather than about the weather.

"The fifth term says the bank works and the gate reset, under your own flood authority, before the season advances. That's four days ago. Your board says Monday."

"The board says Monday because Monday is when I have got the crew." Dren turned round and put his thumb on a line. "Mr Albrecht wants Friday. The gate wants yesterday. I have nine men and a boy and I have got four hundred and sixty parcels on the books and one clerk, and the season does not care which of those is which."

"Where's Albrecht?"

"Cutting peat up past the flats with two men of his own, and he is entitled to cut wherever he likes, and if I take him off the peat I have no peat, and if I have no peat then in the fourth month there is no fuel in forty houses, which is not my office and is going to be in my letters."

Marek put his hand flat on the bench and looked at the board. There were forty lines and none of them was Reed Cut, and one of them was the lower ditch, and the lower ditch was Tavi Venn's ditch in every practical sense in the district, because she had cut it since the fourth month and had told the magistrate so in a courtroom.

"You have had the fifth term for eight days," he said. "Has anybody told Albrecht?"

"He has read the court paper. He came down on the eighteenth and asked me a question."

"What question?"

"He asked whether the schedule that names him is the schedule that says he owes it." Dren put his hand flat on the bench on the other side of Marek's, so that the two of them were leaning on the same piece of wood from opposite ends. "And I said no, and he said that if it is not, he will cut the peat anyway and take the consequences, and I said that is the correct answer, and then we both stood there for a while."

"Who else has asked?"

"That," said Dren, "is why I have got nothing on the board, and it is the first sensible thing anybody has done with this ruling since the twelfth."

Marek looked at him properly for the first time since the eighth of the eighth month, when a district water clerk had stood in a river courtroom with his hat in his hands and explained how a general paper shelters a particular one.

"What do you need," he said.

"Not a favour," Dren said. "I need the office to post its own schedule at the gate, in public, under the office's own flood authority, with the memorandum number on it and the date on it and my name on it, and I need it up before the crew starts, because a crew with a schedule works and a crew without one guesses, and what a crew guesses is whose ditch it is."

"You will have it."

"You will not have it from me." Dren took a folded sheet out of the bench and put it on the wood. "This is what I have written. It is the office's authority, it is the memorandum of the eighth of the eighth month, and it is the fifth term of the ruling of the twelfth, and it says the bank works and the reset are the district water office's duty under flood authority and not under the said instrument, and it says the household of Aldren Venn is not required to perform the field-side upkeep for the purposes of that order. It names the crew. It names the days. It says what the work costs out of the reserve and it says who pays for it after."

"Then post it."

"I will post it. What I am telling you is that every word on that sheet is a sentence I am signing as a clerk of this office with a certification, and my mother's canal house is the security on it." He did not put his head down and he did not make a joke of it, and Marek understood that this was the man who had signed a general paper in the eighth month to clear eleven weeks of backlog, and who had thought about that every day since. "When the office does the works under its own authority, the works are the office's. And when the works are the office's, the cost of the works is the office's. And the cost of the works is seed out of the fourth month's reserve, and the fourth month's reserve is nineteen sacks short of what the parcels on this board need, and that nineteen sacks comes back on the rate in the fourth month, and the rate in the fourth month falls on the smallest holdings hardest because the smallest holdings have the least ground to spread it on."

Marek read the sheet. The arithmetic on the second half was in a different ink and had been added later, in a smaller hand, which meant Dren had gone home and come back and added the part he did not like.

"Post that too," he said.

"That is the part I am least proud of," Dren said, "which is the part I most want on a wall."

---

They put it up at the gate at the sixth hour, when the light was going, and it took two men from the office and a length of oiled cord and four nails, and Marek held one end of the cord and did not say anything for twenty minutes, which was the first twenty minutes of quiet he had had since the twelfth.

The sheet went up inside the rail where anyone coming off the platform would have to stop and read it, and the clerk's hands made the first line bigger than the rest so that a person standing on the bank could see that there was a writing at all.

Dren read it out to whoever had gathered, which was about thirty people by then, and he read it the way Rell read things, in the plain words, once, and he read the whole of it including the arithmetic, and the lane listened to the arithmetic the way a lane listens to everything, which is with total silence and no movement at all.

Then the two women from four doors down came up with a pot of soup for Tavi Venn, which they had been doing every evening since the ruling, and one of them said, in the voice of somebody offering a plate, "You'll want to sign it, love. When they bring it. You'll want to sign it straight away and get it settled, because that's how you keep it."

"I beg your pardon," Marek said, and got as far as that, and stopped.

He had been in the lane for eight hours and he had not eaten. He understood, standing there with his mouth open and a headache behind his left eye, that there were two sentences available to him. One of them was the sentence a man says when a woman in a lane has just told a frightened girl to sign her name to a duty, and it was a good sentence, he had it, and it would have sounded like a man who had won a case.

The other sentence was the one about the guarantor, and the one about the guarantor did not come.

He stood there for about two seconds. The two women looked at him, and then at the sheet on the rail, and then at each other, and something in the lane went very slightly quiet in a way it had not been quiet before, because a man who is a public person has just failed to say a thing in front of thirty people, and thirty people can count.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I have to write to somebody about a thing I can't say. You should take that as the answer and not as the clerk being strange."

The woman with the pot said, "Well," and gave Tavi the pot anyway.

Tavi Venn was at the platform with the bar in her hand, and she had heard all of it, and when the lane had moved off she said, without any expression at all, "You were going to say something about me to my face."

"Yes."

"What."

"That you have not signed anything and do not have to sign anything, and that if anybody brings you a paper with a duty on it you can send it to a court and a court will hear it before it counts." He put his hand against the rail. "And then I was going to say that nobody in the lane has any standing to tell you what to do with a paper, and I have no standing either, and I have been the person who came down here with the paper on the seventh of the month, so you should not be taking it from me."

"That is the worst sentence anybody has said to me in a year," Tavi said, "and it is the first one that was true."

She turned and looked at the sheet on the rail, and read it, all of it, standing still, in the wind, with the pot of soup going cold in the hands of a woman four doors down who did not go away.

"It is my name," she said. "On the crew."

"It is not on the crew. It is on the ditch."

"They have the same name on them here," Tavi said, "and you know they do, and so do I, and the difference is going to be a piece of paper nailed to a rail in the dark for as long as the oiled cord holds."

"Yes."

"Then it is worth having." She handed him the bar. "You are going to have to go and stand somewhere else now, because I have to be in this ditch at first light and I have told the foreman I will take my bar back at noon, and I would like it to be noticed that I am giving it back."

It was noticed.

---

The crew came at the sixth hour of the morning on the twentieth, and there were nine of them and a boy and a foreman called Wendt Sayer with a wet rope he did not need and would use for something else by ten, and the first thing Sayer did at the gate was read the sheet, twice, and then look at the line where the office had written the order the work was being done in.

"Right," he said. "So it's the office's ditch."

"It's the office's ditch," Dren said. "Under the office's authority. The order says so. If anybody in this lane wants to argue about it they can argue with the sheet, and the sheet is nailed up, and I am the one who nailed it, and I have my mother's house on it."

"Nine men," Sayer said, "and a boy. Linkage first, because if the gate goes the low field's gone and the ditch is the second job and not the first. I'll want the reed-root and the sacking. And I'll want somebody to tell me where the turf comes off, because you can't bank a channel with nothing."

"The turf comes off the field-side boundary strip," Dren said, "which is not the lower field, and the field-side boundary strip is the office's under the fifth term, so the office is taking its own turf off its own ground, and nobody is going to be accused of anything for it."

Tavi was there. She had been there since first light and she was standing on the boundary strip with the bar in her hand and her coat open in the wind, and she was not doing anything, which for a person who has been doing something in that field for five months is a kind of work.

At the seventh hour Sayer put two men on the gate and took three into the ditch and put the boy on the bank with a line, and told Tavi to stand clear, and she stood clear, and the ditch that had been hers for five months became nine men and a boy and a foreman with a wet rope inside a quarter of an hour.

Marek watched it from the bank. He stayed about forty feet away, which is further than he needed to be and closer than he wanted to be.

The gate took until the second hour of the afternoon, and the taking of it was the only part of the two days that looked like anything. Sayer's men got a new keeper onto the hinge and a new pin through the pivot and then had to persuade the whole assembly, which had been sitting wrong since the spring, to come back into line by degrees over about half an hour, with a bar on the linkage and four men on the bar and one man on the platform shouting numbers. The top board came back inside the quarter inch. The lower field drained about two inches by the third hour, which is not much, and which is the difference between a field that can be got in and a field that cannot, and Tavi Venn stood on the bank and watched her own ditch empty and did not say anything, and Marek, who was forty feet away, saw her mouth move once, without a word in it.

Sayer came up the bank at the fourth hour and looked at the sheet on the rail again, which he had read twice at seven and had read a third time by then.

"This is a good sheet," he said, to nobody in particular. "I have worked under a flood memorandum before. I have never once worked under one with a number and a date and a clerk's name at the foot and a line about who is not required. It is going to make some people very unpleasant in about two years, because there is nothing on that rail to argue with."

"You have read it four times."

"Three times properly." He shouldered the rope. "I will tell you what I would do if I were you, clerk. I would get it copied. Not once. Every crew that comes off the water this season, and every man who stands on that platform in the next ten years. There is one rail and one sheet on it, and nails go into a rail."

---

The gratitude was worse than the anger had been. In the fifth month it had been anger, and anger was easy, and anger went at somebody and was finished. This was two women bringing soup and telling a girl to sign, and a foreman reading a sheet twice and a clerk whose mother's house was on it, and a lane that had watched a girl hand a bar back at noon and had immediately begun to say that she was keeping it.

At the ninth hour a woman he did not know put her head over the boundary fence and asked whether it was true that the Venn girl had taken the ditch on.

"Which part of it," Marek said.

"Any of it. I heard she had the whole thing before the court did."

"She cut it," Marek said. "That is what she told the court and it is true and nobody has disputed it. What the paper says is that the office is doing it now, under the court's order, and that she is not required to, and the paper is nailed to the gate and you can read it."

"I've read it. I can't read it that good."

"Neither can most people," Marek said, "and that is not a reason to believe the other thing."

The woman considered this and then said, "It's a shame. She does it well."

And that was the whole of it. That was the shape of the two days. A girl of seventeen who was better at cutting a ditch than nine men and a foreman, who had given her bar back at noon in front of thirty witnesses, and about whom the district had settled on a version that was not a lie and was not true, and that the version would be in four mouths by nightfall and in the seed barns by the end of the week, and that no court in the world could call it back.

That was the price of the fifth term, and it had been named out loud on the evening of the tenth, by the girl, on a step, to a magistrate, before the term existed.

---

At the seventh hour of the evening he walked up the lane to the seed barns, because a thing had been sitting at the back of his mind since the water office, and it was that the twenty-third was three days away and he had not yet told Orra Dunn what a sitting was going to be.

He had assumed, in the ordinary way, that the notice had reached her. The court had listed it in the register in Sarn's hand and Tamsin had read it out to her in a yard on the second day after the ruling, and a date in a clerk's register is a very fine thing until you are the person it is about.

The upper lane above the seed barns was mud to the ankle and the barn door was barred, and there was no light in the barn and no light in the house beside it, and when he knocked, Orra Dunn opened the door with a lamp in her hand and did not put the lamp up and did not step back.

"You have come to tell me about Friday," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you are the fourth person and I have had the same conversation three times and I am going to say the same thing to you that I said to the other three, and then I am going to shut this door, and I would like that understood to be about what I have already said and not about you."

Marek did not say anything.

"A sitting is when the court looks at all of it," Orra Dunn said. "I told the woman who came down the lane with the copies and I told the woman who came down the lane with the notice and I told the man from the seed office. All of it. The barn, the term, the four people I owe, the fifteen-year-old, the creditor who has already had a letter. That is what I get for going to a hearing, and I went, and I have not stopped being glad that I went, and I would like both of those to be allowed at once because that is the state I am in."

"You are right about all of it," Marek said. "That is what a sitting is. I cannot make it smaller and I am not going to pretend to."

"You said that to me on the second day."

"You remember it."

"I wrote it down," she said, "the same night, on the flat of a board, because I have a bad memory for words and a good one for figures, and I have got a figure that I have been doing since Friday." She shifted the lamp so that the light went past him and into the lane, which was a way of saying that the conversation was over but not the door. "Do you know what a creditor can do with a duty that is only pending?"

"Act on it," Marek said.

"Hold security against the barn and name it in a demand and put a number on it, and the number does not have to wait for anybody to decide anything, and I have got a man in Greyfen who has told a seed factor that my floor is coming and that the factor can have it in the second month for less than it is worth now." She shut the door on the words and opened it again on the light. "So when you come down here on Friday to tell me the court is going to help me, you are coming down here to tell me the court is going to help me, and what it is going to do is give a date to the thing I am already afraid of."

"Yes," Marek said. "That is what I think it is going to do. I would rather you heard it from me at a door at seven in the evening than from a clerk's letter on Thursday."

"Then you have come to the right door," she said, "and I am going to tell you the true thing and you will not like it either. If the court gives us a date, the date is a number, and I have got a factor in Greyfen who cannot eat a number."

Marek wrote to Tamsin that night in the drying room at the Venn house, with Mara's wax tablet on the bench beside him and the kettle going, and he wrote four things: that the sheet is up, that the crew has the gate, that the reserve is nineteen sacks short and the rate falls in the fourth month, and that he had tried to say a thing to a woman in the lane and had not been able to say it and did not know whether the not being able was the reading or was him.

Then he added a fifth, because it was true and she would find it out anyway.

*The lane has decided the girl took the ditch. Nobody said it where she could hear. It came out of the two women with the soup, and they said it kindly, and it is going to be in the seed barns by Friday, and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and I have not tried, because trying is how it gets into a record as an opinion of mine.*

He sealed it and put it in the courier's hand for the morning, and the courier took it, and it was the twentieth of the month, and the Dunn sitting was in three days.
