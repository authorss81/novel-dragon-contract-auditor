# Chapter 7: The Order on the Board

The river courtroom at Mosswake held forty people and eleven of them were standing, because the bench seated twenty-two and the lane had sent more than that. The door onto the embankment was propped with a bar and the wind came under it and moved the papers on every desk in the room at once, and the magistrate's clerk had weighted them all with the flat of a bronze rule, which she had done so often that the rule had worn a shine.

Marek came in at a quarter to eight with the file in a leather folder and found Tamsin already at the front, standing beside the clerk's window with her case open, and the whole lane behind her, and the public bench half full of people who had come to find out whether the Venn girls' field was going to be taken.

"Aldren's mother," somebody said. "The old woman, she's ninety."

"Ninety-one," said another voice.

Marek took his place and did not open the folder.

At the top of the room, on the bench, sat Leda Rell with the flood list still in front of her, and beside her the district's own assessor, and behind them the bronze rule holding down everything. At the side, on a bench by itself, sat Pell Vey, who had come out from Auremar on the evening packet, in a good coat, in a district courtroom, which was not a thing a supervisor did for a filing error.

Garren Pyle, who gave his occupation as a certifier and whose name appeared in the fee column of a copy of a court instrument, sat in the second row with a leather case of his own and a certificate rolled inside it.

The clerk read the order.

"Rehearing in the matter of the Venn lower field, Reed Cut. Witnesses in the order entered: for the applicant, the witness to the original instrument, Mara Venn, of the lower field. For the respondent, Garren Pyle, certifier, of the district. For the applicant, Tavi Venn. For the district water office, Hallis Dren. For the water keeper, Sivra Oris."

Marek had read the order on the board at half past six. It had said: *Certificate of the Reed Cut district, entered first, on the ground that the instrument's continuation is a matter of record and the record speaks first.*

He had read it as a piece of nonsense. He now understood that it was not a piece of nonsense, and that this was the problem.

"Magistrate," Tamsin said.

She said it loudly enough to carry, which Marek would not have done, and which turned out to be the only way to speak in a room where the wind was taking the ends off sentences.

"Magistrate, before the applicant opens, I have to ask the court for a pause, and I am asking as the assigned copyist and the maker of the index, and I will hand the court my book."

Rell looked at her. "State it short."

"The witness order on that board was drawn on the ninth and is dated the ninth and is sealed with the clerk's window seal, and it is the third issue of a witness order in this matter." Tamsin put her hand flat on the counter. "The register has two. The first was drawn on the eighth and named the widow first. The second was drawn on the ninth in the morning and named the widow first. There is no third issue in the register, in this building, or anywhere in this district that I have been able to find, and I have looked in the register, and I have the register's own numbering in my hand because I copied the day's docket at noon on the ninth for the relief rolls."

Rell's clerk said, "The window was closed at noon. Flood day."

"The window was closed at noon," Tamsin agreed, "and I was outside it at noon with my case shut, and the board outside the window showed the second issue, with the widow first, and a pin in the second hole, and the second hole is the hole you use when you have taken one down and put one up, which is exactly the practice, and which I have watched happen at this court perhaps forty times this year alone."

"And you are telling me that between noon on the ninth and the posting of the order on this board this morning, somebody drew a third issue of the witness order in this building."

"I am telling you that there is a third issue on that wall, and that the register does not have it, and that it was made out of a room where the register is." She was not looking at Marek. She had not looked at him once. "And I am telling you what else. I was in the copying room at eleven on the ninth. I was in it from nine. There were three people in the room: me, a lamp-trimmer called Wend, and a copyist called Pella Osk, who was asleep over the relief rolls for most of it and who will say so. At half past eleven a man came in who I did not know, and he went to the witness bench, which is at the left of this room, and he was there a while. And I would like the court to notice the window over the witness bench."

The window over the witness bench was a small barred hatch, three feet square, in the left wall, and the morning light came in through it and fell across the court clerk's table.

"Put the lamp there," Rell said.

The lamp was put there, and then Tamsin Rook held up the posted order by its top corner, away from everyone, and tilted it into the light, and everyone in the room leaned forward.

They could see it. It took a moment, and then two or three people said things at once, and the bronze rule had to be put down on the order to hold it steady.

Under *Certificate of the Reed Cut district* there was a second impression of the same three words, struck through, pressed deep into the fibre, and above them the ghost of a line that had begun *...the widow, of the lower...*

"Copying paper takes an impression," Rell said. "Anyone in this room who has ever ruled a correction through knows that it comes up. What I want to know is why the struck line is the widow's and not the broker's."

"That is the question, Magistrate," Tamsin said. "Because whoever did it did not remove the widow from the order. They put the certificate in front of her. If the point had been to keep her from testifying, they would have struck her out. Instead they have put a piece of paper in front of her, so that when she is asked to say what Aldren Venn signed, the record of the instrument is already in the room answering for her."

Pell Vey stood up.

He did it well. He stood up the way a man stands up who has been in front of forty courtrooms and knows that the first sentence is the only one that matters.

"Magistrate, I object on two grounds and I withdraw the second before I begin it, because I made it and it is unarguable." He put up one finger. "First: a copyist's memory is not evidence, and a copyist's private book is not a record, and the young woman is asking this court to overturn a sealed order on the strength of what she remembers and what she has written in a book of her own. That is not a challenge to an order. It is a young person's opinion of a piece of paper, offered loudly."

"And?" Rell said.

Pell sat back down. "And the second was that the source leaf has not been produced and the cabinet is closed and the seal is a First Seat reference, and I withdraw it, because I have been told the leaf will be produced."

Something moved through the room. The bench knew the words *First Seat reference*. The lane knew them better.

Rell said, "How is it that the source leaf is going to be produced, Clerk Kest, when the Notaries' Table has declined to certify a comparison and there is a provincial hold on the file?"

"Under the hold," Marek said, "the file is not to be enforced, removed, or assigned pending further notice. A hold that stops a field being sold does not stop a leaf being read. I asked the Table for a comparison on the ninth. The Table declined. I am therefore not asking the Table. I am asking this court, under its own power to hear a material dispute in a matter before it, to direct that the source leaf be produced for comparison, entered in a custody sleeve, and carried to this court by the clerk who holds the key."

"And if the Table says no?"

"Then the clerk who holds the key will have refused a court direction in open court, on the record, by name, and that will be a fact about the Table and not about me."

Sarn's voice came from the window, flat and dry, and it was the first time Marek had ever heard it without a document in front of it.

"The cabinet key is mine today," she said.

The room went quieter than the wind allowed for.

"Ivet," Rell said.

"I have held the Mosswake key four years, Magistrate, and I have said, and written, and pinned up, and I will say again for the record that I think this is a bad idea and that reopening a settled file in the flood season is how this district eats late." She came out from behind the window with a ring of keys in her hand and a grey sleeve under her arm. "But a hold is not a closure, and a court direction is not a reopening of the docket, and a source leaf that is read is a leaf that comes back into a sleeve with two seals on it. I will produce it. I want the application in my hand, in writing, from the applicant, naming the leaf, the date of the instrument, and the reason."

Marek wrote it standing up. Tamsin took the application and copied the words into her book as he spoke them, which is a thing a copyist does for a person who is being asked to speak quickly, and which nobody in the room commented on, and which Tamsin would think about later.

The leaf came at ten, in the grey sleeve, with a mark on the front and the Notaries' Table's stamp on the wax, and Sarn held it up while Rell read the sleeve, and the clerk wrote the time into the minute book, and Rell said the words *received into the custody of this court*, and a woman at the back of the bench said something to the woman beside her in a voice that carried.

The leaf is a page. It is not a dramatic object. It is a rectangle of good flax paper, the size of two hands, with a ruled border and a printed heading, and at the bottom of it a signature in a reed that has been pressed hard enough to emboss the sheet beneath, and a double reed shorthand in the margin, which is a mark a channel man makes to show that a thing was read aloud twice before it was signed, which is a practice of the Reed Cut and nowhere else. And in the left margin, low down, two pressed notches, ragged, both of them old, both of them slightly out of true.

"It says what it says," Rell said. She had not had to look for long. "Aldren Venn. Seven years from the second month. The field-side upkeep and the winter-seed repayment named to the signer, payable from his estate at the end of the term. The gate and the navigation to Sivra Oris, keeper. The channel and the lower field permitted to the household for the term. And this — *any person who shall assume a continuing duty shall sign a new obligation for it.* Which is the sentence that everybody in this room has been waiting for somebody to read aloud since the first week of the eighth month, and which nobody has read, because it is on the leaf and the leaf has been in a cabinet."

"Magistrate," Pyle said, and stood up, and he was younger than his voice suggested and much better dressed than a district certifier. "If the court is going to hear an instrument, then it should hear a certificate, because the certificate is a matter of record and the record speaks first, and I am instructed to say that the record speaks first."

"Then produce the record and let the court look at it," Rell said. "Show it to the clerk."

Pyle unrolled it. It was a single sheet, and it was a very good certificate, and it carried at its foot an impressed device of a loop of scale crossed by a bar, which is the device of a water-keeping seal and which appears on a great many genuine instruments in the river province, and beneath the device, in ink, a written impression of assent in the conventional formula.

The clerk's window said nothing. Sarn said nothing.

Marek came down off the bench. He did not have permission to be at that table and nobody stopped him.

"Don't turn it over," he said. "Turn it round. Hold it up to the light the way you would hold a leaf, because that is what it is pretending to be."

"It is a certificate of a seal," Pyle said. "It is a certified instrument. It has been certified since the eighth month, and it is the reason the district can move the crop."

"Lift it."

Pyle lifted it. The morning light came through the sheet from the barred hatch over the witness bench, and where the certificate was thin the fibres glowed, and where the impression had been pressed into it the fibres had been crushed, and the crushed band showed a rim.

"Set it down flat," Marek said, "and put the lamp at that angle, and look at the edge of the impression where the loop crosses the bar."

They did that. It took a while. The court clerk came and looked, and Sarn came and looked, and Rell came down off the bench and looked, and then she said, quietly, to the whole room:

"That is a press mark."

"A press mark," Pyle said. "A seal is pressed."

"A living scale is laid," Rell said. "A true seal is made from a scale impression, spoken assent, and a witness record, and it is laid, and the rim of it is uneven because the scale moves and the holder's breath moves, and it takes about eleven seconds and the holder is present for all of them. This was cut into a matrix and pressed in a workshop with a screw press, in one movement, in the cold, by somebody who was not here." She put two fingers on the paper. "And there is a second thing. Look at the left margin."

There was a notch in the left margin of the certificate. One notch. Clean. Even.

It was the third.

"Two on the leaf," Rell said. "Two on the witness copy. And a third on a certificate, in the position a third takes when a person is standing in the witness line." She straightened up. "Somebody in this district knows exactly what a witness mark is for, and has used the same mark to say the same thing three times, and it has worked three times, and it is not going to work a fourth time in this room."

Nobody in the room said anything for a moment, and the bronze rule moved a sheet of paper on the clerk's table all by itself in the draught from the door.

"Magistrate." Pyle gathered his case. "I am a certifier. I have carried a certificate that has been set in good order and I do not know whose hand pressed it and I will not say. I am instructed to speak to the record. The record speaks to a continuation attached to the field, and the district cannot move the crop without it, and if the court spends the morning on paper instead of on eleven hundred households then the court will find out in the spring what that costs." He put the case down. "I withdraw. I will not read the record into this hearing."

"You will not read the record," Rell agreed, "and you will not leave this building until the court has taken your name and the name of the office that instructed you, and I want both, and I want them before the recess."

Marek looked at Tamsin across the room.

She had not moved from the window. She had her book shut and her hands flat on it, and Marek understood, looking at her, that she had been frightened for the entire hour and had decided at some point in the first ten minutes to be competent in public anyway, which is a different thing and much harder.

There was a lot Marek wanted to say to the room, and he had promised, on a bank, in the dark, in words, not to say any of it in the first hour.

Rell read the memorandum from Reed Cut, which Sarn produced in the same minute book, dated the eighth of the eighth month, signed by Hallis Dren, clerk of the district water office, which recorded that the season's bank works on the Reed Cut were authorized pending inspection and that the office would be ready to receive corrections against the district's records on the ninth. Dren had signed it to clear a backlog. He had signed it on the eighth and the correction arrived on the ninth, and the memorandum and the correction were a day and a night apart, and the memorandum did not name the Venn field or the Venn family or any particular parcel, and it was a general paper about banks.

"He signed a form," Rell said, to nobody. "Mr Dren will be here at four and he will say so and he will be telling the truth, and it will not help him, and it will help me, because it means the memorandum is not the authority for the correction. It is the schedule."

Then Rell did two things in a row, and did them quickly, as if she were afraid of herself.

First: she made an order that the source leaf, the witness copy, the filed correction, the memorandum, the certificate produced by Pyle, and the copy made by the roll-keeper Tamsin Rook be entered together as one exhibit, sealed in a marked sleeve in the custody of the court, to be carried to the Mosswake registry and not to any other office, and that no copy of any of them be made except by the court or by a person the court names in open session. And she read into the record, in language the lane can use, the name of the roll-keeper, because the record ought to say who had put the page in front of it.

Second: she issued a limited audit of the Venn file, named to the instrument, named to the exhibit, and to no other instrument, and no other file, and no other person.

"You asked for a comparison of a leaf and a correction," she said to Marek. "I am giving you more than a comparison and less than a certificate, and I want the distinction in everyone's head, including yours. A comparison is paper. An audit is a person being made answerable for what the record does. I am naming you in it, Clerk Kest, and you may refuse, and if you refuse I will order the leaf back into the cabinet and this hearing will be a hearing about a copied notch and nothing else."

"No," Marek said. "I will not refuse it."

"Then you will sit where you can see the exhibit, and you will not leave the room while it is out, and if anybody in this room objects to a junior clerk of the Roll auditing a scale instrument in a river court, they may say so now."

Nobody said so.

Rell looked out of the door at the water, and then back at the room, and Marek watched her choose.

"Clerk Kest," she said. "You have brought me a seam. I will tell you what a seam is worth in this district. It is worth exactly nothing by itself. A seam proves that a record was made badly. It does not prove who made it, and it does not unmake a ruling that four hundred people have already been told about, and the moment I take this exhibit into a ruling I am going to have to say something general, because a court cannot rule on one widow without saying a sentence that the next file will copy, and every general sentence I say tonight is going to be picked up in six weeks by a clerk in a warm room who is behind on a docket and will be flattered to have it." She put both hands flat on the bench. "I am not refusing to rule. I am telling you that I am going to need language broad enough to keep four hundred households in seed through a flood, and that whatever I say in that chair at two o'clock this afternoon is going to be copied by people who do not know your name."

"Then say it," Marek said, "and let the widow hear it before you say it, and let her refuse it if it is wrong."

The room went very quiet.

"Sarn," said Rell. "What time is the recess."

"Half past eleven, Magistrate, if the court is taking evidence at two."
