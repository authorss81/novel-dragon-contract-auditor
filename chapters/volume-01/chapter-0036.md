# Chapter 36: A Name on a Door

Pell Vey offered it on the twenty-ninth of the third month, at the seventh hour, in the third gallery, standing at the end of Marek's own bench with a file under his arm, and offered it in the way a man offers a thing he has offered before, which is without any weight at all.

"There is a standing going in the Table," he said. "Provincial filings examiner. It is not a great name. It has no seal and it is not a commission and it does not survive this building. What it is, is that a district registrar answers to it, and that the answer is mine to give and yours to take, and it goes on the outside of a file with a room on the door, and the rate of pay is not the point and you know the rate of pay is not the point."

"Who asked for it to exist."

"Nobody. Standing examiners are made by the head of the Table when there is work and not enough of it, and there is work, and there has been work for four months, and there is going to be more of it, because in nine provinces there are forty-six district offices and a form series in a corridor and no named person anywhere in the chain, and that is a job, and it is a job that a man who has read a series in a corridor can do better than a man who has not." He shifted the file. "You have read it eleven times. I have watched you read it. You knew about the drawer before I told you and you went and got a ladder and stood in front of it and did not reach, which is the only reason I am standing here."

"And the terms."

"The terms are that you take provincial filings from this gallery, and that a district's answer goes to you, and that you sign it." Pell Vey put the file down on the desk. "And the other term, which is the actual term, and which I am going to say out loud because I have found that saying it out loud is cheaper than the other way: the correction on the Venn file is a routine filing defect. It is a defect in a filing, made by a broker, in a district, in the ordinary way brokers make them. The practice around it is routine. What you have done about it is not wrong and I do not want it characterised as wrong, but it is a junior clerk reading a series in a corridor and telling a form's author what it is for."

Marek Kest did not say anything for about four seconds, and in those four seconds he worked out, quickly and in the wrong order, that the sentence he had just heard was the sentence from a yard behind the Chancery on the fourth of the tenth month, built up into a load-bearing wall.

*A form with a run on it is a form about a business, and a business which prints four hundred things for four hundred courts has somebody in it whose job it is to know how many of the four hundred have complied.*

She had written all of it down. She had written the hour and the place. And under it she had written a line in her own hand about a sentence being one too many, and she had said *ask him one day, not today*, and it was the twenty-ninth day of the third month and it had not been asked, and it was going to be asked now or never, and he could not ask it, because the only way to ask it was to tell Pell Vey that his roll-keeper had a record of him, and he had given her a rule about that on the thirteenth of the twelfth and the rule bound him more than it bound her.

He could not say the word. He could not even say the shape of the word.

"I am going to say no," he said, "and I want it written down, and I want it written in the same form as the twentieth of the eleventh."

Pell Vey did not move.

"Now or in a room with a witness in it."

"Now, then, and with a witness in it, and I would not take anything else, and you may draw your own conclusions about why I would not."

"Fetch the intake clerk."

The boy came at about a minute's notice and wrote the time in the margin in ink and initialled it, because nobody asked him to, and this was the third time in six months he had done that for the same man and he had never once been thanked for it, and it is on the record in a file that nobody reads.

Then Pell Vey dictated, and he dictated it quickly and without stopping, which is a thing he does.

> *Offered by the head of the Notaries' Table to Marek Kest, clerk of the Inheritance Desk, in the third gallery of this building, on the twenty-ninth of the third month, at the seventh hour:*
>
> *Admission to the Table as a standing examiner of provincial filings, with a room, a drawer that is not the cabinet, a rate of pay, and the standing of a person to whom a district registrar's answer is given and by whom it is taken. The standing carries no seal, is not a commission, and does not survive this building.*
>
> *In consideration: that the correction filed on the ninth of the eighth month in the matter of the Venn instrument is to be treated by the holder of the standing as a routine filing defect, and the practice surrounding it as routine, and no district's answer in the matter is to be framed by reference to any series held in the corridor behind this gallery.*
>
> *Declined by the applicant on the twenty-ninth of the third month, at the seventh hour, in a room with a witness, before it was asked for in writing. The terms of the offer are retained in the file.*
>
> *Pell Vey, Notaries' Table.*

And Marek Kest signed under it, which he had not been asked to do, and which Nevin Trost had taught him to do in a warm room with a stove in it in the eleventh month, and which was the single most useful thing anybody had taught him in six months.

Pell Vey read it, and then he read the last line of it, and then he stood in the third gallery of the Roll Hall for a moment with his hands empty, which is not a thing he does.

"That is the second recorded refusal with my name on it in four months," he said.

"Yes."

"The first one cost this building a sentence and forty-six districts. This one costs this building a standing examiner." He picked the file up again. "I want to say three things and I am going to say them quickly because we are both of us going to be busy for a week. One: you have just refused the only thing anybody has offered you in six months that would have made nine paragraphs answerable, and you refused it for the reason that it comes with a condition, which is a reason I would have given in the fifth year and I did not, and I have thought about that all afternoon."

"Two: I am not going to argue with you about the correction. I do not know whether it is routine. I have signed the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth month, which says a great deal about the whole of it, and my name is on that, and now my name is on this, and in four years a person is going to read both and ask me why I let a junior clerk answer for my office twice, and I will have to say that he wrote a better letter than I would have written, twice, and that is going to be a very short answer by the third time."

"And three."

"Three: you have again done the thing where you ask in writing for less than you wanted before anybody has asked you to, and I want you to know that I have now seen you do it three times and I have never once seen you do the opposite, and I do not know whether that is a discipline or a disease, and I have not decided, and I am going to go and think about it on a boat."

He went to his own gallery. He did not ask about the corridor.

---

What he had actually refused, when he made himself go back over it on the stairs and count it properly, was not a room and a drawer.

It was the letter.

A standing examiner of provincial filings is not a person who checks other people's work. That is what a deputy is. A standing examiner is a person who may issue, in his own name, under his own hand, an instruction to a district, and a district holding a flood docket is obliged to comply with it, and every such instruction goes out on the ordinary distribution, which is forty-six places, and every one of them is a place that will comply because complying is cheaper than explaining.

He could have written one. He could have written it that night, in the alcove, in about forty minutes, and it would not have been a rule and it would not have needed to be. It would have been *Provincial Continuities, flood seasons, filing practice: no endorsement entered in the model form on or after the first of the current month is to be treated as an individual filing unless it is itemised and numbered, and any endorsement of which the district has no number is to be reported to this office with the instrument.* Forty-six districts, on the ordinary distribution, in a hand, over a name, with a date.

It would have worked. It would have worked in about eleven weeks and it would have found the one at Millbrook Reach in a fortnight, because a district holding a filing with no number is a district holding a filing it cannot produce, and a clerk who is told in writing to go and look for those will go and look.

And it would have been the fourth thing he had refused to do and the first one he could have done himself, and the difference between them is that the first three other people did them and this one would have been his, and the reason that matters is not pride. It is that a thing a man issues himself is a thing a man is the author of, and there is no version of this in which a man who has authored a general instruction to forty-six districts is afterwards able to say that the only thing wrong with the sentence is that nobody wrote it down.

He sat with that for a long time and then he did the only honest thing available to him, which was to write it out and file it nowhere.

*I could have issued the instruction that would have found the Millbrook filing. It is one page. I have written it in my own book tonight in full, with a date, and I am not going to send it, and I want the reason written down where I will find it in a year, because the reason is not a good one and I am going to forget that in a year.*

*The reason is that I would be able to. That is all. There is no principle in it. A man of twenty-nine with a dispute, no standing, and a mother whose name is in a blank, would have been able to reach forty-six districts with one page of his own hand tonight, and there is not one single person in this building who would have stopped him, and I want that written down in my own hand by me, in a book nobody else has, in a city where my own office has spent four months telling a district that its standing instructions are the authority and its own ordinary is enough.*

*In a month I will be able to see whether that is courage or shame, and today it is the other thing, and the other thing has a name and the name is Pell Vey.*

---

He did not write to Nevin Trost either, and he thought about that for longer than he thought about the letter.

The refusal with Pell's name at the foot of it went into a file in this building, and Pell Vey had signed the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth month, and there are now three documents in Auremar with Pell Vey's name on them in a matter he does not control, and he has asked twice for ninety words that came out of a corridor in this building on the ninth of the eleventh month, in a room with no witness, from a person who is not the head of the office, and which are in an examiner's hands and were never minuted.

The rule was made on the thirteenth of the twelfth and it is a good rule: neither of them moves it without a name on it. And the rule was made for a case in which Pell's exposure was a signature on a reply that said an office had offered a young clerk something and put him in a position where he had to.

Pell's exposure is now different in kind. It is not a signature on a statement about a practice. It is a signature on a statement about the correction itself.

Which meant that the ninety words mattered about four times as much as they had in November, and that the rule he had made with a copyist in a copying alcove on a good instinct was now, for the first time, inconvenient to the man he had made it about, and he was not going to break a rule he had been proud of for four months because it had become inconvenient to somebody else.

He wrote that down too, at the eleventh hour, in one line, and the line was: *I am not writing to Trost, and the reason I am not is that I designed the rule to protect me and it is now protecting him, and a rule that only ever works in one direction is not a rule and is a preference.*

And then he added, after a moment, in the same ink:

*And Tamsin has a piece of paper in a drawer that she has told me about four times, and it is about a quarterly return, and I have not collected it, and I have known about it for a hundred and sixteen days, and I have not collected it, and I am not going to, and I want it set down tonight, in my own hand, while I still know exactly what I am doing, that I have now twice built a rule out of a piece of paper in somebody else's coat.*

---

He told Tamsin Rook at the ninth hour, in the alcove, standing, because he had been sitting down twice.

"Pell offered me a standing examiner this evening and I refused it and the refusal is entered and I signed it."

She was copying a filing book and she did not stop, which she had never done in six months, not once, and Marek noticed the not-stopping and understood it immediately and correctly.

"Who was the witness."

"The intake clerk. He wrote the time in the margin in ink and initialled it and nobody asked him to."

"And you have told me four hours after you decided, on the evening of the day, in a room, having already got it written and witnessed." Tamsin Rook went on copying. "I am not going to do the speech. I have got four lines in a coat pocket in your coat about a quarterly return and I have not collected them and I am not going to collect them this month, and I want you to know that they are still there, and I want you to know that I know exactly what you have just done and I am not going to reward it by pretending it was different this time."

"I know."

"I am going to say the one thing and then I am going to copy until the eleventh hour, and the one thing is this. You have just given up the only thing you came to Auremar for, and you did it in a room with a witness, and you came down here and told me at the ninth hour, and you are not proud, and I have watched you be proud about a worse thing than this for six months." She turned a page. "Go to bed."

He did not go to bed.

He sat on the end of a bench in the copying alcove of the third gallery of the Roll Hall until about the eleventh hour with the file of a rejected offer on his knees, and the thing he could not get past, and it was not the standing, was this.

He had expected to be wretched. He had told himself on the stairs that he would be, and had looked forward to it in a way he was not proud of, because a man who gives up something real ought to be wretched, and then had gone in and said no in nine seconds, and had felt something arrive in the space where the wretchedness was going to be that was not wretchedness at all.

Relief.

And then, about four minutes later, the other half of it, which was that a man who is relieved to give up a standing has not been wanting a standing. He has been using it. A standing in a gallery in Auremar whose job is to know how many of the four hundred have complied was never going to be an end to anything, because there are forty-six district offices in this empire and a form series in a corridor and a woman four hundred miles away in a house in a lane who reads a paper out loud with no advice, and none of those three things is fixed by a man in a room with his name on the door.

He wrote it in his own book, at the eleventh hour, in two sentences, and they were:

*I have wanted for six months to be the person who can make nine provinces answer, and it turns out that wanting it was the cheapest thing I have ever done, and the cheapest things are the ones that feel like relief.*

And then, because he had promised himself in the seventeenth of the eleventh that he would always write the opposite of what he wanted underneath:

*The relief is not a reason to refuse things. The relief is a reason to find out what I actually want, and to do that in the fifth month, and not tonight, and not out loud.*

---

The counterparty notification went out on the thirtieth of the third month and it was the only thing in the whole of this matter that looked like a summons and was not one.

Marek Kest took it up to Reed Cut himself, on the river boat, and it is a single sheet on a district water office's own paper, and it is the form that office has used for nineteen years to tell the holder of a scale instrument that the instrument is named in a proceeding, and it has one line on it that no form in the Chancery has and that the water office has had since before either of them was born:

*She is notified. She is not under the authority of this court and this court cannot require her to do anything.*

Hallis Dren read it, checked the reference against the water office's own book of scale instruments it has countersigned, found it, stamped the copy that stays behind, and sent it up the water on the morning boat, and wrote in the copy, in his own hand and without being asked, the number of the page the instrument is on, because he has been a clerk of that office for six years and he knows what a bare reference costs a person who has to look for it.
