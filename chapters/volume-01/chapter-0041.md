# Chapter 41: Two of the Four Are Hers

The barn held tilling seed and a bar of soap's worth of nothing else, and Orra Dunn had been counting the sacks since the first of the month because counting them was the only thing in the house she could do that was not waiting.

Forty-one sacks, four pounds' worth of which had come down the lane in a cloth in the ninth month of last year from a neighbour of forty-one years' standing who had walked half a mile in the rain to give it to her and would not let her thank her. The rye had gone thirty-one miles up a canal to a mill at Coldsfoot in the second month and the sale was in a book and the book was right. What was left in the upper barn was a floor of packed earth with a chalk line on it where the rye had been, and the chalk line was the exact width of the rye, and a person could stand in the barn and look at it and see the size of the thing that had gone.

The hearing was on the twenty-first of the fifth month, at the second hour, in the Mosswake Roll Court's own room, and the confirmation was on the wall at the window in a clerk's small hand with a shilling's worth of paper behind it, and Nessa's name was on it, and Orra had the copy because she had paid the shilling and because she had walked down to the sill twice to be sure that the second name was there.

Both names were there. Not in a column, not under a heading that said what they were. Two names on a line, and the line said the household is to be heard and the girl is to be heard, and the day on it, and that was all, and it was more than had ever been on a piece of paper about this house.

---

"What is on your slate," said Nessa Dunn.

"Fifteen is a strange age to be curious."

"I'm not being curious." The girl was at the table with her sleeves pushed back, doing nothing with them, which she had been doing for a quarter of an hour. "You've got the slate. You've had it since the first of the month. You've read it to me twice and both times you stopped at the end of the fourth one and looked at me, and you didn't do that with the other eleven."

Orra put the slate down flat.

"Eleven what."

"Questions." Nessa came round the table. "There are four of them and they're at the court window on a board, and the woman from the seed barns wrote one of them, and you learned them off somebody's slate in the winter because you can't read a sentence, and you have said them to me twice and both times you have stopped after the fourth and looked at me."

"Eleven is still a strange age."

"It's a strange age to be asked something and to have to be the one who works out what the question means." Nessa put her hand flat on the slate the way her mother did, which she had learned in the same room. "Say the four."

So Orra read them off the slate in the flat voice she had spent a winter getting, because the first time she had read them she had put a question mark on the end of the third one and Wenna Sallow had taken it off for her without saying anything about it.

*One. Is there work in it?*

*A ditch. A gate. A barn. A bench. A boat. A load.*

*Two. Whose name is on it — yours, or a word that walks about, like the holder?*

*Three. If you stop, whose name answers for it?*

*Four. Who read this to you, and do they get paid if you sign?*

"That is the whole of them," said Orra. "And the third one is the one that is about us, and I have thought about it for nine weeks and I have not got a better answer than the one I have got, which is that the word answers and not you, and that I am sorry about it, and that you are the only person in this house it is going to be true for."

"Mother."

"I know what I am saying."

"You have been saying it to me since the twenty-third of the ninth month," said Nessa, "and you have never once said it to yourself out loud, and I have listened for nine weeks for you to."

The barn was quiet for a moment, and out in the yard a rope creaked.

"Sit down," said Orra. "There is a thing I have to do and I want to do it with you in the room and I do not want to be asked about it afterwards."

---

She had had the slate since the first of the first month, when she had stood at a window in the rain and asked to be put on a wall and been told no, and had then asked for a different day and been given a refusal with a date on it instead, and had paid a shilling for the copy of her own refusal. And the thing nobody had designed was the part that came after: a name on a board is a thing a creditor can read.

Anselm Grice's boy had bought four copies off that slate in the four months since. Four, at a shilling each, and Orra had watched him do it the fourth time and had worked out that a man does not buy the fourth copy because he has not understood the first three.

And the thing that had happened because of it was this. Grice held a paper on the seed-share, served on the twenty-ninth of the first month, which stood. He held a second paper — Orra knew it existed because a man in Greyfen had told her so in a doorway in the second month, for nothing, in the way that men in Greyfen tell women in doorways for nothing — and that second paper was on the barn term itself, on the floor of this barn, and the forty-one sacks and the chalk line, and it had not been served, and it was not going to be served before the twenty-first of the fifth month, and the reason it was not going to be served was three words in a letter from a house off the Greyfen quay which Orra had copied out onto the slate on the ninth of the second month, and the three words were: *before the hearing*.

Service before a pending hearing, and the household could stand in a room and say that the creditor went round them, and a court that hears that sentence once does not hear anything else in that file for a year.

"So the board is why my seed is not gone," said Nessa.

"Yes."

"And if the twenty-first goes badly."

"Then the third paper comes, and it is on this floor, and there is a lawyer, and there is a man who has been writing letters for a fee since the second month." Orra put the slate down again. "And I am not going to stand in this barn on the fifteenth of the fifth and tell you that the board has saved us, because the board has not saved us. The board has made a man in a counting house buy four shilling's worth of paper about me. What the board has done is that a man with a lawyer has to do this in a room instead of doing it in a lane, and he would rather not, and he is not a coward, he is careful, and careful is the same as frightened in a man who owns two things."

---

"What are you going to do with the slate," said Nessa.

And Orra told her, which took a while and which she had thought about at the vat and at the sluice and in the dark, and what she told her was this.

The court could not do anything about Grice's paper. Nobody in that room had the power to lift it. A served paper is a served paper, and a clerk's letter is not a bar to a creditor and a magistrate's promise is not a bar to a creditor, and a line on a slate is not a bar to a creditor, and if anybody had stood up in that room on the tenth of the fourth and told the Dunn household that the twenty-first of the fifth would stop a man who has already served a paper, the person who said it would have been lying in a room with a girl in it.

So Orra Dunn was not going to ask the court to strike anything.

"I am going to ask for the opposite," she said. "I am going to ask that his paper be read out loud in the room, in front of you, all of it, and that the reading be written down as having happened. That is all. That is the whole of what I am going to say and I have said it out loud twice now and it still does not sound like anything."

"Read out who."

"The paper. The one he served. With the date on it and the thing it is against and the price."

"Ma—"

"And I want to say the reason and I cannot find a good one." Orra looked at the chalk line on the floor. "Because he has a lawyer and a served paper and we have a date, and on the twenty-first there is a room with a magistrate in it, and the only thing I have ever found out about courts in nine months is that a thing said out loud in a room has to be answered in that room, and a thing nobody has said is a thing nobody has to answer. And if I go in there and say *that paper is a lie*, I have said a thing about a man I cannot prove anything about, in front of sixty people, and he will have a lawyer who will stand up and ask me one question and I will not have an answer."

"So what will you say."

"I will say: this is the paper, this is what it says, this is who it names, and this is what it does not say, and I am not asking you to take it off me, and I am asking you to hear it." Orra pushed the slate across the table. "I am asking a court to hear a creditor's paper instead of to stop him. I do not know why that is the right thing. I have gone over it for nine weeks and I do not know, and I am asking anyway, and I am telling you that I do not know, because you are going to be in the room and you are fifteen and you must not find out at the second hour that your mother did not know either."

---

The four questions were written out on the back of the copy of the slate in Orra Dunn's own hand, with Nessa's name at the top of it, and it took the better part of an hour because Orra's hand is a seed-merchant's hand and does four things well and a fifth thing not at all, and a question mark is a fifth thing.

> *The four questions, put as I would put them, for the twenty-first of the fifth month, by Nessa Dunn, of the upper seed barn, who is fifteen, in her own words, with her name on it.*
>
> *One. Is there work in it, and is the work mine, or is it the work of whoever is in the room when it is done?*
>
> *Two. Whose name is on it, and is mine one of the names on it, and is mine the first one?*
>
> *Three. If I stop, whose name answers for it?*
>
> *Four. Who read this to me, and if I say I do not understand it, what happens to them?*

Orra read it out four times, and the fourth time she read it as it was written, and Nessa took the slate and put it down on the table and looked at it for a while.

"The third one is yours," she said.

"The third one is ours. It is the one I have been carrying since the ninth month and I am not giving it up to be clever with."

"The first one is different. It says *is the work mine*. That is not the same as *is there work in it*. That is the whole thing. That is the whole thing about a person and it took you a year to get to it and I got there in a minute and I want it in." Nessa put her finger on the second one. "And this one. *Whose name is on it, and is mine one of the names on it, and is mine the first one.* That is what I want to know. Not who is on it. Whether I am on it at all, and if I am on it, whether I am the first name or the last one, because in this file I am the last one, and the last one is the one that pays."

"That is two of the four," said Orra.

"That is two of the four."

"And the two you have kept are the two you could already answer, and you have kept them exactly as they are."

"Yes." Nessa Dunn squared the slate. "Because the other two are the ones I do not know the answer to, and I am not going to have my mother's handwriting make them sound better than they are. If somebody reads those two out in a room and somebody answers them in a room, then somebody will have said them. That is all I am doing. I am not asking anybody to know the answer. I am asking somebody to have to have one."

---

Ilsabet Kell came up the upper lane on the arm of her son at about the seventh hour on the seventeenth, which was four months later than she had meant to and no later than anybody had arranged, and Bodo Kell had asked her twice in the yard whether she was sure and had been told to stop asking, and had stopped.

She had a box in her own hand and she would not put it down.

"Four months," she said. "I want that said first, in front of the girl, because I told a woman on the second of the first month that I would stand in the second month and it is the fifth of the fifth, and the reason is not hers and it is not mine, it is that nobody would have it, and a thing nobody will have waits until somebody will."

"You do not have to do this."

"I have never in my life had to do anything so I do not see why I should start." She put the box on the table. "It is the four lines. You wrote them in my words and you made me sign them, and I have read them about once a month since, and I have not changed a word, and I want to know whether the court will take a box off an old woman in a room, and I do not, so I am going to be in the room when it is read and I am going to have said first that I am sixty-one and I lent it to a house of forty years standing and not to a girl of fifteen, and I would like the reading of it and the reading of this to be in the same hand in the same book on the same day, and I do not care what else happens on the twenty-first."

"Bodo."

"I have told him." She did not look at her son. "I have told him and he has not asked me to stop, and I would like it noticed that not asking is a thing he chose and not a thing I got."

---

On the twentieth, at the fourth hour, the confirmation came up from the court in a folded sheet with a line number on it, and Orra read it out twice in the barn, and then read it out a third time because Nessa made her, and the third time she had the slate in front of her and read it against it, line by line, which took eleven minutes and which found nothing wrong, and which was the most useful eleven minutes Orra Dunn had spent since the first of the second month.

Then she wrote four lines at the foot of it, which is not what a clerk writes at the foot of a confirmation, and which Sarn read at the window on the twenty-first before the second hour and entered in the day's column because it is her window and her column.

> *Entered at the household's request. Orra Dunn, the upper seed barn, asks that the paper served on the forty quarters of winter rye on the twenty-ninth of the first month be read out loud in the room at the hearing, in full, in front of the person it names, and that the reading be entered. She does not ask this court to strike it, to hold it, or to do anything about it, because she has been told that this court has no power to do any of those things and she believes it. She is asking for a hearing of it. A. Dunn. Witnessed by the household.*
>
> *Second: the household asks that the girl be asked, and not told.*

Sarn read it back at the sill and said, "I will put it in and I will tell you now that I do not know whether the magistrate will read a creditor's paper into a hearing, and I have asked, and the answer was that it is not a thing a magistrate does, and I have written the question down with the day on it, and it is on the board for anybody who comes to the window, and if she says yes on the morning then it is hers and not mine."

"Then it is hers," said Orra.

"It was always going to be hers," said Ivet Sarn. "That is the whole of what a slate is. You put your name on it so that a person you have never met has to answer you, and it worked, and it worked on me this morning, because I have never in nine years been told by a household what I should write in a column."

---

The girl stayed up after the others had gone down to the flats, because she was fifteen and there was nothing else to do with a fifth month, and she sat at the barn table with the slate in front of her and the copy of the confirmation and the four lines in her mother's hand, and she asked the question that Orra Dunn had known was coming since the seventeenth and had hoped to get to the twenty-first without answering.

"Ma. If I stand up in that room and say it is a lie, what happens."

"Nothing," said Orra. "That is the answer and I have had it since the ninth of the tenth month and I have not liked it. Nothing happens to you. The magistrate cannot make a girl of fifteen say a thing she does not believe, and cannot stop her saying a thing she does, and will not try, and a lawyer will stand up and ask you when you signed it and you will say never, and the room will do what a room does with two people who are both telling the truth and only one of them is owed anything."

"So it does not matter what I say."

"It matters exactly as much as it matters and no more." Orra put both hands flat on the table. "Which is the first honest thing anybody has offered you in a year, and it is not much, and you may have it."

Nessa Dunn sat with that for a while.

"Write it up again," she said. "The four. In the same hand, because I want it to be my mother's hand and not mine, and I want it to be in front of me on the table on the second hour so that if anybody says *a girl of fifteen wrote her own questions* they can look at it and see the paper my mother ruled and my name at the top of it."

"Your name is at the top of it now."

"Then leave it at the top of it and rule the lines again, and if you get a wrong stroke on the second one I would rather know tonight than in a room."
