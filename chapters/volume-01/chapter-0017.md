# Chapter 17: The Mark

The room above the chandler's shop had a smell in it that Marek had stopped noticing in about the fourth day and had started noticing again on the eighteenth of the tenth month, a month after the inventory, because the room was empty and the fire had not been in it since the constable's boy had swept the floor and gone back to his other work.

Cold ash. Lamp oil. The particular sourness of ninety-one leaves of a broker's book that have been copied twice.

There was still a bar across the door, and the district was still paying for the room, and the order held the room until the last of the seized papers had been copied and each protected person had his own copy of his own papers delivered to him, and eleven of the thirteen had not signed for theirs.

The chandler came up at the eighth hour with a candle to talk about it, which was a thing Marek had not expected and which turned out to be the most sensible conversation he had had all week.

"Two months," the chandler said. "I have had a bar on my door and a man with a chain on my stair and a queue of people who were frightened to come in, and the women who buy candles do not care whose office it is, they care that there is a queue, and the queue has gone and taken my custom with it."

"Your rent is being paid."

"My rent is being paid by a district that I did not choose and cannot refuse," said the chandler, "and I am not asking you for anything, and I want you to know that I have said the same thing to the officer, who was decent about it, and I would like it noted that nobody in this building has been rude to me."

"Noted."

"Noted where?"

"Here," Marek said, "in the copy book, with the date, and a copy goes to the district, because you have told me a true thing about your trade and it is the sort of thing that ought to be written down in case anybody ever says a chandler's shop is a neutral place."

The chandler looked at him for a moment and then put his candle on the table and went down.

---

The eleven who had been on the bench had not gone home on the eighteenth of the ninth month and stayed there. Four of them had shut.

One had put a shutter across a counting office on the Kiln road and had it shuttered still, and the shutter was not because of the constable, it was because the two investigators who had come in the third week were not constables and had not shown anybody anything, and the difference between the two had taken a fortnight to explain to a man who had been ruined in a day. One had sold his plates. One had gone to a second district with his family and his stock. And the fourth had simply stopped answering letters, and his wife had written to the district to say that he was not stopping on purpose, he was a man who worked with his hands and the inventory was a kind of weather that had come in.

None of that was anybody's fault and all of it was a consequence, and the district's letter about it used the word *disruption*, and Marek read the word in a cold room on the seventeenth of the tenth month and did not have a better one.

---

The three who could not work were not the three he would have picked.

He had expected the frightened ones, and two of the three were frightened: a wool factor of about fifty who had gone grey in a month and who could not get a merchant to look at him across a counter, and a man who kept a toll book at the Barking gate and who had been asked twice by men he had charged for eleven years whether he was a witness.

The third was a woman of about fifty-five called Ilsabet Roon who wrote wills, marriage settlements, and small sea protests for the dock labourers, and who had come to the district house on the ninth of the month and had been told, politely, by a man in a good coat, that they would want their paper from a different writer in future.

"They do not want a different writer," Ilsabet Roon said. "They want somebody who is not in a file. That is what you have done to me and you did it in a fortnight by writing my name down properly."

The protection order that Marek had drafted in the ninth month defined protection as three things: no seizure of the papers of a named protected person, no attendance at a broker's office without a court officer, and no exclusion from a hearing.

None of those three things was a trade. All three of them were about paper, and a person who earns by writing paper was not protected by anything on that list, because what she had lost was not paper and was not attendance at a broker's office, and could not be shown to have been taken from her.

"Put her in the record," said Ruhl Dunnet, who had come up to the room at the ninth hour with a coat on and a list. "I cannot help her. I can put a line in the record that says the order does not extend to a person's competence, and I will put it, and it will be read by about eleven people, and eleven people is more than none."

"Does it change anything."

"It changes what is written down," said Dunnet. "In nine years it has changed what is written down four times and what happened twice. I stopped expecting it to do anything and started expecting it to be evidence, and I would rather you had the evidence than the expectation."

---

The girl at the copying frame came up at the tenth hour on the same day, and she was seventeen, and her name was Bryn Mawer, and she had been Dray's for two years, and she was not on the bench, and she was not on Marek's list of thirteen.

He had noticed her on the first day of the inventory. He had written down, in his own notes, that she was better than the copyists in his gallery, and he had thought then that she would be a problem for somebody.

She stood in the doorway with her hands behind her and said, "I am not here to complain about anything. I know what happened and I know whose office it was and I am not going to say he was unkind to me, because he was not, he paid on the Friday and he always paid on the Friday."

"Then what."

"I have got nothing since the eighteenth of the ninth month and I have got a room I rent off a woman in Candlewright Lane who is now taking a man from the chandler's trade in the front," she said. "And I would like to be examined in the copying, and I know that is a thing you cannot do and that you have told me already by being the sort of man who does not say yes at once."

Marek looked at what he had written in the ninth paragraph, which was the paragraph that named the thirteen and defined the protection, and which was nine lines long and the only paragraph of the nine he had enjoyed writing.

"Neither can I," he said. "Not in the term you mean. I can put your name in a supplementary request to a higher office and it will be read by a tired man in a cold room and it will cost me about a fortnight of his patience, and I have not got a fortnight of his patience, I have got a request that has been sitting unanswered since the eighteenth of the ninth month and I am about to ask that office for something much larger than your name."

"Then do not put it in."

"If I do not put it in, it is not written down anywhere that a woman of seventeen was copying Venn documents in a broker's office on the seventeenth of the ninth month with the file in front of her and the copying in her hand," said Marek. "If it is written down, then in about four years somebody who is looking for the person who did the copying has got a name and a date, and the name is yours, and it is on your side of the paper, and that is worth a fortnight."

Bryn Mawer stood in the doorway and thought about it, in the way of a person who has been thinking about a thing for a fortnight and has not been asked.

"Right," she said. "Then put it in. And do not put in that I was frightened, because I was not."

"You were not frightened."

"I was not," she said. "I was angry. They are different and I would like it written down properly."

So he wrote it down properly, in a supplementary sheet, with the date and the room and Dunnet's initials as the officer in the room, and she read it before he sealed it, which he had not offered and which she had assumed, and which he understood afterwards was the reason she had come.

---

Brammel came at the second hour of the afternoon and he came alone, and he had a paper of his own in his coat which he took out and put on the table face down.

"The green box," he said.

"It is sealed in the district store, item nine of eleven."

"It is a box of my papers. It has been in that room eleven years and I have paid for the storage of it every year and it is mine, and I want it back, and I want it destroyed."

Marek looked at the paper on the table and did not touch it.

"You understand those are two requests," he said.

"I understand they are one request," said Brammel. "I do not want it destroyed because I want my own name out of the inventory, and I want my name out of the inventory because I have been in that office eleven years and I signed things in that office without reading them, because he was Corvin Dray and you did not read the paper when Corvin Dray handed it to you, and I have known that for about a month and it has not gone away, and every one of those papers is in that box, and there are four of them with my name at the bottom of them and my mark beside my name, and if that box is opened in a room by a man who is not me then I am a man who signed four things he did not read, and I will spend the rest of what I have left explaining that, to people who will not care, at a length."

Marek sat down on the edge of the table.

"Who read them to you."

"I do not know," said Brammel. "That is the part I would like you to write down. I do not know whether a man read them to me or a woman read them to me or nobody read them to me and I signed because he put the pen in my hand, and I have been in this province forty years and I have signed papers I did not read in three offices and I have never once asked, and I would like to know whether it was him, and I cannot have it, and so I have come to ask for the box to be burned."

"Mr Dunnet."

"I have already asked him. He has told me twice, in the same words, that an inventoried article is not destroyed on the application of the person it was taken from, because then the inventory is a list of things that no longer exist, and the inventory is the only thing in this business that anybody can check." Brammel put one finger on the face-down paper. "I have accepted that. I am not a fool and I did not come up here to shout at a constable. I came up here because you are the man who wrote the list of the thirteen, and I want to tell you that the protection order you wrote has just given me back the only copy of me that exists."

Marek had not thought of that and it took him a moment.

"The inventory copy," said Marek.

"Wait," he said. "Say the rest of that. Whose copy is it."

"Which one."

"The one in the case. Whose copy is it."

"Twice over," said Brammel. "It is a copy of my papers, made in that room off my papers, so it is mine; and it is a copy of the inventory, which is a document of the court, so it is yours. I asked the officer about it on the Thursday and he said the district's own count and its own inventory stay in the district store and come back to nobody, and that what comes back to me is my own half." He took his hand off the paper. "I would have taken the whole thing if he had offered it and I am telling you that so that you know what kind of man is standing in your doorway. So the four things I did not read are in my own house now, in my own case, and they are my own half of a copy, and there is a receipt, and I have spent eleven years being somebody who does not keep a case of papers, and I am going to be somebody who keeps a case of papers for the rest of my life. Thank you very much."

There was nothing to be said to that, and Marek did not try, and after a moment Brammel said, "The name."

"What about it."

"Item nine says *Brammel, of the Candlewright Lane bench, a client of the said office, whose papers were in the green box under the window*. It does not say what I did there and it does not name an office, and if it stops at *a client* then a man has to look for me before he knows anything." Brammel straightened up, and he was sixty and he had got the thing he came for in his hand. "Take the rest of the line off. *Whose papers were in the green box.* I do not mind being a client. I mind being the sentence after it."

Marek wrote it out and showed it to him and read it back, and the clerk's hand on the bench above them wrote it into the return of the inventory in the ordinary way, and Brammel read the return before he signed it, which took him a long time and which was, Marek thought, the single most impressive thing anybody in the lane had done that month.

Then the box came back on the Thursday, in a leather case, with a receipt, and Brammel signed for it in a hand that was not steady, and took it down the stairs himself rather than let the district's boy carry it, and the district's boy let him, which was also correct.

---

Dunnet brought the offer up at the fourth hour of the afternoon and brought it badly, which was to his credit.

"From the district," he said. "Not from me. I am the man who has to hand it over and I have had it in my coat since the ninth hour and I have been waiting for a moment when you were not in the middle of something."

"Say it."

"They will take Corvin Dray's plea on the local counts. The correction in the Venn file is struck out of the record and the file is restored complete, which means the instrument goes back into the file as Aldren Venn signed it, in his own hand, with two notches, and there will be a minute saying the file was restored and the applicant did not object. And in exchange your request is narrowed to this district. The form series and the two provincial entries go out of it."

"And the eleven."

"And the thirteen," said Dunnet, "keep every protection in the order, and the district says so in writing, and the district means it, and I have read the writing."

Marek sat with it for a while. Then he said, "No."

It came out faster than he expected and Dunnet, who had been braced for a speech, had nothing ready for it.

"That is the whole of your answer."

"It is the whole of my answer," Marek said, "and I am going to tell you the three reasons inside it, because you are entitled to them and because I have been given the bad reasons by better men than me all month. One: it restores the Venn file and it restores it by making it true that there was nothing wrong, and there was something wrong, and the reason I can prove anything at all is that a man sold a third notch for four shillings. Two: it does not touch the correction's author, and it does not touch the form, and it does not touch the man who wrote the four lines. Three: it closes my request by agreeing that there was nothing to ask about." He put his hands on the table. "Those are the reasons. None of them is that it is a bribe and none of them is that I am noble. It is a good offer and I am turning it down because the only thing I have found out in a month is a price list and a man in a cell, and if the price list goes into a drawer tonight then in the fourth month somebody is going to do this again and there will be no price list."

Dunnel wrote for a while, and then read it back, and then made him say it again so that the record and the mouth matched, which was the whole of his trade and the reason he was good at it.

"Offer declined, hour as given," he said. "Reasons given and read back." He shut the book. "And the plea?"

"Refused, and entered."

"Entered where?"

"Entered on the record, at the hour, in front of the magistrate who will take the matter, and it goes on the district calendar, and the calendar gives a month, and there will be a trial in the next sitting of this court, on a count of forging a witness sequence and selling a pressed certificate, and he will be convicted on the ledger and on the box with forty-one leaves in it against a docket of forty-three, and it will take a morning, and it will not be about the four lines." Marek said it without any pleasure at all, because it was true and because he had thought about it for a fortnight. "I have not been asked to help with it and I have not offered. If a man in custody gets a better deal by saying nothing for six months then he will get it, and there is nothing in the world I can do about that, and I would rather say it out loud in a room than discover in the fourth month that I had been counting on a conviction to carry an argument."

Then Dunnet said, "You will have to tell her."

"Yes."

"Not me. Not through me. She is owed it from the man who refused it and not from the constable who carried it."

---

The statement was taken on the nineteenth of the tenth month, in a cell in the district house, with a magistrate from the district bench, a clerk of the district, and Tamsin Rook, and Marek was not permitted to be in the room, which was the correct decision and which cost him the whole morning.

He spent the morning in a corridor with a bench in it and the sound of a door at the end of it, and twice a man came through the door and read out a number of pages, and both times the number was lower than the last.

Tamsin came out at the sixth hour and sat down on the other end of the bench and did not say anything for about a minute.

"He named a place," she said.

"Marek—"

"He named a place and no people," said Tamsin. "Eleven pages and not one of them is a name, and I have written every one of them down, and I can hand you the fact of it: there is not a person's name anywhere in that statement, and there is a description of a place in it, and a description is worth almost nothing, and I have never in my life been so glad to write down something worth almost nothing."

She had her book open on her knees and her hand flat on it, the way she had it in the room above the chandler's when Dray had said the thing about the boy who brings up the sheets.

"Read it to me," Marek said.

So she read it, in a corridor, in the flat voice she used in hearings, and he listened to it twice.

The first four pages were the shape of the business and he had all of it already. The fifth page was the one that was new.

Scale matrices, Tamsin read. Not seals. Not certificates. A matrix is the plate, and a plate is not a seal and cannot be a seal, and what a plate is for is the making of a pressed impression that looks like a seal, and what a matrix is sold for is to somebody who wants a press and no witness, which is a person who has already decided what impression they want and has gone shopping for the tool that makes it.

"They are bought," Tamsin said, "and Dray has never bought one, and he has never seen one bought, and he has described to me, in detail, and in the right order, the wrapper they come in."

"How does he know."

"Because he has been the man in the middle for thirty-one years and a man in the middle is told things," said Tamsin. "A counting house that sells plates to notaries will take a customer's order. It will not know what the customer does with the plate. It will know the district, because the district is on the order, and it will know that the order came through a broker, because brokers do not send their own name."

"And the owner."

"Four leaves of it, and it is the only place in ninety-one leaves where I have seen a mark instead of a name." She turned the book round. "It is a square, filled, with a bar through it and a nick in the top edge. It is drawn small, in the margin, in the same hand as the rest of the ledger, and against it he has written the word *paid* and nothing else, and there is no name and no street and no initial anywhere on ninety-one leaves, and what he has given me instead is a row of counting houses by the Kiln sheds, which is a place with forty doors in it and is therefore no place at all."

Marek took the book and looked at it. It was a small solid square with a line through it and a notch cut out of the top left corner, drawn quickly, the way a man draws a thing he has drawn nine hundred times.

"Where does it go."

"It does not go anywhere. That is the whole of the thing and it is why I have been sitting on this bench for an hour rather than walking." She pointed. "Thirty-eight," said Tamsin, before he asked, because he was going to. "Thirty-eight lines of that ledger carry that mark in the *carry* column. Thirty-eight. And the ledger is one broker's book for a district with four hundred and sixty parcels on the water office's boards, and thirty-eight is not a number for that. It is a number for something else. It is a number for a house that supplies every broker in the country who wants a plate."

"How many districts."

"Three." Tamsin said it without any emphasis at all, which was how she delivered the worst facts. "Auremar on the great majority. Glasswater on four lines, all of them in the spring. Emberwatch on two lines, both of them in the second month, and one of each of those six is a provincial entry, and the two provincial entries are the ones you named in your third paragraph, and the mark is against them in the same hand that wrote *paid* thirty-eight times."

The corridor was very quiet. A door opened at the far end and a man went through it and the door shut.

"Emberwatch is four hundred miles away," Marek said.

"It is four hundred and forty by the river road and rather more by the coach, and the coach does not run in the second month because of the frost, and if it did run it would take a person four days," said Tamsin. "And no one in this building has written to Emberwatch. There is no request in. There is no preservation demand served. Your fourth paragraph names four offices and Emberwatch is not one of them, because you had Glasswater and Emberwatch as *entries in a ledger*, and an entry in a ledger is not an office, and a very tired man in a very cold office was entirely right about that a month ago, and it is the reason that district has had a month of nobody knocking on its door about this."

Marek put his hand over his eyes, which he had been doing four times a day for a month and which was not helping.

"And the eleven pages with no names," he said. "He will not name the four."

"He made the same speech he made in the room above the chandler's and he made it better, because he has had a month to think about which of the two things he wanted more, and what he wanted more was to be the man who said it first." Tamsin shut the book. "He said a client who wants a sentence does not give his name, and a man who does not give his name pays cash, and that he has never refused a cash customer in his life. And then he said the thing I have written at the top of the page in his own words, and I am going to read it to you because I want you to hear it in the right voice."

She read it.

> *I have never once been able to find out who owns the house on that row and I have never tried, and I want it entered that I never tried, because trying would have cost me about four years of trade, and I did the arithmetic in the first year and I decided against it and I have been the better-paid man ever since. If it comes out that I knew, tell them I knew in the first year and told nobody, and that is worse than the other thing and it should be. I would rather be the man who did not look than the man who looked and got nothing for it, and there are eleven pages in here with no name in them and that is not a trick. It is a man being paid in not knowing.*

Marek sat with that for a while.

"He has made his own carefulness into evidence," he said.

"He has made his own carefulness into a matter of record, in his own hand, in a cell, with a magistrate and a roll-keeper, and it is the first thing he has said in thirty-one years that is worth more to anybody than it is to him," said Tamsin. "And I have written down, as he asked, that he would rather be the man who did not look, and I have written down that it is worth very little to us without the other thirty-one years, and I have not been kind about it, and I was not going to be, and I told him so before I wrote it."

---

The magistrate wanted the statement received. The statement could not be received as it stood, and the reason was not anybody's fault and could not be fixed by anybody in the room, and Marek had known it would be and had gone on hoping.

A statement that names a place, and a mark, and two districts, and no person, is not evidence against two districts. It is a broker describing where he buys plates, and a broker's account of a purchase is a broker's word, and a magistrate in a district house has no more power to act on a word about Glasswater and Emberwatch than a chandler has to move a bench.

Which left the request, and the request was Marek's, and the narrowing was the price of the statement.

"You can have the statement," said the magistrate, "and you can have it received and minuted and held, and it is worth having, and I have read it twice and I am telling you it is worth having. And I cannot hold it against a district four hundred miles away on a prisoner's word, and if I tried the district would come back with a writ and I would lose it, and then the eleven pages would be an argument in a room and not a record."

"I understand."

"Then understand the rest of it, which is that a request that accuses no one and identifies no person is a request that a higher office can answer by doing nothing, for a year, without anybody being able to say that it was refused." She put the pages down. "You want it to say something. Name a thing it can act on."

Marek looked at the nine paragraphs he had written by hand on the eighteenth of the ninth month and signed and dated, with a young man's initials in the middle of the last page.

He had not named the two provincial entries as places where a practice was carried on, because he had been frightened of exactly this, and Tamsin had told him on the quay that a request which cannot be answered by a person cannot be answered at all, and he had heard her, and he had named them anyway as entries, and the compromise had just cost him a cell and a morning.

He wrote a short amendment, on a sheet of the district's paper, and the clerk stamped it and entered it, and the second paragraph of the amendment read: that the request be read as being confined to the sale of copied witness marks and the supply of pressed matrices within the district of Auremar, the entries at Glasswater and Emberwatch being reserved and not abandoned.

"Reserved and not abandoned," Tamsin said, reading it over his shoulder in the corridor.

"Yes."

"That is a sentence a person can be run over by in ten years."

"It is," said Marek, "and I have just signed it, and I am telling you now rather than when you ask, and I want you to write the time on it, because I would like there to be a record of the fact that I signed it and told you in the same hour."

She wrote the time on it, and then she said, "And when the thing comes back in, with a number on it, and somebody in another province holding a piece of paper with our four lines on it?"

"Then I put it back in," Marek said, "in writing, on a different ground, and in a document of my own making, and I will not put a thing in a document of yours again without your name at the top of it, and you may strike that out of my mouth in the corridor if I do."

---

He walked down Candlewright Lane at the eighth hour of the evening with the district's copy of the amendment under his arm, and the lane was doing what the lane did at the eighth hour, which was shutting, and the counting house two floors down from the Chancery had its lamps lit, and he looked at its door from the bottom of the stairs and it was an ordinary door with an ordinary plate on it, and he had walked past that plate a hundred times.

He had found a criminal. It was a good find and a clean one, and the ledger was in a room in this building and a man was in custody who would be tried on the ledger and on a box with forty-one leaves in it against a docket of forty-three, and four of the eleven had shut, and a woman who wrote wills for dock labourers had been told by a man in a good coat to find somebody else, and a girl of seventeen had a name in a document because of him.

He had done something about it. That was the strange part. For thirty days he had been a man with a stamped request in a drawer, and now he was a man who had caught somebody, and the sentence that had put a child in a surety line had gone from being a thing a frightened magistrate said in a hurry to being a line in a broker's own price list four hundred and forty miles from here, and neither of those was a thing he could hold.

He stood at the bottom of the stair with the district's copy under his arm and worked out, standing up, on a dark lane, the only thing the day had actually given him. There were two entries in that ledger with a district against them instead of an address. One of them was Glasswater and one of them was Emberwatch, and Emberwatch was four hundred and forty miles away and had a frost on the coach road and had never in the whole of his life been written to by anybody in this building, and the request he had signed that afternoon, with a young clerk's time written beside his signature, had just made sure that it stayed that way for another month.

Then he went down the lane, past the counting house with the ordinary plate on the ordinary door, and the candle in the chandler's shop had gone out by the time he got to the bottom of the stairs, and the bar was still on the door, and the ninety-one leaves were in the district store under a number.
