# Chapter 24: Four Questions

The fourth term had survived a whole month in the lane and the reason it had survived was that Ivo Harker had the best voice in the district, and the reason it was going to fail was about the same thing.

Nell watched it happen on the second of the twelfth month, at the fifth hour, in the cold, with nine people standing round a trestle table and a piece of paper in Coll Fenner's hand.

*Any person who thereafter assumes a continuing field-side duty must sign a new obligation for it.*

Coll read it out, slowly, in the plain way, and stopped, and a man at the back said, "Say that again."

So Coll said it again, exactly the same, and the man said, "Say it slower."

And Coll Fenner, twenty-eight years old, off the Reed Cut bank, a man who had made four of the twenty-six public copies on the back of a seed tally while arguing about this very term and had been right for the wrong reasons, did the third thing that any reader in any room in any empire does when a listener asks for it again, which was to say it a third time, a little louder, in exactly the same words.

And the man said thank you and went away, and the woman beside him said, "I have understood it perfectly and I could not tell you one word of it."

Nell Kest stood at the end of the trestle table and worked out, in about four seconds, that the reading table had just become a window with no clerk behind it, which was the only kind of window in the building that was worse.

The woman was about sixty and had been to the table nine times since the twelfth of the ninth month and had never once said she could not read, and the reason she never said it was that she has a good memory for voices and no reading at all, and it had never occurred to anybody, least of all the woman, that these were different things.

---

They worked on it for two days in the dye-house workroom with the vats cold, because it is the only room in Greyfen that has a table long enough and a person who does not mind being wrong in it.

Tamsin Rook came out on the second evening with the term in fair hand and a slate, and Nell said, "Do not read it to me."

"I am not going to read it to you."

"You are going to read it to me in about four minutes in that voice you use in hearings and I am telling you now that I will understand every word of it and be no use whatever afterwards, and so will that woman, and so will about half of this lane."

They tried four versions and threw three away. The first was shorter, and a short term is still a term. The second was expanded, and a term with words added to it is worse than a term without them, because now there are two things and a person is being asked to hold both. The third was a summary, and a summary is exactly the door, and Tamsin said that out loud and put the third one in the stove.

The fourth one worked and it worked for a stupid reason, which is that it had nothing to do with the fourth term.

---

It was a woman of about seventy at the seed barns who did it, and her name was not asked for, and she was asked for a second day, and she came back.

Nell put it to her as a game, because Nell is a person who has been eleven sentences ahead of everybody in a room since she was fourteen, and the fastest way to get a plain question out of an old woman is to pretend it is a game.

"You've had a paper," said Nell. "A man brought a paper. I want to ask you four things about it and I don't want you to tell me what you think it means, I want you to tell me what is in it. All right?"

"Four things. Go on."

"One. Is there work in it?"

"Cutting the ditch," said the woman at once. "There is a ditch in it."

"Right. Two. Whose name is on it?"

"His. The father." A pause. "Or the word. He says the word as well. *The present holder.*"

"Who is the present holder."

"The girl, then, and now not the girl, and that is the part I did not like." She looked up. "The word is a name that walks about. That is what it is. A word that walks about instead of a name that stands still."

"Yes. Three. If you stop doing the work, whose name answers for it?"

"Whose name answers," she said. "Then it is the walking word. It is the walking word that answers and not the girl. So the girl can stop, and the paper does not care that she stopped, and the ditch is still in it."

Nell wrote that down and did not change a word of it.

"Four. Who read it to you?"

The old woman thought about that one properly, which was longer than the other three together.

"Two," she said. "A woman from the house, and then a man from the office. The man had it in his hand before I had asked for it."

"And does he get paid if you sign it?"

"He gets paid either way," said the old woman. "That is the only one of the four I would have said by myself and I have not said it by myself. He gets paid either way, so he is not the one to ask whether you understand, because it makes no difference to him which way it goes."

---

They wrote it on a board in the lane on the sixth of the twelfth month, in chalk, on a board that had been the back of a seed tally and had a list of sowing days on the other side, and it is still legible under about four winters of weather, which is the whole of the immortality a chalkboard gets.

**1. Is there work in it?** A ditch. A gate. A barn. A bench. A boat. A load.

**2. Whose name is on it?** Yours, or a word that walks about, like *the holder*.

**3. If you stop, whose name answers for it?**

**4. Who read this to you — and do they get paid if you sign?**

It is four questions. It is not a law, and Tamsin wrote that on the back of the board in ink, and then rubbed half of it out with her thumb, and what was left was *it is not a law and it is not advice and it is what a person asks a paper*.

There is nothing in it that runs. It wants no inheritance, no continuation, no guarantor, no record, no seal and no court, and that is the whole of why it travels. A seven-year-old can carry it down a lane in her head and a woman who has never learned to read can say it out loud, and it is short, and it costs nothing.

The second thing that was written on the back of the board, and which is the reason the board exists in a lane an hour from the vats, was the fourth question, and the four of them were put there in this order on purpose: the fourth is the one that decides whether the other three can be trusted. A man who is paid either way is not a person to ask a frightened woman whether she understands a word, and the lane had been asking frightened women, for a month, in a good voice, for nothing.

---

The levy sheet came off the table leg that same evening.

Nell had hammered it on with two nails and it had been read by everybody in the lane and laughed at, and on the sixth she took it off and put a board on the frame of the trestle instead, and on the board, under the four questions, there is a list of names in chalk, and the list is not a rota, and the only rule on it is the rule that anybody may add their own name and nobody may take another person's off.

That is a different thing from a rota. A rota is four people who have agreed to be there, and a rota is a promise about an afternoon. A list of names that people add themselves to is a statement about a whole lane, and it has no owner, and it cannot be thanked, and it has not got a woman standing behind it, and that last part is going to cost her something she has not priced yet.

The chalk rule stayed exactly as it was. A paper may be brought to that table and read out loud in the plain words, and no advice is given, because that is not what this table is. What was added, in a different colour of chalk, was that anybody may ask the four questions of a paper, in any order, and that a person who does not understand one of them has said something useful and not something naughty.

Ostyn Vare came down the road on the seventh and stood at the table for a while and read the four questions, which he has read twice, and then he said the thing that had been hanging over the lane since the ninth of the tenth.

"I have been audited," he said.

"I know."

"Not the bad kind. The kind where a man comes with a book and reads the exemption and asks who signed the amendment and when." Vare put his hands in his coat. "Nell. I have been the person in this lane you brought a paper to for nineteen years, and I am not going to be able to be that again for a season, and I want to say the sentence myself instead of waiting for somebody to work it out and say it to my face."

"Go on."

"A foreman who has been audited stops being the person anybody brings a paper to," said Vare. "Because the first thing a man thinks when he has got a paper and a foreman in the same room is: the foreman will be asked. Not the man. The foreman. And after that nobody in the room says a word, and I have watched it happen in my own workroom twice this year with a different man in my chair."

"Who do they bring it to now?"

"They bring it to a woman off the vats who reads out loud and does not want anything," said Vare, "and they do not bring it to me, and they are right not to, and you have four questions on a board that say so in writing, which I would not have thought of and which I am going to go and say out loud in four houses I know of."

He did it that week, on his own two days, in four workrooms, and it is the reason the amended clause got into four more schedules before the middle of the month, and he did not mention it to the guild and the guild did not ask him to.

---

She took the paid work on the seventh of the twelfth and it was the best-paid thing she had ever done.

The amended clause had to be copied by hand into the schedule of every house that signed it, in the assessors' own wording, on their own form, because a form is not an amendment until it is in the schedule of the house it governs, and There are eight houses on the dyers' lane and four of them had signed, and the nine pounds a quarter of the levy had to be collected before the assessment in the fourth month and written out against each name.

Nell went round it herself, on her two afternoons, and it took four days, and at the Caudle house at the end of the lane the woman came out with the money already counted into a cloth because she had been expecting her since the twenty-ninth of the tenth.

"Two pounds and five and a quarter," said Nery Caudle. "And I want it written out, with the quarter on it, and the date, and I want it put in a book and not on a slate, because a slate is weather."

So Nell wrote it out on the back of the clause, and dated it the ninth of the twelfth, and read it back, and Nery Caudle made her read the figure a second time, and then Nell put it in the book with the other three houses' shares against their names, and the book was a levy book with a wage schedule inside it and it is the reason four houses on a lane can prove what they paid.

"I am not going to tell you that was fair," Nell said, in the yard, with the cloth still in the woman's hand. "You paid two pounds and five and a quarter out of one wage for a clause that protects a girl you have never met in a house four days from this one, because a man in an office with a book was frightened of a form."

"I know what I paid for," said Nery Caudle. "I wrote it on the back in my own words. Did you read it back twice."

"Twice."

"Then that is the difference between the two of us," said Nery Caudle, "and there is not a clerk in Auremar who could put it better than that."

---

The thing that happened in the second week of the twelfth was that the table did not need her, and she found that out in the worst possible way, which is that she was four days in bed with the damp in her chest and could not get down the lane at all, and it went on.

She came down it on the fifth day, expecting the yard to be standing still, and it was not. The board was up. The four questions were in a second colour of chalk under the first, which meant somebody had wanted them bigger. And there were more names in the list than there had been a week before, and two of them were children, and there was a line under the four questions in the smallest and neatest hand in the lane: *I read one out on Wednesday and I did not tell anybody what to do about it, like the rule says. — the woman from the seed barn.*

Nell Kest stood in the lane mouth in the dark and looked at the board and had the very specific feeling, which she took about four minutes to identify and then refused to soften, of being unnecessary.

It was not a slight. She made herself say that out loud in her own head, twice, on the walk up to the house, because she had watched four women in this lane be quietly ruined by a man being indispensable and she was not going to be one of them at twenty. And the part she missed, and missed more than she had expected to, was not the board. It was that on one of the four days a woman had come down the lane at the fifth hour with a paper in her hand, and there had been somebody at the table, and that somebody had read it to her, and nobody had written down that she had ever come, and there was no version of the fourth question that asks a paper who else was in the room.

She wrote that down in her own book that night in four lines, and they were: *The table runs without me. That is what I wanted and it is not what I wanted. On Thursday a woman came about a paper and somebody read it to her who did not know who had brought it, and the only thing that would have helped is a person at the end of the table with a book who writes down who came, and I do not have anybody to be that, and it is not the table's job.*

Then she put the book down and went to bed, and the chest was still bad, and the doctor at the landing was a penny a visit and she had four pounds in a cloth in a drawer and was not going.

---

The letter came with the evening post on the fifteenth of the twelfth month and a boy brought it to the table, because the table was where letters were brought, and there was by then a person at the table who knew that.

It was one sheet, on paper from a place with a different cut to it, and it was addressed to *the table in the lane, and whoever keeps the copies*, which was an address Nell had never seen a letter use, and it asked for a copy of a form, and it asked for the four questions as well, and it was signed, and the signature at the foot was a woman's hand and a name and a place.

*From Iselle Oster, at Millbrook Reach, a cooper's house, three hundred miles down the water. I am writing because a woman on a landing told me there was a lane up here that reads papers out loud and will not tell you what to do. I am not writing about a paper of my own. I am writing about a paper of my husband's shop's, and I want to say first that the shop has lost the gauge, and the gauge is the master's gauge, and my son cannot be examined for the trade without it, and it went on a bond on the fourth of the twelfth month, and it is the fifteenth today, and nobody has yet told me that it was not a debt.*

Nell read it twice and then took it inside and put it on the vats flat with a stone on it, and stood there for a while with her hands flat on the stone.

A gauge. A thing of iron with a face on it, in a drawer, the size of a hand, worth nothing to anybody who is not a cooper.

And nobody four hundred miles from a court in Auremar knew it was gone.
