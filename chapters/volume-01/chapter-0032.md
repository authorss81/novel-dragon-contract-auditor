# Chapter 32: What the Court Can Do

A limited sitting is a thing a court grants to make an argument smaller, and the granting is done in writing, and the writing is what you actually go and get.

Marek Kest had spent four days in the third week of the second month trying to get it, and had learned the shape of it, and the shape of it was this. A limited sitting is not a hearing. A hearing is asked for by a person with a grievance. A limited sitting is asked for by a person who has a document and has been told he cannot use it, and it is granted because a court that will not hear a narrowed thing ends up hearing a wide thing, and a wide thing is a thing that gets appealed.

The application went up the river on the eighteenth of the second month in three pages, and came back on the twenty-second with nine lines on it in Nevin Trost's small firm hand, and the nine lines were the most useful thing that had happened to Marek in two months, because every one of them was a wall and the walls were drawn accurately.

> *A limited sitting may be granted in the Venn matter, listed on this court's own sitting list, and heard by the court, for the following purposes and no others.*
>
> *First: to hear a sworn statement made by a party to the instrument of the nineteenth of the second month of the year four years back, as to that party's own duty under it, in a form this court will set.*
>
> *Second: to record what a party says about that party's own duty, and to enter the statement in the minute book, and to give the parties copies.*
>
> *It may not order disclosure of any document. It may not strike, void, amend, or suspend the correction filed on the ninth of the eighth month. It may not touch the series MC/FS, or any standing instruction of this or any other district, or any return made under any instrument sealed with the fire-and-water device. It may not be described afterwards as an audit, a review, a hearing of the application, or a decision on any question. It may not be cited in any other matter.*
>
> *On the application of the applicant, it may not be used to support any application that is not made in this court and on this file.*

The last line had a small mark beside it, and the mark was Trost's, and under the mark in the same hand was: *The seventh line is mine. I have added it because you asked for six and I think you wanted the seventh and did not know it. — N.T.*

---

He read it eleven times over two days and got the same thing out of it every time, and the thing was smaller than it had been on the fourteenth of the ninth month and larger than it had been on the twentieth of the eleventh, and both of those were the correct size for what he had at the time.

On the fourteenth of the ninth month his best available outcome had been a process that somebody else would run, and it had taken three people and a water office and a magistrate's nine minutes to build it.

His best available outcome now was one sentence, in a form that could not be reissued, made by a person who could not be compelled to make it and might refuse.

That was the whole of the narrowing, and it went on one side of a piece of paper, and what was on the other side was everything he had wanted in December: the name of the person who signed the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth, the office that decided in ten days to take a sentence out of a lane and set it in type, the quarterly return that made the honest answer small, the authority behind the sealed instruction, and the nine paragraphs themselves, which were in a folder in a room two floors up in a building he could walk to in nine minutes and could not get into.

He had a piece of paper in his coat with a corner of it folded over, and the corner of it had been folded over since the thirteenth of the twelfth month, and it was four lines in Tamsin Rook's hand, and it said that a quarterly return sealed with a fire-and-water device made the honest answer small and that a small truthful number is more useful to a machine than a large lie, and on the back of it, in the same hand, that she was not agreeing with anything on the front of it and that she would collect it.

He had been carrying it a hundred and ten days and had not read it again in fifty, because he knew it by heart, and because every time he took it out he found himself looking for a way to be right about the worst reason in the world.

---

What he could not get past, in the anteroom and afterwards and on the stairs, was the fourth of the nine lines.

It may not be described afterwards as an audit.

He had been an auditor in exactly one way since the seventh of the ninth month, which was that he had held a piece of paper up in a room and read it out and found the join in it. He had never claimed a stage and had never wanted one, and had spent four months being extremely careful about the words he used in public, and had gone on saying *audit* in private to Tamsin and to himself about nine thousand times, and the word had been the load-bearing wall of his entire inner life since the seventh of the ninth.

And a court in a river province had now written, in a small firm hand with a mark beside it, that when this thing is finished nobody may call it an audit, in this file or in any other, for ever.

He sat on the third step of the stair outside the anteroom for about four minutes with that.

It was not a punishment. It was the most reasonable thing in the nine lines, and if it had not been there the minute book in six months would say *audit* in the heading, and a clerk would file a matter of the Chancery under audits, and an audit is a thing an office does, and an office doing one has a file number and a year and a heading, and then in four years somebody reviews it and the review has a form.

What he could not get past was that he had walked in on the seventh of the ninth with a piece of paper and read out the difference between two versions of a sentence, and that the difference was real, and that he was now going to have to find out whether a thing that was true on the seventh of the ninth was still true in a room in the first week of the fourth month without ever once being allowed to use the word that said it was the same kind of finding.

He wrote in his own book, on the stair, in his own hand, the single sentence he had been keeping sentences in since the fifth of the ninth month, and it was:

*A finding that cannot be named in the record is still a finding, and it is worth less in the room and the same in the street, and the whole of the work from here is making sure that the street is where it is done.*

And then, four lines under it, because he had promised himself in the eleventh month that he would write down anything that was the opposite of what he wanted:

*And if the street is the only place it can be used, then a person who can stand in the street is worth more than a clerk, and there are about forty of them, and I am not one of them, and I have spent four months building things for them and calling it work.*

He got up off the step and went and asked Pell Vey to certify a list of parties.

---

The application went down the corridor to the anteroom outside the Table's gallery on the twenty-fourth of the second month, at the second hour, with Pell Vey on one side of it and the young clerk of the intake desk on the other, who had come because a thing that is certified has to be certified in front of somebody.

"The parties to a scale matter about a Table filing are certified by the head of the Table," said Pell, without sitting down. "Read me the list."

Marek read him the list. The Mosswake Roll Court and its clerk. The estate and household of Aldren Venn, and Mara Venn and Tavi Venn as the persons holding. The Reed Cut district water office as the party holding the gate schedule. The Standing Office of Provincial Continuities, as the office whose series the applicant is not asking this court to touch, entered for the purposes of notice. The applicant.

"That is four parties and an office and a dead man's estate," said Pell.

"Yes."

"And you have left out the person you want to hear." Pell put his finger on the second page. "The water keeper is not a party on this list. She is a counterparty to the instrument, and a counterparty is notified and not joined, and there is a form for the notification, and the form is not yours and is not mine and is kept by the water office that holds the counterparties, because a district water office has kept a book of the scale instruments it has countersigned since before either of us."

"Then somebody in this building can send it."

"Nobody in this building can send it," said Pell Vey. "A dragon is not served. She is notified, by the office that keeps her counterparties, in a form that office has used for nineteen years, and it goes out on a district water office's own paper, and it does not come back with a seal on it that means anything to us, and it comes back with her answer or it does not come back at all."

He said it without any weight on it at all, the way a man states a thing about hinges.

Then he said the other thing, and Marek had been waiting nine days for it.

"I want to say one thing about the seventh line and then I am going to my gallery and I am not coming back."

"Go on."

"That seventh line is Trost's, and it is correct, and it is going to cost you more than the other six." Pell Vey picked up the application and did not look at it. "If you list the Office of Provincial Continuities as a party for the purposes of notice, on a sitting that cannot touch its series, then a person in that office reads the minute book in six months — and there is no person, which is the entire reason it is on your list — and the reading is done by whichever clerk opens the book for something else. And that clerk will find a minute book with a Table filing in it and a series number in the heading and no decision, and he will put a note on the front of the file that says the matter concerns a series, and then in two years there will be an audit of this file, and the audit will be about the series, and it will not be your audit and you will not be in it and you will not be able to object to a single word of it."

Marek Kest stood in an anteroom of the Roll Hall and found that the wall he had been looking at had a door in it that he had not seen, and that the door was made by the act of asking for a notice.

"Take them off the list," he said.

"Take them off the list and you have an instrument with two living parties and a dead man's estate, and the record will say the applicant asked about a form and got an answer about a duty, and there will be no form in the file at all, and no clerk in six months will ever have any reason whatsoever to open it." Pell put the application down. "That is the trade. I have been signing things under this Table for nineteen years and I have watched the same thing happen four times, and it is always the notice that does it, and it is never the argument."

And then Marek nearly asked him about the yard.

It was right there. It was the second hour of the afternoon of the twenty-fourth of the second month, in a room where the only other person was a clerk of twenty-five who was writing the time in a margin, and Pell Vey had just told him, unprompted, that a dragon is notified and not served, and had told him where the form is, and had said the word *counterparty* out loud in a room, and had not once asked Marek a single question about anything.

The question had been in Marek Kest's mouth for about nine seconds. It was the question of the fourth of the tenth month, and the answer to it was in a copyist's own book, dated four days after the conversation, in full, with the hour and the place, and a line written under it about a sentence being one too many.

If he asked, Pell would find out within the hour that his roll-keeper had kept a record of him, which she had a right to do and had told nobody, and which Marek had been refused. If he asked, he would be a man disclosing to a supervisor that his colleague had a file on him. And if he did not ask, then for the rest of this matter, the only conversation in which anybody in this building had ever come close to saying what the office knew would be a conversation nobody in the office had ever had.

He did not ask.

He had promised her a rule in the thirteenth of the twelfth, and the rule was that neither of them moves a thing about this file without a name on it, and asking a supervisor a question in an anteroom with a clerk in it is not a way of getting a name on it.

"Certify the list," Marek said, "and I will take the counterparty notification up to Reed Cut myself and leave it with the clerk there, and he will send it on his own paper, and it will come back on his own paper, and I will put both of them in front of the court on the day, and neither of them will mean anything to anybody who has never been to that district."

"That is exactly right," said Pell Vey, "and I taught you none of it, which I find harder to bear than the rest."

He went to the gallery, and the young clerk of the intake desk wrote the time in the margin in ink and initialled it, because nobody asked him to and because he is the only person in this building who has ever done that twice for the same man.

---

Marek took the list back down to the alcove at the fourth hour and put it on the frame in front of Tamsin Rook, who read the nine lines and the small mark and Trost's note, and then read the two lists again.

"You have taken the office off the list."

"About an hour ago."

"You have also written a second page," said Tamsin. "Which is a list of what this application does not ask for, and it is dated the twenty-fourth, and your name is on it, and you wrote it before you took the office off the list, because the order of the ink is the other way round."

Marek did not say anything.

"You narrowed this yourself."

"On the twenty-first. I wrote the list of things I am not asking for before anybody asked me to and before I knew whether the sitting would be granted, because in October I narrowed the audit request by my own signature and that worked, and because if I have to be asked what I am not asking for then somebody else decides what I am not asking for, and they will decide it in a way that is generous to them."

"That is a very good reason." Tamsin Rook put the sheet down flat on the frame. "And you did it in an anteroom at the second hour and came down here at the fourth and told me about it in the same conversation, which is two hours and a coffee's walk, and I want you to hear the second half of that sentence, because the first half is a compliment."

"I heard it."

"Listen to it. In October you withheld a packet for nine days and I found out because you were not able to help it. In November you withheld a fact for nine days and told me afterwards, on purpose, because the letter to Rell had gone out, and I have forgiven that and I will not forgive it twice. And this week you have done a third version of it, and this version is the one you would have got away with, because it is in writing, and it is filed, and there is your name on it, and nobody could ever say you hid it."

"I did not hide it."

"You told me two hours after you had decided, in a room, having already written it down and had it witnessed. That is not telling me. That is handing me a document and hoping I read it as a confidence." Tamsin Rook pushed the sheet two inches towards him. "You are getting better at this. That is what I want you to notice. In October you hid a thing because you were frightened of me. In November you hid a thing because you had a plan. This week you did a thing at the fourth hour and told me at the fourth hour, and the only thing that has changed is that you are now fluent about the moment."

Marek picked up the sheet and read his own second page, which was a list of nine things he was not asking for, in his own hand, with the date on it, and it was a good list, and he understood, reading it, that it was about a third of the reason the request was going to survive.

"Write the rule down," said Tamsin. "The way we did the other one, with the date on it. Because I am not going to trust a habit, and I have learned that I cannot trust a good instinct either, and the difference between them is that one of them can be written down and the other one has to be believed."

---

The application went up the river again on the twenty-sixth of the second month. It listed four parties and a dead man's estate. It listed nine things it was not asking for, and the ninth of the nine was the audit request of the eighteenth of the ninth and its supplement of the eighth of the eleventh, and it said in one line that the applicant did not withdraw either, and that the filing of this application had no effect on them.

Marek read that line back twice before he sent it, because it was the first sentence he had written in four months that was entirely about keeping a thing alive.

The Mosswake Roll Court granted the limited sitting on the second of the third month, listed it on its own sitting list, and set it for the first week of the fourth.

And the last thing in the scoping note was not a wall at all. It was a direction, and it read:

> *To hear a sworn statement as to a party's own duty under the instrument, the court requires the name of the person who is to make it. A statement in scale form is made once. A party who has made one cannot be asked to make it again in the same matter, and any person who proposes to be asked twice should be asked once and asked well.*

And the only name in nine provinces who could answer the question *whose duty did this instrument create* was the woman who had held the Reed Cut gate for seven years, who had been at the channel in the ninth month of the ninth year, whose name was in the third operative term of a ruling that had been read aloud in a lane to about two hundred people, and who could not be summoned, served, cited or produced by any part of this building.

The form for her notification was in a book in a low building three hundred miles down the water, and it was kept by a clerk of the district water office, and it was a district's own paper, and it was nineteen years old.
