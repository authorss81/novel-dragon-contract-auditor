# Chapter 31: The Column That Does Not Tidy

There are six things on a line of Tamsin Rook's list and she has only ever written five, and the sixth is the reason a holder did not answer, and she is not going to invent it.

The five are who holds the copy, in whose hand it is, of what — and there are only three whats, and there will not be a fourth — the date it was made, and whether that holder has said they will read it aloud to anybody who comes to the house with a paper. The sixth would be *why not*, and every time she has been tempted to write it she has worked out within about a minute that she does not know it, and a list of guesses in a copyist's private book is a list of lies with a date on them.

So the list has forty-one lines and thirty-eight addresses on it and ten people at the bottom of the fourth column who have not answered, and the fourth column is not tidy, and she decided on the second day of the second month that it would not be.

---

She could account for five of the ten in a sentence each, and she did it in a copyist's alcove in the Roll Hall with the book open on the frame and the stove going, and the sentences were short and true.

One was a woman in Greyfen with four children and no candle, who had answered in the first month and then stopped, because a second letter is a bill. One was in Reed Cut and had stopped answering letters after the seventh of the twelfth for a reason that had a date and a reason attached to it. One was a man who had moved forty miles and got a forwarding address off a neighbour and got the forwarding address wrong, and Tamsin went and got it right on the fourteenth, which took a whole afternoon and a shilling of a boy's time, and wrote again, and on the fourth of the third month his answer came back, with his new address and his house number and a note saying he was sorry about the first one.

One was a man who had been in a bed in the ninth month and whose wife had written back a fortnight later to say he was better and would answer when he was, and had not, and that one sat in the column with a date against it and a reason, because the reason was in a woman's hand and had been given to her.

The remaining one of the five was a house in a row off a Kiln road in Auremar where the copy had been taken by a man who had not put it anywhere and where the people living there now had not known until a boy from the Roll Hall knocked on the door in the first month and asked. He had promised to put it in a drawer and had not, and Tamsin wrote *promised, not confirmed* and left it.

The tenth she had never asked at all, because she had never had an address for him and had not had one since the September, and a person you have never asked cannot be said to have declined to answer, and she had put him in the column anyway, which she knew was wrong and had left in, because the column was where the not-answers went and there was nowhere else on the sheet for him.

And then there were four, and they were together, and she could not do them in a sentence.

---

What she had of Emberwatch was one sheet of good paper with nobody's name on it, and it had been in the case file since the fourth of the eleventh month, when Ivet Sarn had admitted it in the Mosswake matter on her own authority and copied it to the parties, and it had travelled four hundred and forty miles on the strength of a word underlined in a clerk's hand.

The endorsement was on an instrument belonging to a dyers' house. The endorsement was itself ordinary: a printed model, filled in, in the box that says *the person who holds*, which is the box in a model endorsement that everybody reads and nobody argues about, because the whole point of the model is that the person who holds the thing is the person who owes for it.

What was not ordinary was the filing. The endorsement had been entered **itemised**, with a number, and under the number a clerk had written the single word *Itemised* and underlined it, for the next reviewer, in the ordinary way that a careful clerk underlines a thing he has done on purpose so that the next person does not think it was an accident.

Tamsin had looked at that underline about nine times in three months and had never been able to put it next to anything.

She put it next to it now.

The form that says a filing of that kind is to be entered in the routine column and not itemised, for the purpose of the quarterly return, is a quarterly return. It is a page a district office fills in four times a year and sends to a dragon seat four hundred miles away, and it is distributed to district courts and landing offices that hold a flood docket, and a dyers' trade in a furnace town does not hold a flood docket, because there is no flood in a town of chimneys on a pass.

Which meant that the clerk of that combination had never been issued the instruction, had never seen the form, and had never in his life been told by anybody that the correct way to enter a filled-in model endorsement on a house instrument was to give it no number at all.

He had done it properly. He had done the careful thing, the thing a clerk does when a person brings him a paper and he wants the next person to be able to see that he looked at it. He had numbered it, and then he had written *Itemised* underneath and drawn a line under it so that whoever opened the book after him would know it was deliberate.

And he had thereby made the only trace, in the whole of the record, that the four houses in that trade had ever existed.

Tamsin wrote that down in the margin of the list and then read it back and found it was not quite the sentence she wanted, and tried again: *The whole of the damage in Emberwatch has been done by people following forms. The only paper in the town that anybody can find was made by a man being more careful than his form required. The two facts are the same fact and I have been keeping them in different places for three months.*

And then she wrote the sentence underneath it that turned the rest of the afternoon upside down, which she did not see for another six days and which arrived on the twenty-fourth in a letter from a woman in the next province:

*The trouble with a district that obeys a form is that it obeys the form it was given. The trouble with a trade that has never had a dispute is that it has never been given a form at all, and has made one, and keeps it, and it is a good one.*

---

Emberwatch is a furnace town in the uplands, four hundred and forty miles from Auremar, up the coach road and over a pass that is shut by snow for about five weeks a winter, and the road itself is the reason a coach takes four days each way. It is a town of chimneys and coke carts and a dyers' trade that took the water out of the air in the nineteenth year of a reign nobody alive remembers, and it has about two thousand people, and it has never in its history had a newspaper that anybody in the capital reads.

Tamsin Rook had a window, a hearing slate with thirty-one names on it and eleven days left on the trial, a stove, a chest under a winter coat with the list in it, and a week's wages off out of the second month. She wrote the four addresses in the fourth week of the first month, when the confirmations went out, and she addressed all four to the same lane.

The lane has no numbers. She knew that in the way everybody knows a thing they have never seen: the district returns a book of addresses, and the book gives a lane and a trade and, where a lane has one trade, a word for the door. So the four letters went to *the fourth house past the weigh-house on Sillrow, dyers*, and the postal clerk at Emberwatch would have had to walk down a lane in the dark to find out which fourth house past the weigh-house, and there is more than one weigh-house in a furnace town.

And she could not go. Not only the four days each way and the money — the money was the small part, she could have found four shillings — but that a person cannot be in two places and she had spent three months finding out that the whole of her method was somebody standing in a room. A woman of twenty-four who walks into a furnace town four hundred and forty miles from anywhere with a book and no authority is a person from a decision, and the one instruction she had been given about that by a woman in Glasswater with a husband's stick in the crook of her elbow was in the second week of the second month and had not faded.

And there was the other thing, which she wrote in the margin because it was the reason she did not send the letters, and then did not send the letters.

The last person who wrote to that house about an instrument was a man from a factor's office at the far end of a row of counting rooms, in the tenth month, and what came back was a form with a thumb on a line.

Confirming an address is a promise. She had learned that in October from a man who offered three documents back out of an inventory on the understanding that the copyist's record of them was altered, and the shape of it was the same: a woman in a lane says a thing is hers, and a stranger comes to her door about it, and the stranger is a fact in her life that never goes away.

So she put the four letters in a drawer and did not send them, and wrote a fifth, and posted the fifth on the sixth of the second month at four shillings and a halfpenny, which was a rider and not a coach, and which was the largest single sum she had spent on this since the two shillings and ninepence of paper.

The fifth letter went sixty miles to a town in the next province, to a woman of about fifty who was the standing assessor of a Weavers' and Small Dyemasters' Association with eleven houses, and who had come sixty miles in a cart in the first month with a copy of the amended clause in a leather case, and who had worked out in about eleven seconds what the fifth line on the back of the craft exemption form did to a house with a boy on a term, and who had written the fourth question out at the top of a letter to eleven houses without saying where it came from.

It asked one question.

*Who is the person in Emberwatch that a person in a dyers' house goes to, before anybody else, about an instrument?*

That was all of it. Tamsin Rook did not ask about the four houses, because if the answer came back about the four houses then four people in a furnace town would know in a fortnight that a woman in Auremar was asking after them, and she had spent three months arguing with a district about not being allowed to be a person that anybody could find.

---

The answer took eighteen days, which is what a rider post of four hundred and forty miles costs in the way that matters, and Tamsin was in the alcove for all of them and did not get anything else done.

She did the Greyfen copyist's two hundred and forty schedules, because the guild was still paying for the word-by-word and there would be no money in the fourth month. She filled in a fair hand's worth of an assistant registrar's index that had nothing to do with anything, and did it badly, and was told so. She read the answer in her own book — the Millbrook one, the one with no seal on it — four times, which is two more than it needs, and wrote nothing new in it.

And on the fourth of the third month the man who had moved came back, and the column went from ten to nine, and she wrote the date against his name and put the column in order, because she is a woman who cannot leave a list untidy without paying for it, and the paying is nine days of thinking about four people in an upland town instead of the two hundred and eighty-four.

---

The answer came on the twenty-fourth of the second month, five days after the Venn term ran out, and it was four lines and an address, and the four lines were the reason nobody in a furnace town four hundred and forty miles away had ever written a letter about a court ruling.

> *The person is Bettin Rahl, standing assessor of the Emberwatch Dyers' Combination, whose room is over the weigh-house office on Sillrow and whose hours are the second bell to the sixth. You address a house in this trade through the Combination and not to the house.*
>
> *A house in a trade of dyers may not write to a court, a guild, a factor's office, a district office, or a person it has not dealt with before, about an instrument, without the assessor's leave in writing first. The rule is thirty-one years old and it is in the Combination's book at the second leaf and it is the reason we have never had a dispute in this trade.*
>
> *I will say to you plainly, because you asked one question and you are entitled to a plain answer: the four you wrote to are one house. Ferren, the fourth past the weigh-house. Four of them wrote. None of them thought it was theirs to answer, because there is no such thing in this trade as a letter about a ruling. There is an instrument and there is a person, and the person is Rahl.*
>
> *If you want to reach the house you go through me, and I will carry it, and I will tell you what it will cost you and what it will cost them, and I will not tell you either until you ask me again.*

Tamsin read it four times, which is one more than it takes.

Then she sat for a while and worked out the thing she had actually been sent, which was not a name and an address.

It was the sentence about the rule. *A house in a trade may not write to anybody else about an instrument without the assessor's leave in writing first.* Thirty-one years old, in the book, at the second leaf.

A trade that has never had a dispute in forty years is not a trade where everybody gets on. It is a trade that has one door, and behind the door there is one person, and every question a house wants answered has to go through that person, and that person can say no, and if he says no the house has no second place to go, because the second place is a court and the house is not allowed to write to one.

And that is not fear. Nothing in Emberwatch is frightened. There is no threat in it and no danger in it and nobody in that town has ever been warned about anything. It is a rule the trade wrote for itself, thirty-one years ago, for a good reason, and the good reason was that a dyers' trade in a furnace town one hundred and forty years ago had three disputes in a year and lost half its work over them, and somebody decided that the way to stop a trade from being torn apart over a paper was to make sure only one man could read the papers.

It works. It has worked for forty years. It has kept the trade whole.

And what it has done, with nothing in it anybody could point at, is that a house of four women in the fourth house past a weigh-house has held four copies of a court ruling since the twentieth of the ninth month, and has read them, and has understood them, and has had four questions it could have asked anybody, and has had nowhere on earth to ask them, and does not know that it has nowhere, because nobody has ever had to tell it.

---

She put the name on the list on the twenty-fifth of the second month, in her own hand, in the fourth column, against nothing, because there is no line in the list for a name that has no address on it yet.

She wrote it as it came in. She did not put it in a new column and she did not move the four letters out of the drawer, and she did not write *not known to have received* across the top of the four, and she wrote in the margin of the page the sentence she had arrived at, which is the only finding she owns from a fortnight of waiting:

*Who in the world is helped by quiet. Answered in Emberwatch, in the negative, by a trade, on the twenty-fourth of the second month, and the answer was not fear.*

And underneath it, because she is honest in her own book and not only in other people's files:

*The list was made so that nobody would be alone in front of a paper. The list has found me the loneliest people in four provinces and I cannot get to any of them, and I am not going to be able to fix that by writing better letters.*

And then she put the book back in the chest under the winter coat, and went up to the third gallery and stood at the foot of the stairs for a minute, because there was one more thing in the fortnight she had not told anybody, and it was the reason the whole of it had taken twenty-two days instead of four, and it was sitting in a drawer eleven feet away from where she was standing with four envelopes in it that she had addressed and not sent.

---

Marek came down to the alcove on the twenty-sixth of the second month, at the seventh hour, and stood at the end of her bench with his coat still on, which meant he was going somewhere and had stopped on the way.

"You have not been in the alcove for three weeks."

"No."

"What have you been doing."

Tamsin Rook had had three weeks to think about how that question would be answered and had arrived, on the twenty-sixth, at the answer that was true and about two-thirds of the answer.

"I have been waiting for a letter from four hundred and forty miles away," she said, "and it came on the twenty-fourth, and it has a name in it, and the name is the standing assessor of the Emberwatch Dyers' Combination, and the four people who did not answer are one house, and I have written the name on the list."

That was all of it, and it was all true, and it was four-fifths of what she had done in three weeks.

Marek read the four lines on the card she had copied out for him — the rule about the assessor's leave, the thirty-one years, the book at the second leaf — and then read it a second time, which he did not often do with a thing that was about four hundred miles away.

"A house may not write to a court without a man's leave," he said. "In writing first."

"That is what it says."

"And it is not fear."

"It is not fear," said Tamsin. "That is the whole of the answer to a question I have been carrying since the twenty-second of the month, and I would have given a year of my wages for it to be fear."

He looked at the card for a while and then he said, "You could have got that in a fortnight if I had sent somebody."

"You could have got it in four days if I had gone, and I did not go, and I went on not going for three weeks because I could not work out how to arrive in a furnace town without turning four people into a fact in a book." She shut the card away from him, which was not unfriendly. "And there is a thing in the alcove drawer I have not told you about, and I have decided within about nine seconds of you standing on that bench that I am going to tell you about it in about three months, and not one word before, and you are going to sit there and work out for yourself that the number of things I am not telling you has gone up by one, which it has, and that I did it on purpose."

Marek Kest sat down on the end of a bench in the copying alcove of the third gallery of the Roll Hall and looked at her for a while.

"Three months," he said.

"Or the next time I can say it without it being about you."
