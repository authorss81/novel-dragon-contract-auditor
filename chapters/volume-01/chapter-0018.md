# Chapter 18: The Rota

The dye-house was in Greyfen, which was an hour down the road from the lane mouth where the reading table stood, and Nell had spent the whole of that week moving between the two of them and had stopped being able to hear the river by the time she got to the vats.

What she could still do, on the flat of the dye vat with a lump of chalk, was sums in her head that a professional could not do in a book.

She had three of them, and the fourth one was the one nobody wanted.

The first sum was the levy. The amendment had gone round the lane on a Tuesday with a man and a rack, and by the end of the first week the Marrowgate house had signed and the Beckery had asked for a copy to take to their own assessor and the Nine Elms had asked for four weeks and the Caudle house had said nothing at all to anybody, and the four of them between them had raised the levy on four houses by nine pounds a quarter.

The second sum was where nine pounds a quarter came out of. It came out of four places and it came out at a rate the four houses had agreed without anybody explaining it, and Nell did not think that was anybody's fault, because a levy is a levy and the alternative is a levy decided by whoever shouted loudest in a room with a rack in it.

The third sum was the Caudle house.

The fourth sum was the winter, and the fourth sum was the one she had got wrong twice before she got it right.

---

The reserve was spent. That was the district's phrase for it, and the reserve being spent did not mean the district was hungry, it meant the district was going to eat in the fourth month off its own parcels, and every household in the lower flats was going to be short in a week that it would not have been short in, and the reason was a gate linkage and a bank that a court had said the water office must do, and nobody in that district was going to be interested in it in the third month.

The provincial hold was still on. It was still sealed and not to be enforced and not assigned, and it was a piece of paper on a board in the capital that nobody in Reed Cut would remember existed by the fourth month.

And the thing nobody had put on the levy sheet, because a levy sheet is a levy sheet, was that a household which is fighting cannot work.

That was the sum she had got wrong twice. A household with a creditor in it and a date has no hours. A household writing letters to a factor in Greyfen is not in a field. Orra Dunn had gone to a court on the twenty-third and had come back with a date and had then spent nine days going to four doors, and the forty quarters of winter rye had gone into the upper barn on the sixth of the tenth instead of the fourth, and the sixth of the tenth is not a catastrophe, and the sixth of the fourth is the difference between seed in the ground and seed in a sack in a cold room with a woman looking at it every morning.

Her mother had been a ringer in a bleach works and had died of the damp in her lungs in the year after the fire and had never in her life had a date from anybody, and had once told Nell that a family with a date in it is a family that cannot plan a week, and Nell had been fifteen at the time and had thought it was about soup.

She wrote it on the vat flat under the third sum, in chalk, where it could be wiped off if the vats wanted the flat.

*Three households with a date in them and no hours. Dunn — four doors, and the rye two days late into the barn. Venn — a bar back at noon and a canal seat in the fourth month that nobody can tell her about. Sallow — a man who paid eleven pounds over the price for seed a clerk did not certify, and who has wanted to ask somebody something every day since.*

*You cannot litigate a winter. Somebody has to hold the other end of the paper so the family can be in a field.*

---

Marek Kest came to the dye-house on the twenty-third of the tenth month and stood in the workroom doorway for about four minutes.

He had done it three times in six weeks, and each time something in the workroom turned into an office, and on the third time Nell said it.

"You have been here four minutes and Rowen has not put her dye down once and I have asked her twice."

"I have not said anything."

"You have not said anything *yet*," said Nell, "and Rowen is twenty-three and she is in a vat and she is thinking about a thing you are going to ask her, and it is her afternoon. Take the stool at the end and copy."

He looked at the stool at the end of the vat flat, which was the stool for mending, and which had a broken leg and a cushion made of folded sacking.

"That is not a seat for a man who reads."

"It is a seat for a man who copies," said Nell. "If anybody comes in here in the next two hours with a paper about a duty, you are going to say that this room copies schedules and does not read papers, and you are going to say it before they ask, and you are going to say it the same way eleven times, and if you cannot say it eleven times without getting interested then you are going to sit in the yard instead and I will send you out a list."

He sat down on the stool at the end.

He copied for four hours. He copied the four houses' levy schedules into a fair book, and then a wage sheet for the Beckery that had come in wrong twice, and then four copies of the amended clause, because the clause had to go into each house's schedule in the assessors' own wording and the assessors had given out a form with the wording on it and the wording had to be copied by hand into each schedule, because a form is not an amendment until it is in the schedule.

It was the dullest thing he had done in eleven days, and it was the first thing he had done in eleven days that nobody had come to the lane about.

He copied well. He was the sort of man who could copy, and when he copied he did not talk, and at the fourth hour he asked for more ink and Nell brought him more ink and did not say anything else.

---

Tamsin Rook's letter came up the road on the twenty-fourth with the courier, and it was four lines long.

*It is yours now and I am not coming back for it, and do not let them put a clerk at it. A. R.*

Nell did not want it. She said so to the lane, at the table, that evening, to about fourteen people, and got laughed at, and said it again on the twenty-sixth to about nine.

"You read it out four times a day for six weeks," said Coll Fenner. "You will read it out four times a day now and it will be a different thing and you will not be able to say why."

"That is exactly what I am afraid of."

On the twenty-seventh she wrote four names on the back of a levy sheet and nailed the sheet to the leg of the table, and the four names were this.

**Ivo Harker.** Sixty-eight, keeps a tin under his bed, has not bought paper in four years, copies in a hand that is very slow and completely legible.

**The seed-barn copyist.** The woman in the hood who will not give her name and who will not need to, and who has been at this table every day for six weeks and made four of the twenty-six copies in a schoolroom hand that turns every descending stroke into a small loop. Entered on the sheet as *the seed-barn copyist* and left that way, on purpose, because a rota can hold a person who has no name in a book, and a name in a book is the thing she has spent four years not having.

**Coll Fenner.** Twenty-eight, off the Reed Cut bank, who made four of the twenty-six with another man on the back of a seed tally on the sixteenth of the ninth month, arguing about the fourth term the whole way through and getting it right because they argued, and who would not put his name to it then and has now, because a rota cannot run on a man who has not got a name.

**Wenna Sallow.** Nineteen, of the upper district. Kept the copy of the tally that her father Rennie bought out of Sarn's hand for a shilling and that Sarn initialled, and can write a fair hand, and has had a fortnight of a thing in her house she does not understand, and has wanted since the twenty-third to be the person who explains it to somebody else.

Nell hammered the sheet on twice because the nail went in crooked the first time, and then she wrote under the names, in chalk on the table itself where it could not be missed:

**Rota. If a paper comes to anybody in this lane with a duty on it, they may bring it here, and anybody on this list will read it out loud in the plain words, and they will not be told what to do about it, because that is not what this table is.**

That was the whole of it. It took one evening, it cost a levy sheet and a piece of chalk, it had no number, and no court had heard of it.

---

The child came on the twenty-eighth, in the middle of the afternoon, while the rota's first reader was still setting up.

She was seven. She came up the flat part of the lane from the seed barns on her own, in a coat that was not hers, and she stood in front of the trestle table and said, to nobody in particular, "Bettany Skail says to tell the people at the table that her sister's boy is not to sign anything on Friday, and if anybody brings him a paper he is to have it read out loud first, and she says you worked it out down here and she wants to know if that is the same thing."

Coll Fenner crouched down so that he was the same height as the child, which Nell had watched him do to a bollard an hour earlier and was quietly pleased about.

"Which people at the table."

"The ones who read," said the child. "She said the ones who read."

"What is your name."

"Nell. Like my aunt. My aunt's called Nell."

"Then we are two Nells, and you are the small one, and I am going to remember that." Nell knelt down on the other side. "Do you know what the fourth term says."

"It's the one where you sign," said the child. "Bettany's sister's boy heard the man at the gate say the holder pays, and Bettany said he was wrong, and then she asked whether there was a fourth term, and Ivo read it to her out of the book in her own kitchen, and she said: that is the one that means no."

The lane went quiet the way it does.

"That is exactly the fourth term," said Nell. "Say it back to me in your own words and I will write it down, and then I will give it to Wenna, and Wenna will put it in the book with my name on it as the person who asked for it."

"You are going to write down that I said it."

"I am going to write down that a person asked for the fourth term to be read at her house before a paper was signed," said Nell, "and I am going to leave out the seven-year-old, because in about four years you will be eleven and you will not be able to walk into a room and ask anybody for a thing, and if your name is on a list now then in four years you can point at the list."

The child thought about that with the enormous seriousness of a person who has been sent on an errand and intends to complete it.

"Will it be in a book."

"In four books. In this lane, in Greyfen, in a room in Auremar, and in a girl's own."

"All right," said the child. "And I am not to sign anything on Friday."

"You are not to sign anything on Friday, or ever, or until somebody has read it to you and you have understood it and somebody has written down that you understood it, and there is a girl called Nessa Dunn who is fifteen and knows exactly how that is done, because a man in a good coat put her name on a paper she never signed, and you go and find her when you need it."

The child said, "Is the fifteen-year-old the one with the slate?"

"That is the one with the slate," said Nell. "Her name is on the board at the court window and anybody can read it off, and you go up there and say it out loud, because that is what a slate is for, and it is the whole of what it is for."

And the child went back down the flat part of the lane in a coat that was not hers, and the table went on, and by the time the light went there were six people waiting.

---

Mara Venn brought the wax tablet out onto the step of her own house on the evening of the twenty-eighth, in the cold, and put it in Nell's hands, because Nell was the one who came up the road.

"You are going to write it down properly now, and put it in the record," Mara said, "and say that the household kept these figures, and that they are the household accounts, and that they are hers, and that they go down with the ruling and not with you. You will be gone in a year and the figures will be here in forty. I have watched four people in this lane be glad about something for a week and I would like one thing in it that is going to still be true when all four of us have moved."

Nell held it and did not open it, because it was not hers to open, and said that she would have it copied and read back to Mara before it went anywhere.

And on the twenty-ninth Nell went to the Caudle house at the end of the lane in Greyfen, after a shift, with the form in her coat and the number on the card, because the man on the rack would bring it on a Tuesday and it was better that it came from her with a person in the house.

The house was the last on the lane and it was the smallest, and the yard was clean, and the woman who came out was Nery Caudle, thirty-nine, with a bar of soap in her hand and four children behind her of whom the eldest was eleven and the youngest was four. Nell had seen her at the reading table four times in six weeks and had never spoken to her, and the woman had been at the table four times and had never once come to it with a paper, which Nell had noticed and had thought about more than she wanted to admit.

"Mrs Caudle. I'm Nell Kest, off the vats, and I've got a form the man on the rack is going to bring you, and I'd rather you got it from me with a person in the house than from him on his own."

"Will it cost us."

"Yes."

"How much."

"Two pounds and about five, across the quarter, off the four houses, worked out by the assessors and put on a sheet." Nell took the form out. "And I am not going to tell you it is nothing, because I have done the sum on the flat of a vat with a bit of chalk and I have it in front of me, and out of the four houses on this lane you are the one where it comes out of a single wage."

Nery Caudle did not move and did not put the soap down.

"Mike keeps the bench and I keep the house," she said, "and that is two wages, and I have a girl of eleven and a boy of eight and two that small, and Mike's wage goes on the bench and mine comes off it, and I have done this nine years."

"Then I am not going to pretend it is a small thing. What I am going to tell you is what it buys."

She read it out. Not the levy — the other half. Tool debts named to the departing worker, the sum set out, eight weeks, and the balance and the cost of recovery carried by the Greyfen Dyers' Tool Fund and by no household and no worker.

"And what happens," said Nery Caudle, "if I sign that, and I am the one who signs it, and I am standing in this yard."

"Then in two years' time a girl comes to your door and leaves, and she takes her own tools, and nobody in this house is answerable for what she owes, and it is not the next girl and it is not you." Nell turned the form round. "And if you do not sign it, then in two years' time a girl comes to your door and leaves and she takes her tools, and if she cannot pay in eight weeks then it goes to the next woman who takes the bench, and that woman has not signed anything and does not know about it and cannot refuse, and that is the thing I am not going to let happen in a house with a bench and a one-woman wage in it."

Nery Caudle looked at the form for a long time.

"Nineteen years I have had that bench," she said. "My mother had it. There are four of them on that bench now. And you are telling me there is a woman off this lane in Auremar who got a thing written that none of us could write for ourselves, and it has been three weeks."

"Three weeks," said Nell, "and it cost the man who read it to me his night's sleep, and it costs you two pounds and five a quarter, and I have put both of those on the same sheet as the form, so that whoever reads it in a year knows it was not free."

"Write this down," said Nery Caudle.

"Ma'am—"

"I am not signing something in my own yard that I cannot point at afterwards." She put the soap down on the step, which was the thing Nell had been waiting for without knowing she was waiting for it. "You write on the back of it: that I signed it in my own yard on the twenty-ninth of the tenth month, that I was told the levy, and I am not going to pretend I did not understand the levy, and that I signed it with two pounds and five a quarter of my own wage in it because the alternative is my next girl pays it and she will not know what she is paying."

Nell wrote it and read it back, and Nery Caudle made her read it twice, and then she signed it, and then she asked for a copy, and Nell had not brought one and lost an hour getting it from the house, and did not mind.

---

On the thirtieth of the tenth, at the fourth hour of the afternoon, Wenna Sallow came to the table in her turn, with the rota sheet in one hand and her father's copy of the tally in her coat.

Marek Kest was at the far end of the trestle with the rota's book, because the rota needed a book and the book needed a hand that could keep it, and he had been given the book by Nell on the understanding that if anybody came to him about a duty he would say the same eleven sentences, and he had said four of them that afternoon and had not been asked for any of them.

Wenna Sallow stood on the other side of the table with her arms folded, and she was nineteen and she had been standing at a table for six weeks with her arms folded.

"Marek Kest is in the yard," she said, to nobody in particular, and then, when nobody answered, again, louder, which was the trick she had learned in six weeks and which Nell was going to have to unlearn in her.

"I am at the end of this table," said Marek.

"I know you are at the end of this table, I have been looking at you since I got to the lane." She had the copy of the tally out. "My mother has been asking since the twenty-third. She wants to know if there is a duty standing against the Sallows. Not against the Dunns. Against us. And she will not ask at the window because she has been going to that window for forty years and she does not want a clerk to look at her, and she will not ask the vicar because the vicar will tell her to be patient, and she has asked me three times this week and I have told her three times that I do not know, and I have been standing here for an hour working out how to say it to a man who is copying a book."

The yard was quiet. The vat next door was still going and nobody turned round.

Marek put the reed down.

"Your mother can come to the window," he said, "and there is a slate on a board at that window, and anybody can read it, and her name is not on it and I would not put it on it. And I am going to tell you the true thing, which is that I do not know whether there is a duty standing against the Sallows, and I have not looked, and I am not going to tell your mother that there is not, because I have been in this province for two months and every single thing I have found has been a thing that somebody in a warm office was certain was not there."

Wenna Sallow stood on the flat ground in the middle of a lane and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"That is the first time anybody has said *I do not know* to anybody in this lane."

"It is the first time anybody has known it and said it," said Marek.

And the words came out of him without any difficulty at all.

He noticed it happening the way a man notices a floor he has just stepped onto in the dark. For two months there had been one sentence he could not get out of his mouth, whatever room he was standing in, and it had not been there that afternoon in front of a nineteen-year-old girl who had carried a question for a week, and the sentence that had replaced it was worse than the one that was gone, and it had come out easily.

Nell Kest was at the end of the table and she saw his face do it, and she said nothing, and she went on standing there until he had picked the reed up again and gone back to the book.

---

The confession happened in the workroom at the vats, at about the eighth hour of the same evening, with the dye gone cold in the vat and Vare gone home an hour before, and it happened badly.

Marek came in behind her and stood by the flat with his coat still on.

"About this afternoon," he said.

"Don't."

"I am going to have to."

"Then have it here, because I have got four names and a chalk and a rota and about an hour before it gets properly dark, and I am not doing that in front of them." She did not put the chalk down. "Go on."

"There is a thing I have not told you about our mother."

Nell put the chalk down on the flat.

"Her name is not on any record. Not one, in this empire. There is a Chancery roll from the year of the fire and there is a line in it that says a copyist of the third gallery was lost, and under the line where her name would be there is nothing at all, and the thing that is in the place of her name is a word that means she was struck. I have had a copy of that roll since I was seventeen. I did not take it out of the building. It came to Father in a hand, in an envelope with nothing on it, and he has it in a tin of his own, and I found out in a week in the spring that he had it and I did not tell you, and I have been carrying both halves of that for about two months."

"And the debt."

"There is a file in this city with a property and a debt in it, in our name, disputed, and neither part of it is enforceable and neither part of it is free, and it has been open and shut and reopened four times since the fire and every time it was shut somebody who was a child at the time had no say in it." He put his hand flat on the vat flat, which was not his flat. "And there is a part of it I have not been able to read, because to read it I would have to open something that is sealed, and I have told a constable in a room that I would not open it, and I am not going to change my mind about that in a workroom on a Thursday, and that is the honest reason and not a good one."

"She is not in the roll," said Nell. "I mean — you have said. I know. I have known since I was eight that the name was not on anything, and I have never once known whether that was because she was dead or because of something she did." She was not looking at him. "That is the thing. That is the whole of the thing I have wanted for twelve years. Not whether she was good. Whether the not-being-there is a thing that happened to her or a thing she did with her own hands."

Marek did not answer that, because it was the answer to the question his mother had actually left, and he was twenty-nine and it was the eighth hour of an evening in a dye-house, and he had brought her a Chancery roll and a tin.

---

The other half came out after, and it came out because Nell asked it, and the asking was not a question about records.

"Have you been able to say it yet," she said. "The thing you could not say. In the yard this afternoon you said it to a girl of nineteen in front of everybody and you said it like a man reading a schedule."

"No," Marek said. "I have not. It stopped this morning. It came out at the fourth hour to a girl I have never met and I have not been able to make it happen since, and I have been trying since about the third hour this afternoon, in my head, in the lane, on the road, and I cannot make it happen, and I would give a great deal to be able to make it happen."

"How long."

"Nine weeks. Since the twelfth of the ninth month, in a courtroom, with a reading in it."

"And the headache."

"Gone. Since about the twenty-sixth." He said it and heard how small it was. "I went to no one. I have not been back to Perrin Ashe and I am not going to, and the reason is not pride, and I want you to hear the real reason, which is that there is nothing to pay a penny for. A thing that has stopped is not a thing that has been treated. If I go up to a chandler's shop and put a penny on a table and he puts two fingers on my eyes and says the word *well* then I will have bought a cure for something I do not have, and I will be a man who bought a cure, and I will be able to tell people about it, and it will be the single most useful lie I have ever told in my life."

"And which of the two things do you mind more."

He was quiet for long enough that the vat ticked.

"The headache," he said. "I mind the headache not being there more, and I have been not believing that for four days."

"Go on."

"The headache was mine. I did that to myself, in a room, with a court in front of me, and I knew when I did it and I did it anyway, and for nine weeks there was a thing in my own head that I could point at and say: this is the cost of the thing I did, and it was a real cost, and it was on my side of the sheet." He put both hands flat on the flat. "And now there is no cost. Nothing in my body is holding any of it. And in about four days' time I am going to be back in a room in front of a man from Examinations, and I am going to be a man who can lie — not a liar, a man who can say a comfortable untrue thing to a frightened person and have it stand there — and nothing about me will say otherwise. That is what I mind. For nine weeks I could not be trusted with a sentence, and I had started to rely on it, and it has been taken away, and it was never mine. It was a symptom. And the man I am going to be for the rest of this is a man who has to decide every single time."

Nell Kest stood at the flat of the dye vat in a workroom in Greyfen with cold dye in the vat behind her and looked at her brother for about as long as it takes a vat to give up the last of its heat.

"Say it," she said.

"What."

"Say the thing you cannot say. To me. Now. About me."

Marek opened his mouth, and the sentence came out, and it was the one he had wanted to say to a woman holding a soup pot on a rail in the ninth month and to a clerk of a fund in a corridor and to his colleague in a copying alcove, and it came out in a workroom in Greyfen to a woman of twenty who had not asked for it and had earned it twice.

He said it.

"And there it is," said Nell, after a moment. "Well. That is the first one since the twelfth and I have got it, and I am not going to spend it, and I am not going to pretend I am going to forget I have got it either, because that is the sort of thing people say to make the person who gave it feel better, and I have been on the receiving end of that from three adults and a vicar."

"I know."

"I am not going to forgive you for the ninth month," she said. "I want to be exact about what I am saying, because you will spend a year doing the arithmetic on it and getting it wrong. I am not forgiving you for the month of the ninth. I went to the vicar on the fourth of the eleventh about the guild fund and I did not tell him about the levy until he asked me, and I lied to him about a sum, and I am twenty years old and I lied to a man in a house about money, and I did that because you taught me that a number can be a weapon and you did not mean to teach me that and it worked. So no. Not that."

"Then what is left."

"What is left is this." She picked the chalk up off the flat and put it in the loop of string round her wrist. "Someday you are going to be standing in a room and I am going to be behind you, and I am going to find out at the wrong moment that there is one more thing. Not about a duty. About us. About who decides. And I am going to need you to have said it to me first, in a room with nobody in it, while you still could. That is the sentence. I have known what it was since I was eight years old and sitting in a room while two grown people decided about me, and I have been waiting twelve years for somebody to hand it to me, and you have handed it to me twice now, once in the ninth month when it cost you and once tonight when it did not, and both times you did not ask whether I wanted it."

"Would you have wanted me to ask?"

"I would have wanted you to ask twice," said Nell Kest, "and asked me on the first one, and I would have said yes, and then it would have been mine to refuse, and it is not mine to refuse now, because you did not ask. So that is the price, and it is not a large one, and I am not going to let it be a large one, and I am still going to be here in this workroom on Saturday. Go home. You are not any use in a lane and you are less use in a workroom, and I have to be at that table at the seventh hour tomorrow."

He went out into the yard and stood in it for a while, in the cold, in a building that had a bench in it with a broken leg, and the headache was not there, and neither was the other thing, and both of those had been his to keep.

---

The two letters came up the lane on the thirtieth, at the fourth hour, with the evening post, and a boy of about fourteen brought them to the table because Nell had told him to bring anything to the table.

One was addressed to *the reading table, Venn lane, and anybody there who keeps copies*.

The other was addressed to *whoever is doing this*.

And the second one was not signed.
