# Chapter 30: Nobody Was in the Room

Perry Ostrow would not come, and Tamsin Rook had asked her twice before she understood that it was not reluctance.

"I am not going," said Ostrow, in the copying alcove, on the second of the second month, with her sleeves pushed up and her husband's stick in the crook of her elbow because she always had it now, wherever she was. "It is not that I do not want to see it. It is that I read to him. He has been blind since the spring and he reads everything in this house aloud to me at the table and I am the reader. If I am gone for a fortnight then he is a man in a house with nobody and that is the whole of what I am and I am not spending it on a journey."

"You could write to me."

"I could write to you," said Perry Ostrow, "and I would be a woman writing to a copyist about a place four hundred miles from where he lives, and in about four years somebody will find out that a woman in Glasswater put a file into a lane and came up the river and slept on a floor, and they will find my letter first, because a letter is a thing that can be read out, and I am not doing that to him."

Tamsin took the book off the frame and said, "Then tell me one thing and I will do it."

"Tell you what?"

"You said in Mosswake at the end of the ninth month that you could not say the thing about your eyes, and I did not push you, and I have thought about it for four months and it is the only thing anybody has ever refused me that I have believed." Tamsin set the book down. "I am going to walk into a house in a district where nobody has ever heard of the fourth month, and there is no document, and there is no court, and the person I am asking will ask me what I am. What do I say."

Perry Ostrow thought about it for a moment, and then she said the thing that made the whole fortnight possible.

"You say you are nobody," she said. "You say it four times, out loud, in the room, before you ask anything, and you say it the fourth time after they have said yes. And do not hand anybody a paper they did not ask for. That is the whole problem. A paper in a district like that means a thing has already been decided, because papers come after decisions, and a woman who has never seen the district and walks in and puts a paper on a bench is a person from a decision. Take the book. Leave the paper in the bag until somebody asks to see it, and write it out in the book at the table, and when you have written it out read it back and ask them whether that is what they said, and if there is a girl in the room ask the girl."

"That is a great deal of instructions for two days' notice."

"It is four months of watching a man read a form that told him he was not obliged to look," said Perry Ostrow. "Do the four things. The rest of it you will get wrong in front of people and it will not matter, and the four will not."

---

She went down the water on the third of the second month in a packet boat with a copyist's book, a bundle of three sheets, and a sheet of the Mosswake court's own paper with Sarn's stamp in one corner and nine words on it saying that the sender was not a court and had no authority, which she had written herself, because Sarn, after five weeks of arguing about the cost of a window, had begun stamping things and had stopped asking what for.

Millbrook Reach is a landing, a row of counting rooms, a coal stair, a wheelwright's yard and about four hundred people, three hundred miles from Auremar and a day and a half by water in the second month.

The landing office is a room with a bench, a stove, a rack of standing instructions, and a book. The clerk is called Sabra Ollett and she is forty-one and she has been doing it for nine years, and she came out to the boat herself, which is not a thing clerks do.

"You will be the woman from up the water," she said.

"I am nobody," said Tamsin Rook, on the step of the landing, with her hand out. "I have no seal, no office, and no authority, I am not a court, and I am not a magistrate's clerk, and there is a letter in my bag that says so and if you would rather read it before you let me in I would entirely understand and I would go and stand at the inn until you had."

Sabra Ollett read the letter standing on the step, in the wind, and gave it back, and said, "Come in, and shut that door, and mind the step, and I am going to say something to you in the first two minutes and then we are not going to talk about it again, because I have been carrying it since the ninth of the first month and I have not told my own clerk."

---

What she had to say took about ninety seconds.

"Nine years," said Sabra Ollett. "Nine years I have kept this book. Every filing accepted in this district under flood authority goes in the routine column, in order, with the date and the district office stamp and the instrument and the ground, and it is not itemised, and it is not heard on its own, and that is the form and I have never once wanted to do it otherwise and I have never once been asked to explain it to anybody.

"And in the tenth month I stamped one. It came in on a Thursday, filled in on a printed sheet, and I stamped it and entered it in the routine column with a date, and I did not itemise it, because itemising it is not what the form is for. And in the first week of the twelfth month a man came from a factor's office at the far end of the row and took a cooper's gauge off a shop bench as security under it, and he was very polite about it, and I did not know anything about it until the woman came down on the ninth of the first month to ask me why, and then again on the nineteenth, and then once in the second month.

"And I could not show her anything, and it is not because I hid it. It is because there is nothing to show. It is in the book. It is on the ninth line of the routine column for the tenth month and it says: guarantee, shop goods, bond, and the name of a district office, and that is all it says, and there is no number against it, because the form says the filing is not to be itemised, and if it is not itemised it does not get a number, and if it does not get a number then it cannot be found by anybody who has not got the book in front of them, and I have got the book, and I found it, and I read it out to her in about a minute, and there is not one word on that line about a woman or a girl or a shop."

"And the sheet it was entered on," said Tamsin Rook.

"The man brought it in himself," said Sabra Ollett. "It was not sent to me by a court. It came in on a Thursday in a hand that was not a clerk's, filled into a printed form, and I stamped it because a district landing office stamps what is put in front of it, and I have stamped four hundred of those in nine years, and I stamped this one in about four seconds, and I have thought about those four seconds every day since the ninth of the first month."

Tamsin Rook sat down on the bench in the landing office at Millbrook Reach and put her bag on the floor with the three sheets in it, and did not open it.

"Who is the form from," she said.

"There is a drawer."

"There is a drawer," said Sabra Ollett. "Second bay. It is the district's standing instructions and it is where the form is kept, because the form says to be filed with the standing instructions and not with the papers, and I have never in nine years taken it out and looked at it, and I took it out on the nineteenth of the first month and I have looked at it every day since."

---

The Oster shop was four doors up from the landing and it is a cooper's, and it is a bench and a stove and a smell of oak and a smell of tar, and the shop is called **Oster and Son** on a board over the door with the *and Son* painted over at some point and not painted out, and the son was twenty-two and had been out of the trade for a year because the trade had gone out of the house.

Iselle Oster was forty-one and she did the trade herself, and she had done it for seven years, and she had been at the window at the landing office twice in the first month and once in the second, and Tamsin Rook had known about her from a letter on a stone on a dye vat for seven weeks.

"I am nobody," said Tamsin Rook, in the doorway, before she was inside. "I have no seal, no office, and no authority, I am not a court, and I am not a clerk of a court, and I came up the water on a packet boat on my own money because a woman wrote to a table in a lane and asked, and I am asking."

That is the first time.

The shop had a boy of sixteen sitting on the bench and a woman at the far bench with her back to the door. Iselle Oster did not ask the boy to leave and did not tell him to come in. She stood in the middle of the floor and said, "My name is Iselle Oster. I have sent a letter and I have got no answer that helps. What is it you want."

"I want to ask you four questions about a paper, and I want to write down what you say, in a book, and read it back to you, and give you the page. And I have got a sheet in my bag with a copy on it, and I am not going to put it on your bench until somebody in this room asks to see it."

"Who is asking?"

"Nobody," said Tamsin Rook. "I have come on my own money and I have a week's wages off and I am asking."

And that is the second time, and Iselle Oster looked at her for a while and then said, "Then ask your four questions, and if you ask me a fifth I shall tell you to go to the landing."

---

She asked them in order.

*Is there work in it?*

"There is work in it," said Iselle Oster. "There is a house's work in it. My husband's bench and my husband's tools and the trade, and there is a man in it who is not my husband and has not been dead in a year, and there is me."

*Whose name is on it?*

"There are three names on it and I will tell you all three because I have been telling people since the fourth of the twelfth and I have got tired of the part where I say one. On the paper there is mine, in the box where the person goes, because he filled it in himself and he put me in it and he did not ask me. And on the surety line at the side there is a second one, and the second one is Tace, and Tace is sixteen and she is in this room and she is not deaf, and she has not said anything since you came in.

"And the third name," said Tamsin Rook, "is where the gauge is."

"The third is the shop," said Iselle Oster. "The gauge is the shop's. That is what a gauge is. The trade stamps it and the master holds it and the master is me, and when I had a husband he was the master and I was the master's widow, and in the seventh year a man at the district guild office stamped it with the shop's name on it because that is what you do to a gauge. And on the fourth of the twelfth month it came off the bench, and the boy took it in a cart, and he was very polite about it."

*If you stop, whose name answers for it?*

"If I stop, the name that answers is mine, and I did not sign, and I have never signed anything in twenty-two years except a bond at a mill, when the boy was born." She said it flatly. "That is the whole of the answer to your third question. It is mine. It has been mine since the fourth of the twelfth month and it will be mine in the fourth month and it will be mine when there is no gauge in this town at all, and there is no word on the paper for me to point at and say: that is the clause. There is a box. He wrote *the present holder* and he wrote me under it, in a different hand, on a form."

*Who read this to you, and do they get paid if you sign?*

That one she did not answer straight away, and Tamsin Rook waited, and the boy on the bench — Tace Oster, sixteen, four days out of a school term, a girl with a good head and a bad chest for cold rooms — said:

"Two men. A factor's man came for the gauge and he read it out of the paper, all of it, twice, and the second time slower, and he was paid by the factor whatever I do. And before him a man from the office came and read a part of it and would not read the part with the word *holder* in it and he put his thumb over it, and he was paid by nobody that I know of and I have thought about that since the fourth of the twelfth and it is the part I cannot get past."

Tamsin Rook wrote it down. Then she said, for the third time, "I am not a court," and then she wrote it out on the page in front of them as well, at the top, in the same ink, and asked Iselle Oster to look at the four questions and tell her whether they were what had actually been asked.

"They are not," said Iselle Oster. "Yours is better. Yours asks me four things and his asked me one thing, which was to put my thumb on a line."

Then Tamsin Rook read back everything she had written, and corrected four words at the request of a girl of sixteen, and asked the fourth question a second time because the first answer had been what her mother thought she had said, and got a different answer the second time, which was: *He was paid by the man who wanted the gauge and I do not think he read it twice because I could not read and he could tell.*

---

She wrote the second document in the book at the table in the shop, over three pages, and then she tore the three pages out and put them in front of Iselle Oster and asked her to write her own name at the foot of them, and the woman's hand is not a good hand and it is legible, and it says *Iselle Oster, cooper, of Millbrook Reach, and I have written the four answers as they were given to me by a woman who said four times that she was nobody.*

And then Tamsin Rook did the thing she had come three hundred miles to do, and it took eleven minutes, and it is the only page in the whole of this case that has no office behind it.

She opened a fresh page in the copyist's book and wrote at the head of it, in a fair hand, in a cooper's shop, in ink out of her own bag:

> *This is a copyist's book. It has no seal, no office, and no authority. Nothing written in it is evidence of anything. Any person may read it. Any person may write in it. Whoever has written here has written because they were asked to by somebody they did not have to answer to, and they have been told four times that they are nobody.*

And then under that, before anything else, she wrote the date, and the place, and the name of the person who had said the four answers, and the name of the person who had answered the fourth question twice, and the fact that a district office had entered a filing in its routine column in the tenth month with no number against it, and that a gauge had been taken from a shop bench on the fourth of the twelfth month under it.

Which means there are now two documents in the world about a cooper's gauge, and neither of them is in a court, and one of them says on its face that it is worth nothing.

---

The thing that showed her the outside of her own work happened on the thirteenth, and it happened in about four minutes, and it was not the part of the fortnight she had come for.

The man who had come for the gauge came back to the landing office at about the ninth hour to ask, in the ordinary way, whether there was a woman from up the water in the district asking questions, and Sabra Ollett told him the truth, because she was not going to lie to a factor about her own door, and he went away satisfied.

And on the same morning, in a yard at the far end of the row, a wheelwright had copied the four questions out onto the back of a bond receipt in his own hand, because a woman had read them to him off a board in a lane about a month before and he had asked her whether he might write them out, and she had said yes, and he had written them out badly, and he was using them that morning on a creditor who had come at him about a debt of his own.

And Tamsin Rook, three hundred miles away, would never have known, and could not have stopped it, and would not have wanted to.

"It is a good thing," said Sabra Ollett, coming in from the yard at the eleventh hour with her coat off. "That is what I am telling you. I have had nine years of being a landing office that does what the form says, and this week there is a man in this district using four questions that a woman wrote on a board in a lane, and he is using them on a creditor, and the creditor came to me about it and I could not help him, and I want you to understand that I have thought about nothing else since the nineteenth of the first month, and this is the first thing since the nineteenth that has made me feel as though the form is not the worst thing in this building."

Tamsin Rook sat on the bench in a landing office three hundred miles from anywhere with a copyist's book shut on her knees and found out what she had been building.

A form with a run on it had reached this district in the tenth month and had put a woman's name in a box and taken a gauge off a bench. And four questions written in chalk on a board in a river lane had reached the same town two months later, without a run, without an office, without a seal, and without anybody's permission, and the difference between the two documents was that one of them could not be asked about by anybody at all, ever, and the other one could be asked about by anybody in this room.

That was the whole of the difference. It was the whole of it, and she had known it since the thirtieth of the tenth month in a workroom in Greyfen with a cold dye vat behind her, and it had taken her sixteen weeks and a three-hundred-mile journey to be able to say it in a sentence without wanting to be more certain about it than she has any right to be.

She wrote it at the foot of a page in the book, and then she crossed it out, because it was the sort of thing a man writes in a book about himself, and then she wrote it out again underneath in a fairer hand with the date on it, because it was not a thing about herself, it was a finding, and a finding has to be written twice, once for the person finding it and once for whoever reads the book.

She came back up the water on the fourteenth of the second month in the same boat she had come down in, and the good stock was still in her rooms in Auremar and two shillings and ninepence of it was gone, and the list of forty-one names was in a chest under a winter coat, and thirty-one of the forty-one had answered.

And on the twenty-second of the second month, in a book in a room in Auremar with a stove in it, on a page dated the twelfth of the second month, and carried up the water in a cooper's hand and a girl's, there were four lines in the mouth of a girl of sixteen who had been answered for by her mother and had answered for herself, and the district that lost it had obeyed a printed form perfectly, and the whole of the case since the seventh of the ninth month came down to a question nobody in this case has yet been able to put in a way that can be answered: who in the world is helped by quiet.
