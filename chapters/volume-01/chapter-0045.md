# Chapter 45: Sixty Yeses and Four That Are Worth a District

The first thing on the board on the second of the sixth month, in a hand nobody recognised, was *C. Fenn — reed cut bank — took these off the board on the second and is taking them down the water to a house with a paper in it*, and underneath it in the same chalk, *and I am not taking anybody's name off*.

The second thing on the board was in the hand of the woman from the seed barns and had been there four months, and it said: *I read one out in the second week of the second month and I did not tell anybody what to do about it, like the rule says. — the woman from the seed barn.*

Nell Kest looked at that on the second of the sixth month and then went and stood at the trestle with her hands behind her back for about a minute, because a woman of about seventy had written the month and the week on a board in a lane to say that she had not broken a rule, and nobody had ever asked her to be that careful, and it had been her own idea, and it had survived four months of weather.

---

They came down the lane on the ninth of the sixth month because Marl Crenn had said they would, and they came because a decision had come out of the guild in the fourth month with four houses' names in it and three of those four houses had not known until the ninth of the sixth that the levy had ever been argued about.

The four had been named in a finding, in writing, with reasons, on a sheet the guild's own examiner had signed, and the finding was against them, and the reason it was against them was that they had signed.

> *Upon the assessment of the quarter, and upon a challenge to the recalculation of the levy of nine pounds a quarter made on behalf of the four houses that entered a second instrument, the court finds: that the recalculation was made after the quarter was struck and not before it; that a house that has entered a second instrument since the sixth year holds its exemption upon the first instrument read with the second; that a levy raised upon four houses for the cost of a form entered upon those four houses is not apportionable among the other houses in the trade; and that the four houses are answerable for the whole of it.*

Three and a quarter pages. The Caudles at the top of it, because they had signed first, on the twenty-ninth of the tenth month, and Nery Caudle had put her share in a cloth off her own wage and had asked for it to be taken in the same coin, and there was a farthing over, and the farthing was hers.

Nell Kest read all three and a quarter pages out loud at the trestle on the ninth of the sixth month, with about ninety people in the lane, and she read the names of the four houses, and she read the Caudle's two pounds five and a quarter, and she read the farthing.

The Caudle was at the back of the lane and did not come. Roke was there and stood with her arms folded the way everybody on that lane does, and said nothing at all while the whole of it was read, and then said one sentence, at the end, in a voice about four feet high.

"So the farthing's a cost as well."

"It is not a cost, Roke. It is a thing a house says about itself and it is in a box somewhere and it will mean you were first."

"I know what it means," said Roke. "I told you what it means in the third month and you wrote it down and it is on a wall at an assessor's now and I have not once had a single thing come of it. Nine pounds a quarter off four houses, four times a year, for a year, is thirty-six pounds out of a lane, and every one of the four of us can do that, and the other four in this trade will find out about it in about eleven months, and then the next four houses are going to be the ones who are out, and it is never going to be the four of us again." She unfolded her arms. "I am not saying it to be clever. I am saying it because you have just read out three and a quarter pages that say the four of us are answerable for the whole of it, and I would like the lane to have heard the other half of that sentence out loud before it goes home."

And then she stopped, because the woman at the front of the lane had said, out loud, in a fair hand's worth of a voice, "Is there work in it," and there was about a second of quiet, and then there was not.

And then she stopped, and said, "Before the next thing. The next thing is a letter from a house in another province and I am going to read it out and I am not going to say whether it is the right answer, and I am going to say that out loud now so that if anybody in this lane thinks I am going to say, they can stop me."

The lane stopped her for about a second and then let her go on.

The assessor of the Weavers' and Small Dyemasters' Association with eleven houses had come sixty miles in a cart and was standing four feet from the trestle with her leather case under her arm, and eleven of the eleven had not come, and one had, and it was the house whose letter it was.

The fourth answer. The fourth question, asked of a house in another province, and the answer was three words long.

> *Nobody read it to me.*

"I want to say three things about that and then I am going to move on," said Nell Kest. "One: the woman who wrote that is here, and her name is on the paper, and she is standing four feet from me, and she has told me she does not want to be named twice in a day and so I am not going to name her again. Two: she signed a second instrument in the sixth year and nobody read it to her, and the box in it had her house's name in it and not hers, and the box was a box a guild had told an assessor to fill in four years earlier in an instruction that was not about her and was about tool debts. Three: and this is the one that is not about her."

She put her hand flat on the flat of the trestle.

"Three: she is the only one of the eleven who has said that, and there are eleven, and I have had the answer from the other ten in one form or another for two months, and nine of them is *I asked and I was told* and one of them is *I do not know* and this one is *nobody asked me at all*. And that is a different answer and it is the only one of the three that nobody can do anything about, and I am not going to pretend to you in a lane that it is the same as the other two, because it is not, and because if somebody writes this down in a year they will write down that eleven women were asked a question and eleven women answered it, and the truth is that one of them was not asked."

---

Then she asked it, which was the thing she had actually come down the lane for, and the fourth question took two hours and a half and about two hundred people took part in it in some form, and Nell Kest did it in a way that no court has ever done a thing and no guild has ever permitted, which is that she asked it badly, out loud, in the open, at the end of a trestle table, in a lane, to anybody who wanted to answer it.

It is a different instrument in a room of two hundred than it is in a kitchen with one person in it, and the difference is not the words. In a kitchen a person can say *I do not know* and it costs nothing and it is private and the person goes home and nobody is a fact to anybody. At a table in a lane in front of ninety people, a person who has come down to be helped cannot say *I do not know*, because saying it is now a public act, and a public act has a cost, and the cost is that every person in the lane now knows the face of the person who could not answer, and will know it next season, and in a trade of two thousand people in nine provinces a face is a thing you can be asked about by a man in a good coat.

She read all four questions out at the fourth hour and then said, out loud, in her own words, that she knew that, and that she was going to do it anyway, and that anybody who wanted to be asked could come and stand at the trestle, and that she was not going to call on anybody, and that she was not going to look at anybody who did not come, and that she would read out the answers afterwards and would not say which of them were right.

A hundred and four people were in that lane on the ninth of the sixth month between the fourth hour and the seventh. Seventy-four of them were asked, because the asking went down one side of the lane and came back up the other, and thirty were in the back half behind a wall and a cart and the door of a house that was being emptied, and nobody went and got them, and she said so out loud before the numbers, which is the part that goes in the record.

> *Asked of seventy-four people in the open, at the trestle, on the ninth of the sixth month: is there work in it; whose name is on it — yours, or a word that walks about, like the holder; if you stop, whose name answers for it; and who read this to you, and do they get paid if you sign.*
>
> *Sixty said: yes, I am paid to read them, and no, I am not paid for the answer.*
>
> *Ten said: I do not know.*
>
> *Four said: nobody read it to me.*
>
> *Thirty people in the back half of this lane were not asked, because the asking did not go round, and that is a fact about the method and not about the people in the back half.*

---

Then she read out the four, because the four are the ones.

"You four. I am going to read out your answers and I am going to read your names, and the rule on this board is that anybody may add their own name and nobody may take another person's off, and I am going to ask you to add your own name under this line, and I am not going to add one for you and I am not going to ask you for one twice."

Three of the four were women she knew and one was a man of sixty-one who had come out of a house and had not expected to be in a lane at all. Two of them said they would not give a name to be read out. One said she would. The man said he would and then said, after a moment, that he would rather not, and that he wanted it written down that he had been asked twice and had said no twice, and that a person being asked twice is the whole of what he had heard about and did not want to be it.

Three names went on the board. And under them, in Nell Kest's own hand, in chalk, and it stayed there under four winters of weather:

*Three, and one who said no twice, which is also an answer and is on this board. Asked of seventy-four. Thirty in the back half of this lane were not asked and that is on this board too. — N.K.*

The woman from the seed barns read that from four feet away, at about the seventh hour, and said, out loud, "That is the first time anybody has put the number of the people who were not asked on the same board as the answers."

"It is a lane," said Nell Kest. "It is not a court and nobody has to do anything with it."

"It is more than a court does," said the woman from the seed barns, "and I am seventy-one and I have been in this lane since Michaelmas and I would like that written down somewhere, and it will not be, and I do not mind."

---

And then, because the Mosswake Roll Court is forty minutes away and the door of that room opens onto the embankment and there is a board on the outside of it with two registers on it and a shilling a copy, Nell Kest went down to the court window at about the seventh hour on the ninth of the sixth month with nine people, and paid a shilling for a copy of a line, and asked Ivet Sarn for the decision of the twenty-first of the fifth month, which was on the board between the register of pending duties and the hearing slate in a clerk's small hand with a line number against it and a shilling a copy.

Sarn read it out. All of it, in her own hand, standing at the window, with a queue behind her and nine people from a lane in Greyfen standing in it, and she read the third of the five twice, which was the one about the sixty households, and nobody in the queue said anything about it.

Then she said, "There is a second thing on this board and it is not mine and it is not the court's, and you asked for the paper and not the news, so I am going to tell you it is here and then I am going to stop."

The second thing was a sheet of a guild's paper, dated the second of the sixth month, with a seal on the bottom corner and a line number, and it said that Tavi Venn of the Venn lane, Mosswake, had been examined on the twenty-sixth of the fifth month, by Odile Fenn, the Mosswake guild witness, for the work of cutting and maintaining a channel, in reading, writing, arithmetic, ditching on the level, and the setting of a gate linkage to a maker's gauge, and had passed, and had been entered in the guild's book as a canal worker, and might now be taken on by a master on a written engagement, and might not be taken on on a verbal one.

Wendt Sayer had paid two shillings and sixpence for it out of the district works fund under the head *labour, examinations, engaged*, on the sixth of the fourth month — seven weeks before the woman sat down with the girl — as a recruitment cost and not a kindness, and a man who was not him would read that head in a year and ask why, and there is no answer in the head that is better than the one he wrote.

Nell Kest read the sheet twice and gave it back and did not say anything about it, because Tavi Venn had a step and a gate and a mother and had been asked once already in her life to fetch a thing for a room and had refused and been right, and there was nothing Nell Kest could do for a girl with a certificate.

---

At the table, on the way back up the lane, she said the thing the board had never said.

"Two things and then I am going to the vat," she said. "One. The four questions are mine only in the sense that I am the person who gives them away. They are on that board because I put them there and I will take them down when I want to, and any one of you may take them off that board and use them, in any order, in any house, in any trade, in any district, and there are two people who have already done it and one of them is a man off the Reed Cut bank who is standing four feet from me and is not going to say anything about it, and the other one is at the top of this lane and is seventy-one."

"And the second thing."

"The second thing is that a question asked of a person in a kitchen is a different instrument from the same question asked at a table in a lane, and I have spent five months learning that and I have not written it down anywhere and I am writing it down here in front of you. In a kitchen a person can say *I do not know* and it costs nothing. Here it costs a face. And I put thirty people's not being asked on this board in chalk, in my own hand, with my own name under it, and if anybody in this lane ever says that the four questions get anybody anywhere then I want them to read the line about the four."

Nobody wrote it on the board. Nell Kest wrote it in her own book that night, in nine lines, and dated it the ninth of the sixth month, and read it back twice, and did not send it to anybody, and did not think that was a failure.

---

The boy from the lane brought a letter up to the trestle at about the eighth hour on the same day and put it on the flat under a stone and went away without waiting, which he had been doing since the twelfth month of last year and had never once been thanked for, because he was fourteen and there are four hundred and forty of him in nine provinces and nobody has the name of any.

It came from four hundred and forty miles away. It was in a schoolroom hand. And it was not a question.

Four signatures, and four women, and the first one was a name Nell Kest had read in a letter on the sixteenth of the fourth month and the second and third and fourth were names she had never seen in her life and had no idea existed.

Under the four signatures, four lines, one under each name, and each line was the same sentence with the answer in it, and the last of the four had the man's own handwriting gone slightly wrong at the end of it, the way a hand does at the end of a sentence a person has read out loud before.

> *I asked the fourth question of a paper in this house and the answer was that the man who read it is paid by the combine, and there is one of him in four hundred and forty miles.*
>
> *I asked the fourth question of a paper in this house and the answer was that the man who read it is paid by the combine, and there is one of him in four hundred and forty miles.*
>
> *I asked the fourth question of a paper in this house and the answer was that the man who read it is paid by the combine, and there is one of him in four hundred and forty miles.*
>
> *Same as the three above. I wrote it out twice and checked it, because I am the one who reads for the others when they are not sure, and I would like it noticed that I have not added anything to it.*

Four lines, and the same answer four times and not one word of it different except the hand, and no question anywhere in it, and no request, and not one word about a document anybody had sent them.

A person in a furnace town had taken four things off a board in a lane four hundred and forty miles away, or been told them, and had put them down in a kitchen, and had used them, and had written down what happened, and had sent the answer to a table where nobody had asked for an answer.
