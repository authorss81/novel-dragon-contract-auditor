# Chapter 28: Early

The hearing slate had been on the wall of the Mosswake court window for five weeks and the register of pending duties had been on the wall of the Mosswake court window for three months, and they were hanging one above the other in Sarn's two handwritings, and the thing Orra Dunn had learned from looking at both of them every time she came down to the flats was that nobody in this province could read a name off a board and get anything out of it.

An entry is a fact. A fact is not a bar.

She knew what Grice was, because Grice had told her what Grice was in the eighth month of the ninth year in a room with a stove in it and a boy who wrote down everybody's name. *I have got a paper on you and I have told the man in Greyfen that I have got a paper on you.* Four hundred and eleven against a floor worth four hundred and eighty clean and two hundred and ten with a duty on it, and a man in Greyfen who would take a floor in the second month for less than it was worth now.

What she wanted, and had wanted since the twenty-third of the ninth month, was not to be cleared. She had stopped wanting to be cleared somewhere around the fourth day of the tenth month, when a neighbour of forty-one years' standing walked half a mile in the rain to hand her four pounds of tilling seed she did not need.

She wanted a day. One day, on a board, that she could carry up the lane and put in front of four people who would otherwise have to be given a *pending* and nothing else.

---

She went down on the second day of the first month, at the eighth hour, with a slate of her own in her coat, and she was in the queue for eleven minutes behind a man with a gate bill and behind two women who had come about a seed-share, and when she got to the sill she said, "Clerk. I want to be in the other one."

Sarn's pen stopped.

"The hearings."

"Yes."

"That is not a thing anybody goes in the hearings for," said Sarn. "That is a list of the people who came. There is no form for it. You do not get entered in a list of things you did, it is not a court, it is a wall with two headings on it."

"Then put me on the wall."

Sarn put her pen down, which is what she does.

"Orra Dunn. I am going to say this once and I am going to say it properly, because you have come four times in four months and I have watched you get further each time, and the fourth time you asked for a thing that nobody had thought of, and I would like you to hear me say a no the way I say the yeses. Your file is a barn term of the third month. The fifth year, and the third month, and there is no flood season in it. The process the roll-keeper has been building since the twenty-ninth of the eleventh is for flood-season continuity filings, and you are not in one, and if I write your name on that slate under the heading of hearings then every person who reads it will understand that you have asked to be heard under a process that has not heard you and cannot hear you, and in a fortnight it will be in a book that somebody in Greyfen is buying copies of, and it will be true, and it will be useless."

"I know it is not a flood-season filing."

"You have been in here three times since the twenty-third of the ninth month waiting for a thing you have been told twice is not going to happen as a matter of form," said Sarn, "and I would like you to hear the reason out loud from a person who is not being kind to you, which is that I cannot put you in a register of hearings on the strength of a request to be heard. A register of hearings is a record that you were heard. You have not been heard since the twenty-third and you cannot be entered as though you had."

And Orra Dunn stood at a clerk's window in a wet district and found that the thing she had walked forty minutes in the cold for was refused in about eleven seconds, by a woman she had never once been able to dislike, on a ground she had not thought of.

---

So she did the thing nobody expected, and she did it in about a minute, standing at the sill with her own slate in her hand and the rain coming down the steps behind her.

"Then I want to ask for a different day," she said.

"Different from which."

"Earlier. I want the fourth month's review called in the second month." Orra put her hands flat on the sill, in the way she had put them flat on it on the third day of the ninth month, when she had asked for a register and had been told there was no such thing. "Not instead of it. Earlier. In the second month, before the second month is half gone."

Sarn did not write anything.

"I am going to tell you why," said Orra Dunn, "and I am going to say it in the window and not in a letter, because a letter can be put down anywhere and this is a thing that has to be in a room. Anselm Grice bought a paper off Emrys Tale on the ninth month for a hundred and six, and he will not serve it on me and he will not serve it on Nessa, and he will serve it on the seed-share, and the seed-share is forty quarters of winter rye in the upper barn and it is the girl's, and he has been to two lawyers about it, and one of them says it cannot be taken and one of them says it can be taken if it is under the ground. He has told me he will do it in the second month. He said it in his own door in the ninth month and he has not changed his mind and he is not a man who changes his mind.

"So on some day in the second month a paper arrives about forty quarters of rye that belong to a girl of fifteen who signed nothing, and on that day the pending duty stops being a question and becomes a fact, and after that day there is nothing in this world anybody can do about the rye, because by then it will have been sold and the sale will be in a book and the book will be right, and Grice will be able to stand in a room and say the thing happened before anybody heard her, which will be *true*, and he will have paid a hundred and six pounds for it and four pounds to a lawyer to write the paper.

"And he does not care that you cannot hear me today. He cares that there is no day in this case until the fourth month. The fourth month is the day you have given me and it is a good day and I am going to hold to it with both hands. But a day in the fourth month is a day in the fourth month, and the paper comes in the second."

Sarn had picked up the pen and then put it down again, which Orra Dunn noticed and would think about for a week.

"Grice has told you a date for a paper he says he will not serve on you."

"He has told *me* a date. He has not told the court a date. There is no paper in this building with a date on it. If there were, I would be told, and you would be told, and it would be entered, and that is what I have been trying to get at for three months and I have not got it and I am not going to get it because I have not paid anybody to serve it and I am not going to."

"Then I cannot enter a date you have been told about in a private door."

"I know you cannot," said Orra. "So write the request. Write that the household of the upper seed barn asks that the review entered for the fourth month be called in the second month, and write that the reason given is that a creditor holds a paper and has said he will serve it on the seed-share in the second month, and write that the household has been told of that by a creditor and not by this court, and that if the court does not know of it the court does not know of it. And put it on the slate with today on it. And write on it that it has not been granted."

Sarn looked at her for a while.

"That is four things," she said, "and three of them are against me, and one of them is the only reason I would write it."

"Which one."

"That you are telling me in the window," said Ivet Sarn. "If you had written me a letter I would have had to write to the magistrate about whether a letter from a household is a thing that can be answered, and I should have had to say no, and the answer would have taken three weeks and would not have had a name at the top of it."

She wrote it in her own small hand, on the slate, and dated it the second of the first month, and gave it a line number because everything on that board had a line number even though nothing on that board had a number, and then she wrote at the foot of the line, in the same hand, in the same ink, *Asked. Not granted. Entered at the household's request and not at the court's.*

And she gave Orra Dunn a copy, because it was a shilling, and Orra paid the shilling.

---

It worked the way a shilling does, and not the way a hearing does.

Grice's boy came on the fifth day and bought two copies at a shilling each, which is fourpence more than he needed to, and Orra saw him do it and understood at once that a man does not buy the second copy because he wants to read it twice.

He wanted it in the house.

On the eighth day a letter came to the court window from the seed office in Mosswake, in a good hand, with the word *Enquiry* at the top of it, and it asked whether the review entered for the fourth month still stood, and it asked whether the court would say in writing that nothing would be done about the pending duty before that date, and it asked — and this was the part that Orra got a copy of afterwards, because Sarn copied it to her as a person entered on the slate, which is the whole of what being entered on a slate is for — whether the hearing slate could be said to mean that a hearing would take place on the day asked for or on the day entered.

Sarn wrote all three answers back in her own hand on the eighth, and copied them to Orra, and the third answer was this: *That the slate records what has been asked for and what has been entered. That the court will not say in writing that nothing will be done about a duty before the fourth month, because the court has no power to promise that and because a creditor is not bound by a clerk's letter. That the household of the upper seed barn has asked for the review to be called in the second month, that the request is on the slate with a date on it, and that the magistrate will decide whether to grant it, and nobody in this building can promise the household when.*

And Grice, in the ninth month, had also promised to hold to the first week of the fourth month and not one day past, and to do it only if Nessa's name came off the paper in front of him — and had never put that promise in writing, which Orra had asked him for twice.

Before he served, and seven days after he had written his enquiry to the window, Ilsabet Kell came up the upper lane on the arm of her son, which is a thing nobody in that lane had seen her do in four years, and she had come about a thing Orra Dunn had not asked for.

"The floor is not security," said the old woman, before she was inside. "I said it in writing in the ninth month and Bodo got it witnessed and I have it in a box. So I want you to hear me say what I am about to say and I want you not to thank me for it."

"Go on."

"I am willing to stand in the second month, in a room, with my own words, and be asked about the note, in front of Grice, in front of the factor, in front of whoever you want." She put her hand on the table. "I am sixty-one. I have buried a husband and I have lent money to four people in this lane and got two of it back, and I have never in my life been in a room where a thing I wrote was read out, and I have found out this month that I have been lending to a household with a duty standing on it without knowing that is what I was doing, and I would like that on the record in the correct words and not in yours."

"You know what it costs you."

"Write it out for me," said Ilsabet Kell, "because you are better at it than I am, and then tell me."

So Orra Dunn sat down at her own board with a charcoal stub in a loop of string round her wrist, and it took the better part of an hour, and what came out at the end was four lines in a woman's own words, and the fourth one was the one that mattered and the one that Orra could not make herself write for more than a minute:

*I am told that a court cannot promise that nothing will be done about the note of my son before the fourth month, and I have been told this by the clerk at the window and I believe her, and I am going to the second month anyway with my own words and my own note in my hand, and if it is taken then it is taken off a woman of sixty-one who lent it to a house of forty years standing and not to a girl of fifteen.*

"Read it back," said the old woman. "And do not read it back kindly."

Orra read it back. Then the old woman signed it, and Bodo witnessed it, and they walked down the lane, and Orra stood in the barn with the four lines in her hand and understood for the first time in three months what it was to be arguing for somebody else instead of being argued at, and that the whole of the skill consisted of writing the sentence that says *I know what this costs and I am doing it anyway* in the other person's own words, and then living in a house where the rye had gone thirty-one miles up a canal.

---

He served on the twenty-ninth of the first month, on a writ out of the Greyfen district, on the forty quarters of rye in the upper barn, and it was a full month earlier than the day he had named in his own door in the ninth month, and four weeks earlier than the day she had asked for on the slate, and Orra Dunn was standing in her own barn with a lamp when the man came up the lane with it, and Nessa was in the loft.

---

She had it out of the barn in a night and a day.

Forty quarters of rye is a cartload, and a cartload of rye in the first month of a winter in a district whose seed reserve is nineteen sacks short is not a thing you move in a hurry or at all, and what she moved it with was the two carts of a house four doors up who owed her nothing and bought forty quarters' worth of a winter tilling seed from her in the sixth month, and Bettany Skail, who walked down to the barn twice in that day without being asked and carried sacks up to the loft the whole time without being told to.

The tilling seed went into the loft, the rye went into the carts, and the carts went out of the upper lane at the fourth hour of the following morning, and the paper was served on forty quarters of rye that were, at the moment the paper was read out, thirty-one miles up a canal on their way to a mill at Coldsfoot with an unpriced sale agreed in an inn and not one signature on it yet.

Emrys Tale met her on the road with the cart, which Orra had not expected and would think about for a month.

"I sold him the paper," Tale said, "and I have been telling myself for three months that the reason was the hundred and six, and it was not the hundred and six."

"What was it."

"It was that I did not want to be the man who sold a paper on a girl of fifteen's rye," said Emrys Tale, "and I sold it anyway, and you have just made me drive four miles in the cold to be in the room when it happened, and I would like it noticed that I came."

"It is noticed," said Orra, "and it is written down, and I am going to say one thing about it and then I am not going to say anything else about it ever. You sold it. I would have sold it. There is a difference between the two of us and it is not a good one and I am not going to insult you by pretending there is."

The cart went on up the canal. Behind them, at the top of the upper lane, the four horses that had come for nothing were standing in the road with the boy who drove them asleep on the tiller, and Orra Dunn looked at them for about as long as it takes a lamp to be answered, and then she went down to the court window, because the hearing slate was on the wall and there was a line on it with a date on it and a line number, and the first thing anybody in the lane was going to ask her about the rye was not *where is it* but *what does the slate say*, and she had four days to make sure that the answer to the second question was a better one than the answer to the first.

Sarn was at the window. The request was still on the slate with *not granted* under it, in her own ink, with the second of the first month against it, and the hearing register above it had nineteen names on it and four of the nineteen had said the words *I do not understand this*, and both of those were the reason Orra Dunn had got four weeks and would say so to anybody who asked, in those words, in a yard, without dressing it up.

"Morning," she said, and put a shilling on the sill.

"For what?"

"For a copy of a line," said Orra Dunn, "on a day when a person can afford a shilling, which I could not do a week ago, and which is the only reason I am not standing here in the rain asking you to hold a date you cannot hold."

And in the loft of the upper seed barn, a girl of fifteen who had signed a school paper, a hoe and a seed-share paper, and who had asked in open court for the seed-trade examination and been told it was gated on the fourth month, came down off the loft ladder and stood in the middle of her mother's floor and said, "Ma. I am coming with you when you go to the fourth month. Whatever it is. I am not staying here and being right about it afterwards."
