# Chapter 8: The Sentence the Court Wrote

The court sat at two o'clock and the room had changed in the recess. More of the lane, and four men from the seed barns, and two women in Greyfen work-cloaks who had come in from the back door, and Hallis Dren of the Reed Cut water office standing at the side with his hat in his hands and a rolled paper under his arm, looking like a man who has arrived early for something and would rather be doing it later.

Rell came in and did not sit. She stood behind the bench with her hand on the exhibit sleeve, which was lying open on the clerk's table with the leaf, the correction, the memorandum, Pyle's certificate, and Tamsin Rook's comparison copy laid out on it in the order they had been received, which was a thing Marek had not asked for and had privately been grateful for.

"This court will hear the applicant on the instrument," Rell said, "and then the district, and then it will rule, and I am going to rule this afternoon because the district is two days from the fourteenth. I am going to say out loud, at the beginning, that I know what a ruling does. I know that what I say in this chair is not only between these people. Clerk Kest has told me that, and he is right, and it is not his fault that it is true, and I would still have had to be told, because I am not a fool and I have done this eleven times." She looked along the bench. "I am going to say a general thing. It will be wrong in some way I cannot see. I would rather say it here with the widow in the room than say it in a letter in March."

Nobody in the room said anything, and Marek felt the sentence land on him like a plate being set down in front of him.

"Mara Venn," said Rell.

Mara stood up and did not sit down again. She had her coat on and she had come down the lane in it, and she kept her hands folded in front of her the way she had kept the dish cloth.

"Tell the court what you signed, and what you did not sign, and what was brought to you on the field."

"I signed nothing," Mara said. "I was a witness. I stood in the water office in the second month of the year four years back and I heard my husband read a paper and I gave my name at the bottom, which is where a witness goes, and I made my mark at the foot and not at the side, and I have never in my life signed a continuation of anything."

"And in the sixth month?"

"In the sixth month a young clerk came up the lane with a paper and asked me to put my mark at the foot of it, and I asked him what it was for, and he said it was so the field would not be left without a hand if anything happened to Aldren, and I said my husband was dead and I have two hands, and he said it was about the field and not about me, and he put the paper away and went off up the lane, and he did not come back." She looked at the clerk's table, where the clerk's pen was still moving. "I told this court on the tenth of the month that I would say that twice and say it the same way both times, and I am not a woman who says a thing differently because it is being written down. So the court is going to hear it twice."

"Again," said Rell.

Mara Venn said it again, in the same words and in the same order, and the clerk wrote it out again, and Rell read both versions back to her and asked her whether they were the same account, and Mara Venn said that they were the same words and that this court was welcome to have both of them, and that she had come here expecting to be asked which of the two she preferred.

Pell Vey stood. "Magistrate, the widow's account establishes that she declined. It does not establish what her husband signed, and the applicant is relying on a leaf that has been in a cabinet, and the applicant's own comparison copy has no standing in this court, and I ask that the application be dealt with as an application and not as a finding."

"You will get your objection," Rell said, "in about nine minutes, and I am going to deal with it now so that it is done, because Clerk Kest has promised to put the leaf first and I am holding him to it. The exhibit is entered. The leaf is a source leaf of the Notaries' Table, produced under a court direction, in a sealed sleeve, with the Table's stamp on the wax, and the minute book has the time it came into this room, and I have no objection to it from any quarter, and if the Notaries' Table wishes to dispute its own leaf it may do so in writing and I will read it. The comparison copy is entered as a copy, not as a record, and it is entered because a court that refuses to see a copy cannot see a fraud, and the maker of the copy will be named in the record and she is at the back with her hands on her book. The application is one to compare a leaf against a correction. That is a small thing. It is what a court is for."

Pell sat down, and Marek understood that he had lost the procedural fight and that Pell had known he would, and that Pell had made him fight for it in front of a full room so that the record would show a junior clerk being corrected by his supervisor at a hearing his supervisor had come to observe. He filed that away and would think about it for a long time.

"Tavi Venn."

The girl stood up at the side of the bench and did not come forward, and she had the iron bar with her, which she had brought into a courtroom, and which she held by the middle like a thing she had been asked to put down and had not.

"I don't remember my father's hand," she said. "I was ten. I've seen the name and I've seen the double reed, and everybody at Reed Cut uses the double reed, so that isn't proof of anything and I'd like it written down that I said so."

"Write it down," said Rell's clerk, and did.

"I signed nothing. I was never a party to anything at Reed Cut except the water gate, where I'm not paid and where I go because the linkage is mine to know. Since the fourth month of this year I've been cutting the western ditch on the lower field at first light because nobody else would, and if you want to know why nobody else would it's because the whole lane has read a sentence about the field and nobody wants to be the first one caught holding it."

Somebody in the back of the room made a small sound. Rell did not look round.

"And what do you want this court to do," she said.

"I want it to say I don't owe it. And I want the gate reset, because it's two fingers out and the lower field's got until Thursday. And if it says I don't owe it, I want it to say it in words I can read to somebody, because there's going to be somebody in this lane who's waiting to be told, and I'd rather read it than be told."

"You will have the words," Rell said. "The clerk will read them before the court rises. Now, the district."

Hallis Dren took off his hat and held it against his chest and said that on the eighth of the eighth month he had issued a memorandum authorizing the season's bank works on the Reed Cut pending inspection and that the office would receive corrections against the district's records on the ninth, and that he had issued it to clear a backlog of eleven weeks because the district had four hundred and sixty relief rolls to certify and one clerk, and that he had not read the correction when it came in on the ninth because corrections under flood authority were entered and not argued.

"Would you have argued it," Rell said, "if you had read it?"

"I couldn't have, Magistrate. It isn't mine to argue. It's a scale instrument. It goes to the district." He turned his hat over. "What I can tell you is that the memorandum is about banks. It says bank works. It doesn't name a parcel, because a memorandum can't name four hundred and sixty parcels, and if I'd written a parcel into it I'd have had to write the other four hundred and fifty-nine and there was no time and there wasn't the paper. So the paper is general and the correction under it is particular, and I have thought about that a great deal since Tuesday."

"That is the most useful thing anybody has said in this room," Rell said. "Thank you. Sit down."

Then she stood behind the bench with both hands on it and said the part that Marek had been dreading since the bank in the dark.

"Clerk Kest has asked for a narrow ruling and has given me a reason. He says the original instrument names a person, and that a person is the only thing in this court that can be made to owe something, and that if the court reads *the present holder* into an instrument that says *Aldren Venn*, then every file in this district that has ever had a named person in it now has a door in it, and the door does not need a person, it only needs somebody to be standing there." She looked at the leaf. "He is right about the instrument. I have the leaf in front of me and I read it and it names him and it requires a signature from anybody who takes the duty on. That is a well-made instrument. Somebody made that instrument in a warm office four years before Aldren died and got it exactly right, and the reason it is exactly right is that it names the person, and if I let the field answer for the man then I have thrown away the only sentence in the document that matters."

"Then the court should rule for the widow," Pell said.

"The court should rule for the widow in the part that is about the widow," Rell said. "And it is going to rule against the applicant in the part that is about the district, and I am going to do it now, and Clerk Kest, you may stand up and object, and your objection will be in the record, and then I am going to rule anyway, because the district is going to flood and I have a bench full of people who are going to be standing in water in February."

Rell read it out. She read it slowly, and she read the whole of it, in the plain words, once, as the room required.

"Where immediate public necessity requires a recorded continuation to prevent loss of food or shelter, the present holder of the burdened property may be joined as guarantor until full review."

Then she said: "That is the sentence. It is not a sentence I invented. It is the sentence this court has been applying in flood matters since the third month, in letters, without a hearing, to about sixty households, and it is the reason nobody in this district was turned out of a house in the flood of the sixth year. It has never been stated in one piece before and it has never been heard. I am stating it. I am doing it in an open court with the widow in the room, and I am hearing the widow, and I am hearing her daughter, and I am hearing a district water clerk who has told me he had eleven weeks of backlog in front of him. That is as clear as I can make a hearing, and if it is not clear enough then the law is in the wrong hands and I would like somebody in this room to write that down and post it."

Marek said, "Magistrate, the widow does not consent to the sentence."

"I have not asked her to consent to it. It is not hers. It is the court's, and it is a rule of this court, and I have made it in a hearing that she has heard."

"Then I ask the court to narrow it to the burdened property and to require a signature before the duty attaches, as the original instrument does."

"And I am going to refuse that, because if a signature is required then the sixty households in the sixth year get nothing, and because a signature that a frightened person is asked for in February is not a signature." She said it flatly. "I know how that sounds in a room that has just listened to a copied notch. I would still rather have them alive and signed in March."

---

In the recess she went to the exhibit with two people, which was a thing she did at the end of a long morning, and Marek went with her because the clerk of the court let him and then wished she had not.

She had a single sheet in her hand, in his own writing, with a boy's mud on the bottom of it, and she read it standing up and then read it again.

"Where did this come from."

"The step of the Venn house, on the evening of the tenth, in a wind, in the dark, from a girl of seventeen who had brought it up out of the field." He did not soften it, because she had read him by now. "She asked for the reset and the bank work to be put on the water office, in writing, with the office's own name on it, and for the order to say that the household is not to do the field-side for it. She said it in one sentence and then she said that she had been cutting that ditch since the fourth month and meant to go on, and that if the sentence says a person has to sign for the work then the first thing this lane will say is that she has been doing it, so she has taken it, so it is hers, and she would rather it was the water office's in a court than hers in a lane."

Rell read the sheet a third time.

"That is a small thing," she said, "and it is the whole case."

"It is the smallest thing in the file."

"It is the only thing in the file that says what is to happen on Friday morning," said Rell, "and the district will have to live with it for a season. I am putting it in. I am putting it in last, so that the last thing anybody reads out of this room is not a sentence about a woman and is an instruction to an office, and I would like that noticed." She folded the sheet once and handed it back to him. "Do not thank me for it in front of the lane. If they hear that I got it from a girl with an iron bar, then the next girl will be told to fetch a magistrate herself, and she will be sent away."

She ruled at twenty past three, and she read the operative words out one at a time, and she numbered them as she went, because a court that numbers its terms is a court that means for them to be cited, and the clerk wrote the numerals down as fast as Rell said them.

"The first:" and then the debt named against Tavi Venn was void, it having never been hers, no person being answerable under the said instrument except the person named in it.

"The second:" and the channel and the lower field were preserved to the household of Aldren Venn for the remainder of the term, that is to say until the second month, when seven years from the signing are up, and no creditor, tenant, surety, or assignee might take the crop or the ground before that date, and the same was preserved to the widow.

"The third:" and the gate and the navigation duty at the Reed Cut remained with Sivra Oris, keeper, according to the terms of the instrument, and were not affected by any of the above, and the court noted that the keeper had declined both the work of the dead man and any transfer of her own duty into his estate.

"The fourth:" and any person who thereafter assumed a continuing field-side duty at that place should sign a new obligation for it, the original instrument requiring it.

"And the fifth:" and the district water office should perform the bank works and the reset of the gate linkage under its own flood authority, and not under the obligations of the said instrument, and should do so before the season advanced; and the household should not be required to perform the field-side upkeep of the said instrument for the purposes of this order.

"That is the whole of it," Rell said, "and I want the room to have one thing straight. The sentence I read you at two o'clock is not one of these five. It is this court's practice, it is a bad habit, and it is the reason I am obliged to use it. These five are about one widow, one field, one keeper and one office. Somebody is going to count them wrong. If they do, I want them to have heard me say that they are five."

That last one was Tavi's, and it had come to the bench on the back of a witness note in his own hand with mud on it, and Rell had read it three times in the recess and had called it a small thing and the whole case, and then had put it last on purpose, so that the last sentence anybody carried out of this room would be an instruction to an office and not a sentence about a woman.

Marek looked at the bench. Tavi Venn was sitting down for the first time since the morning, with the bar across her knees, and the woman beside her had put a hand flat on her back. Mara Venn had not moved at all. She had her hands folded and she was looking at the leaf on the clerk's table, at the double reed in the margin, at her husband's name, and her face was doing nothing at all, which Marek had learned was the face she made when she was feeling most.

He felt it come up in him like air out of a lung. Nine days of a courier's satchel and a copied notch and a source leaf out of a cabinet in a bad coat, and a girl of seventeen would be able to say the words *I do not owe it* in her own lane, in daylight, and the field would be there in the morning.

He had not eaten properly since the seventh of the month. His teeth hurt. He was so tired that the wall behind the bench looked further away than it was.

Sarn lifted the sleeve.

"Clerk Kest," she said. "The court has made an audit of the Venn file, named to the instrument, the exhibit, and no other thing. The exhibit is out. You may look at it."

He came down. The clerk turned the sleeve so the whole of it faced him at once, and on top of the six things that had gone into it that morning, in the chair's own hand and under the chair's own seal, was the sentence, and the five terms written out under the sentence and numbered.

And the record in front of him was not the leaf.

He knew that before he looked, the way you know a room is not the room you think it is. What the court was acting on this afternoon was not the original instrument. It was the filed Venn record — the packet, the correction, the docket, the summary, the ruling — and the sentence the court had just made was now part of it, in the same ink, with the same seal, dated the twelfth, and it was part of it now in a way that would be true from this moment on and could not be untrue again by anybody in this room.

He looked at it, because the court had ordered it and he had accepted the order.

> Named: the present holder of the Venn field.
>
> Promised: the present holder may be joined as guarantor until full review.
>
> Carried after death: the record carries a guarantor duty to whoever occupies the field unless a new hearing ends it.

He stood at the clerk's table with the sleeve open in front of him and the whole thing was accurate. Every word of it was true about the record he was looking at, and it was true because he and Tamsin Rook had put the correct document in front of a court, and the court had done exactly what a court does, which is answer the question that was asked.

The ruling had said the debtor was not Tavi Venn. The reading did not say Tavi Venn. The reading said the field, and whoever was standing in it, and it had a shape that would go on standing in it after everybody in this room was dead.

He felt it go through him the way a held note goes through a wall when somebody in the next room plays it wrong. There was a pressure behind his eyes and it was not pain, exactly, and there was a taste in his mouth as though somebody had blown out a lamp and he had breathed the smoke. His hands were cold. The court clerk said something to him and he heard it a moment later than she said it.

"Clerk Kest. Are you unwell?"

"I'm well. I'm reading."

He put his hand flat on the table, because the table was there, and he read the three lines again, and they were the same three lines, and he understood that they were not going to change, and that no amount of standing in this room was going to make them name a woman instead of a piece of ground.

"Sarn," he said. "I want the sleeve closed and I want the reading entered in the minute book as it came, in those words, with the time on it, and I want the roll-keeper to have a copy of the reading in her own hand before the court rises."

"The minute book is not a place for readings, Kest."

"Then put it in your own book," he said. "Put it in yours and put it in hers and give it to the widow. I am not asking you whether it is important. I am asking you to write it down where it cannot be lost, and I am telling you why, and I am telling the whole room, because the whole room needs it." He turned round. "The court has made Tavi Venn not a debtor. That stands. I have read the record and it stands and I have no doubt in it. And the same record now says that whoever holds that field may be joined as guarantor until full review, and the field does not have a name on it, and names are the only thing this court can hear. Somebody will put somebody's name in that gap. Not today. Not this year. Somebody, in a room, in a hurry, with a good reason. And I would like the widow to have those three lines in her hand before that happens, and I would like them to be the words the court wrote and not the words somebody remembered."

The room was completely quiet except for the water under the door.

Rell said, from the bench, "Clerk Kest. Is that a reading, or is that an opinion."

"That's an opinion," Marek said, "and I have the floor of the court in about forty seconds, so I would like to be permitted to keep the two of them apart for the sake of the record."

"Permitted. Sarn, enter it as he asks. Copyist Rook, take it in your own hand, and give a copy to Mrs Venn before the court rises."

Marek walked back up to the bench. His head was going. There was a headache starting behind his left eye and settling in, in the way his headaches always settled, along a line he could have described to a physician, and it was going to be there for a day and a night and possibly two, and his mouth tasted of ash, and there was a pressure behind his eyes that had a rhythm to it, in and out, in and out, like something breathing that was not him.

On the bench, Rell was reading the operative words aloud, slowly, in the plain words, as she had promised Tavi Venn she would.

Tavi stood up and moved to the front, and Mara stood up and went with her, and Tavi said, "Read the third one again."

"I have read it once, and the record requires that I read it once."

"Read it again anyway. It's for the lane, not for the record."

Rell read it again, and this time she read it looking at the bench, not at the paper, and the room took it down in the sound of her voice rather than the words, which is the whole difference between a reading and a hearing, and which was the only thing Marek had left to teach anybody in this room.

At the door, while the bench was emptying and the lane was discussing the fourth term, Orra Dunn of the upper seed barn came in with her daughter, from the back, and the clerk tried to stop them, and the girl got past.

She was fifteen and she had a shawl on in a season that did not need one, and she came up the aisle and stopped in front of the bench and said, in a voice that carried the way Tamsin's had, "Ma says you wrote a sentence. She says you said the person in the house has to answer for it if the seed can't be paid. Is that right."

Rell looked at her. "Are you Nessa Dunn."

"Yes."

"Have you signed anything at that court."

"No."

"Then no," Rell said, "not from this court, and not in that house, and not by me." And then she stopped, because she had heard the shape of what was coming, and Marek watched her arrive at it at the same moment he did, and watched her understand that a magistrate cannot unmake a sentence in front of the room, and can only make the next one.

"Sarn," she said. "What is the status of the Dunn summary."

Sarn looked at her ledger for a moment and then stopped looking at it.

"Entered on the docket at two o'clock this afternoon, Magistrate. On the authority of the sentence read at two. Filed."

Marek turned round.

The roll-keeper from the upper lane was standing in the doorway with a folded paper in her hand and the folder open on the bench, and it was half past three, and outside the water was coming up the steps again.
