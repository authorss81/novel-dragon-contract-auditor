# Chapter 15: The Second Box

The house book of the Greyfen dye-house was a ledger of everything on the benches, and it had been kept for nineteen years, and it was the single most useful object in the building and the single most dangerous, and Nell Kest had never in her life wanted to read a page of it until the ninth of the tenth month, when the assessor sent for the foreman and the foreman sent for her.

"Take your clause," Ostyn Vare said. "And take the thing Tamsin Rook sent up for you in the spring. And leave your wages on the bench, because the wages are not the conversation."

"Why not."

"Because the wages are a schedule and the schedule is fine and I have read it," said Vare, "and the thing I want you to hold in your hand is the reason you are in the room, and I would like you to have both so that you can see I have not dressed it up for the guild."

---

Marl Crenn's room over the lane's dye-store had a window, a stove, one chair for a visitor, and about nine hundred forms in a deal rack, and she was fifty-one years old and had been the dyers' schedule assessor for eleven of the fifty-one and had signed four houses' exemptions in that time, and when Nell came in she looked at the clause in the girl's hand and then at the man standing up by the stove and said, "Mr Vare, is the girl sitting, because there is a chair and I would like her to use it."

Nell sat down.

"That is better," said Crenn. "Everybody stands up in this room and it takes an hour twice a year. Now. Which of these two is the one you have come about."

Nell put the wage schedule on the desk. The amended clause was on the second leaf, in Tamsin Rook's hand, which was much better than Nell's, and it said, in the words they had gone over for four evenings: that where a worker of this house left the craft before the end of her wage term, the tools in her charge and any debt of the house against her in respect of those tools should be answered by that worker and by no other person; that the debt should be set out in a sum, and paid within a stated term of eight weeks; and that any balance remaining at the end of that term, together with any cost of recovery, should be carried by the Greyfen Dyers' Tool Fund and by no household and no worker.

Crenn read it twice, and the second time she read it with a finger on the last line, where the name of the fund went, and the name of the fund went in as *the Greyfen Dyers' Tool Fund* and not as *the guild* and not as *the house*.

"That is not a fund that exists," she said.

"It exists on Thursday," said Nell. "The assessors make it on Thursday. It is us four houses, the same money as the levy, and it carries a tool debt after eight weeks, and that is all it does."

"It costs money."

"It costs every house money," said Vare. "That is the point of it and I have known it since the evening you told me, and I have come here to agree to it, so you will not have to ask me twice."

Crenn put the clause down and looked at him.

"Mr Vare, I have been eleven years in this room and I have signed four exemptions and I have never once had a foreman walk in and offer me money before I asked for it." She straightened the forms on the desk that did not need straightening. "Now I am going to tell you why I have not slept, and then you are both going to tell me what you think I am frightened of, because you have both already worked it out and I would rather have it said by a working woman of twenty than work it out myself in front of two of my own assessors."

---

"I am frightened of the second box," she said.

The rack behind her had about nine hundred forms in it and every one of them had a box on the back.

"The dyers' exemption is the oldest form in this trade and it is four boxes. It says the house keeps its tools. It says the house keeps its standing. It says the house may take an apprentice against a stated term, and may not take one against a bare promise of labour. And it has a fifth thing on the back, which is not a box at all, it is a line, and the line says that no second instrument in that house may be read without this instrument, and where a second instrument names a person the first instrument is to be read with it.

"That line has been on the form since before I came here. Nobody has ever read it out because in thirty-one years no second instrument in any of these four houses has ever named a person. The wage schedules name a rate and a term. The apprenticeship papers name a master and a term. Everything in this trade is a thing about the *house*, and the whole of the exemption is built on that, and it is a good thing and it is the only reason a woman can hold a craft seat in this province at all without a man in her house.

"And on the twelfth of a month this year a court read out a ruling that says a duty does not pass to whoever comes next unless that person signs. And that is not a ruling about dye-houses. It is a ruling about a field. It is in twenty-six houses. It is on a bench in this building, because Mr Vare put it on that bench in the fourteenth of the ninth month and it has been on that bench ever since, and my clerk listed the bench in the house book in the ordinary way on the second of the month because that is what the house book is for.

"So the second box of the exemption has got a copy of a court ruling in it. And the copy names a person — it names three, one of whom is in this building and one of whom is a woman who owns the gate at Reed Cut — and therefore the first instrument is to be read with it. And when you read this instrument with that one, what this instrument turns out to say is that a tool debt in this house is a thing the house carries, and the next person in the house answers for it, and the seat of the craft is held on that footing. Thirty-one years of certification, Mr Vare. Not one year. Thirty-one years, four houses, and about forty-one apprentices, and the whole of what they have is that a house is allowed to keep its own ground."

She stopped, and put her hand flat on the deal rack, and Nell saw that the hand was not steady and understood that Marl Crenn was a person frightened of a form and not a person who had decided to be cruel to a girl of twenty, and also understood that a frightened person holding a form is exactly the kind of person who signs the form.

---

Nell said, "Ma'am, may I ask you a question that is none of my business."

"You may ask me any question at all, that is the difference between you and the other three houses."

"If we take the ruling off the bench, does the box empty."

Crenn turned and looked at her.

"That," she said, "is the first question anybody has asked me about that form in eleven years, and I am going to answer it honestly, and the honest answer is no."

Nell sat with that.

"The bench gets swept," said Crenn. "The bench is not a court and nothing on it is an instrument until it is listed, and what is listed is what a form is read with, and the bench is swept every fourth day by a woman called Nessa Bell on a Thursday, and I have been signing forms for eleven years and I have never once thought about a Thursday, and if I tell you that taking a paper off a bench empties a box then I have told you a lie to a woman of twenty to get a week out of my own office. And in a month, when a bench has been swept four times, the paper is on the bench again, because it is a ruling and it is in this lane in twenty-six houses and four of them are in Greyfen, and there is no version of this in which it is not on a bench in a dyers' house by the second month."

"Then it does not matter what the bench says," said Vare. "It only matters that we get a second instrument in first, that names a person, and that the second instrument says the debt is hers and not the next woman's, and that there is somebody at the end of it who is not a household."

"That is the clause in front of me," said Crenn.

"Yes," said Vare. "And it is the difference between a bad clause and no clause, and I want to say out loud, in front of the girl, that if she signs nothing and goes and the house keeps the old one, then I am the man who had a document in his hand for three weeks that would have told him a departing worker's tools do not follow her, and I am not going to pretend I did not have it. I would rather lose the house's exemption trying than keep it on the strength of a clause I read aloud to her and then used against her."

---

They drafted the amendment on the back of the levy sheet because there was nothing else to write on, and the last line of it took longer than the rest.

The Tool Fund needed a name and a number and a person. A fund that carries a debt is a creditor, and a creditor with no name is a person nobody can be angry at, which was the exact shape of the trouble from the beginning, and Nell said so, twice, and Marl Crenn wrote it down both times because she thought it was the correct procedure to write down the things a person says when they have been told that the room is not an office.

It ended at the fifth hour of the afternoon. The debt goes to the departing worker, named, with the sum set out; eight weeks; the balance and the costs of recovery to the Greyfen Dyers' Tool Fund; the levy on four houses to be set by the assessors at their next sitting; and the fund to be a body and not a clause, meaning that it would have a name, a keeper, a book, and a year of accounts, and would be inspected, and would go on existing after all four of them were dead.

"Read it," said Crenn.

Nell read it. It was Tamsin Rook's hand and Tamsin Rook's sentences and it did not sound like a woman of twenty writing about vats, and Nell got through about two thirds of it before she had to stop for a moment and stand at the window for a while, and neither Vare nor Crenn said anything about it.

"Sign it," Nell said, coming back. "Both of you. He signs for the house and you sign for the assessors and the fund has got to be made before it can be signed for, so put the date on it that the fund is made and sign it, and I do not want a clause with a hole in it."

Crenn read the amendment again, and then she took out the exemption itself and read the fifth line on the back of it aloud, in full, in the room.

"I am going to sign the exemption," she said, "for this house, this year, on the foot of this amendment, and I am going to do it in front of both of you and I am going to put the date on it, and I am going to tell you both exactly what I am doing, because I have read this room's reasons in a form for eleven years and not one of them has ever been the truth.

"I am not signing because I believe it. I am signing because a form has been read with another form and the box is full, and the box is going to be full in the other three houses by the second month whether I sign or not, and if I do not sign then this house loses the seat and forty-one apprentices lose the standing and Mr Vare loses his post, and I will have saved nothing. I will have lost the same four houses and I will not have got a clause." She looked up. "I am signing because a form that has been read is a form that will be read again, and I would rather it be read the second time with my name on it and my reason in your daughter's mouth than read the second time by somebody who has not met either of you."

She signed it, and dated it, and gave the date of the assessors' sitting to Nell, and then she said the thing that Nell took out of the room and carried to a workroom and a lane and eventually to a bench in a dye-house and eventually to a person in a court corridor:

"Miss Kest, I want to say the true thing about your clause and then I am going to sign the amendment, in that order, and if you take the second part and leave the first part behind then I have not done my job.

"Your clause is good. It is the best thing anybody in this trade has put in front of me in eleven years. A woman can leave a craft and take her own tools and does not have to sell herself to the next one to get out. I want that. I have wanted that since I was your age and could not get it.

"And it is a form. Not a clause — a form. It is going to be printed, and it is going to have a number on it, and it is going to be in a deal rack in this office, and in a month the Marrowgate house is going to hear that a form with a number has been accepted here and the Marrowgate house has a foreman who has been here thirty-one years and who will read it and will say: if that is accepted then what was I exempt on, and I will have to answer him. And a clause that is good for one household and turns out to be dangerous for another is not a clause. It is a form. And forms travel, Miss Kest, in the way that a form travels, which is that you do not have to want it to travel, you only have to have used it once in front of people who are frightened."

---

The last hour of the tenth month was spent getting it copied, because the assessors' rule was that an amendment was not an amendment until it was in the form, and a form was not a form until it was a printed thing with a series on it, and the series on this one was the dyers' fourth series, amendment two, and the number was in the top corner, and it went into the rack on a Thursday, and Marl Crenn wrote the number on a card and gave the card to Nell, who could read numbers and could not read fast.

"They will bring it round themselves," Crenn said. "There is a man comes round with the rack on a Tuesday. He will go to the Marrowgate and the Beckery and the Nine Elms and the Caudle, and he will ask a foreman to sign, and a foreman will ask what happens if he does not, and the man on the rack does not know, because a carrier does not know, and I will be asked to know, and by then the four houses have had a fortnight to be frightened on their own account."

Marek Kest was not in the room and had not been asked to be, and Tamsin Rook was not in the room either, and Nell had copied out the amendment herself in the workroom on the evening of the ninth and had got four things wrong in it and found the fourth on the following morning.

At the end of the week the man with the rack had been to the Marrowgate house and had come back with the form signed, and the Beckery had asked for a copy to take to their own assessor, and the Nine Elms had asked for four weeks, and the Caudle house had not said anything at all to anybody, and the four of them between them had raised the levy on four houses by nine pounds a quarter, which was a real sum of money, and Nell worked out on the flat of the dye vat with a bit of chalk where nine pounds a quarter came out of, and found that it came out of four places, and that the place it came out of hardest was a woman on four and six at the Caudle house who had four children and no other trade.

Vare read the numbers over her shoulder and did not say anything about them, and she looked at the two of them in the reflection in the vat and said, "Then it is a form, and it has a number on it, and four houses on a lane have not been asked to sign anything, and two of them will sign it because they are frightened and one of them has said nothing, and the one that has said nothing is the one that will cost the Caudle house its assessment in the fourth month."

"The man on the rack will go round again," Vare said.

"The man on the rack will go round again," Nell agreed, "and he will not know what is on the back of it, and I have got a number in my coat and I am going to go round the lane myself, and if anybody asks me why I am doing it I am going to tell them the truth, which is that I did not want to be standing in this workroom in the third month wondering whether nine pounds a quarter was going to come out of somebody's winter, and then finding out."

She put the chalk down.

"And you are not coming with me," she said.

"No," said Vare.

"That is right," said Nell, "because a foreman walking round a lane asking people to sign something is a foreman telling four houses what the guild has decided. If I walk round, I am a woman who has left a craft and taken her own tools, and that is a thing four foremen cannot answer, and it is the only card I have got, and I am going to spend it once."
