# Chapter 21: Forty Lines

The board on the wall of the Reed Cut district water office had forty lines on it and no dates, and Hallis Dren had put it up himself in the eighth month of the fifth year because a board with dates on it is a promise and a board without dates is a list, and in the Reed Cut office a list had always been the honest form of the thing.

On the eleventh of the eleventh month he came in at the seventh hour, took his coat off, and put his thumb on the board, and then stood there for a quarter of an hour without doing anything at all, which was not his habit, and the stove, which was lit now and had been lit every working day since the first frost, ticked at him.

The fifth term's work was done. The gate was back inside a quarter of an inch and the west bank had been turfed with turf the office had taken off its own boundary strip, and the crew under Wendt Sayer had gone back to the peat in the middle of the week and to the drains in the flats in the morning, and the sheet with Dren's name on it was still inside the rail at the gate with four nails in it and the cord going slack.

What was left on the board was the district's own season, and the district's own season was forty lines, and Dren could have said any of the forty by heart, and every one of them was going to come due in the fourth month, and the fourth month was when the rate went on the parcels and when the low flats went under, and in nine of the last ten years the fourth month was the one that decided whether people ate late.

That was the number he could defend. Nine years out of ten, the fourth month was the month that came off a person's rate and went through their own door.

What he could not defend was anything that happened after the fourth month, and everything that had happened since the eighth of the eighth month had happened somewhere he was not.

---

He got out the season's papers at about the eighth hour and laid them on the bench in the order he had learned to lay things in, which was the order they were written and not the order they were filed, and which was a habit he had never explained to anybody and which had come from a man in a Chancery gallery and not from a family.

There were five things he wanted and he could not get any of them by asking, so he got all five by working them out.

The first was the count of flood-season continuity filings in the district's own docket. He had it in a book under the counter, because the office kept a copy of the court's flood list the way it kept a copy of the gate schedule, for the ordinary reason that the two of them had to agree and nobody ever made them agree. Fifty-two. Fifty-two filings accepted under flood authority in the Reed Cut district this season, entered the way the district entered them, which was in the routine column and not one at a time.

Fifty-two, and not one of them itemised, and not one of them heard on its own, and that was not a decision anybody in the district had made. It was a column.

The second thing was the hours. He had a habit he had never told his mother about, which was that when he was worried he counted, and so he counted the window.

He wrote the window out on the back of a form, in his upright hand, because he could not trust himself to hold it in his head.

> *The Mosswake clerk's window is open six hours. There are four hundred and sixty flood-season relief rolls to certify, and the last day they can be certified is the sixth of the fourth month, because that is the day the rate is struck. The window certifies about eleven a day when the window is certifying and not doing anything else, which in a flood season is perhaps two days in three.*
>
> *Six weeks is forty-two days. Forty-two days at eleven is four hundred and sixty-two, and we need four hundred and sixty, and that is not a coincidence: a district clerk sizes a flood season so that the window can just carry it, and carries it on the days nothing else is on her. There are two rolls of it spare in the whole season and I have had both of them in my head since the eighth month.*
>
> *So if a process is to be finished by the fourth month, and the last six weeks of it are the last six weeks of the window, then there is nothing in those six weeks at all. A day spent on something that is not a relief roll is a relief roll that is not certified before the rate is struck. A relief roll that is not certified before the rate is struck is a household that eats in the wrong weeks.*

He read it back and then wrote the other side of it, which is the part that is only his, and which he would have to look at again in six months when somebody showed it to him.

Then the third thing, and this was the one he had to go and ask a foreman about.

---

Wendt Sayer came in from the flats at the ninth hour with his hands white to the wrist, because the peat is not clean and the water carries the clay.

Dren put the book on the bench and turned it round so it was the right way up for Sayer, who could read numbers and had never in his life pretended otherwise.

"Two hours," Dren said. "That is my question. If a person comes to the window about one of those fifty-two — comes because they have heard something about their own barn, say — and the clerk writes down what they say and reads it back and it goes into a book, how long does that take."

Sayer looked at him.

"Two hours is a long time at a window," he said. "A man will talk for an hour if you let him and you have to read it back twice, and the reading back is the part that cannot be hurried, because the whole of the value is that he has heard his own words and not yours. And the clerk has to open the file, and the file is not where the window is."

"Two hours."

"Call it two hours and be honest that it is nearer three when the person has come a long way and is angry, which they will be, because a person who has come to a window about a duty they did not sign is not in a good mood, and I have watched men sign things they did not read because a clerk was tired at the end of a long day and the man in front of him was tired in a different way and did not want to be the one who took another hour of him."

"Three hours."

"Three hours and a shilling and sixpence for the witness if you pay one, which the district court does, which is at the rate the court already pays and not a rate I made up, because the rate is the rate."

Dren wrote it down. Fifty-two filings at three hours is a hundred and fifty-six hours, and a hundred and fifty-six hours at a six-hour window is twenty-six days, and twenty-six days out of forty-two is more than half the window given away to hearings that do not certify a single roll.

Then he did the subtraction, twice, because it was the only figure in the whole thing he was going to be judged on.

Sixteen days at eleven a day is a hundred and seventy-six. That is what gets certified. Four hundred and sixty less a hundred and seventy-six is two hundred and eighty-four, and that is the figure, and two hundred and eighty-four households out of four hundred and sixty have their seed parcels come off the rate late, in the worst month in nine years out of ten, because somebody was interrupted to hear them, and every one of those two hundred and eighty-four is on a list he has had in his head since the eighth month and has not shown anybody.

He put the pen down.

"That is a bad number," he said.

"That is the correct number," Sayer said. "Do you want me to make it a good number?"

"No."

"Then stop looking at it like a man who has made a mistake."

---

The fourth thing was money, and money was small, and that was the part that had been sitting at the back of Dren's head since the eighth hour.

He went into the works fund. The works fund is not a large thing. It is what is left in a district after the peat is cut and the drains are done and the clerk's wages are paid, and Dren could draw on it for the running of an office and for nothing else, and the running of an office comes to about four shillings a week in the winter, which is stove and candle and a little oil and the copying paper the office buys for the gate schedules.

The hearing window, if there was going to be one, would want a second chair, and paper for the forms, and the forms written out twice, and the stove kept lit two hours past the time it used to go out.

Eleven shillings for six weeks. He wrote it down and looked at it. It was so small a sum that it did not appear anywhere in the district's accounts as a line of consequence, and it was the entire cash cost of a thing that was going to cost the district half its certification time, and that was the shape of the whole decision and every decision like it in every flood district in nine provinces, and the reason it was going to be made in a fortnight by somebody in a warm office who was not going to be here for it.

The fifth thing was the only one he could not count, and he wrote that down too, in one line, at the bottom, and underlined it.

*What a person is put to who is not heard, in a district where a filing is not heard, is not a number this office can produce.*

He looked at that line for a while. Then he took the reed out and ruled it through, and wrote above it: *Unknown. Not zero. Unknown.*

---

The arithmetic went up on the same evening.

It was four sides of the office's own paper, in his upright hand, and it had no seal on it and no letter at the top of it, and it was headed in his hand: *Fourth month, Reed Cut district. What happens either way.* Then four columns.

The first column was the position with no rule. Fifty-two filings unitemised. Four hundred and sixty rolls in forty-two days at the window's own rate, which is enough by two rolls, and two is nothing. No hearing anybody can ask for, because the court's window is not obliged, and nobody in the district is at fault for that, because the office that says so has not got a person in it.

The second column was the position with a single answer. The same fifty-two. The same four hundred and sixty. Every one of the forty-two days certifying, because no clerk has to stop what she is doing to write down what a man says about his own barn. The rate struck on the sixth. The seed parcels issued on the seventh. No hours lost at all, and Dren put that in as a figure, because a figure of zero is worth more in a column than a paragraph.

The third column was what the second column cost, and Dren wrote it in one line and made himself write it in the same column rather than below it, on the ground that below it would have been a complaint and in it would be a fact.

*In the district that is me: 52 households heard individually, once, at a window, in the fourth month, or not. The sentence is the same in every column. The only column in which a person is spoken to is one in which somebody is interrupted to do it.*

The fourth column was money, and it was eleven shillings, and it was in every column except the second.

He read the four columns back to himself in the order a foreman reads them, which is the order that has no feelings in it, and then he read the third column again, and then he did the thing he had been not doing all afternoon, which was to write the sentence that had been at the back of his head since the fourth hour.

He wrote it in the second column, in the space under the zero, because it belonged there and because it was the reason the second column was the second column.

*One answer gets the rolls out on time. This is not an argument about the sentence. The sentence is not mine and I have not read the file. It is a fact that in nine years I have not seen one month in which a district that knew what to do did not feed its people, and I have seen one month in which it did, and I know which of the two is the one this column is worth.*

Then he put the pen down and sat back, and looked at what he had written, and understood that he had just described a reason for the sentence to be made empire-wide, and that he had described it in a district where the seed reserve is nineteen sacks short and his own mother's canal house was the security on a sheet nailed to a rail at a gate four hundred yards away.

He was not going to take it out.

He read it once more and then he made himself do the other half of it, which was to write, at the foot of the fourth side, in the same upright hand, the sentence that made the third column a cost and not a complaint.

*Nobody has to be interrupted to hear a person. Somebody does. That is the whole of the difference between the second column and the first, and it is not a large sum, and it is not mine, and if it is not somebody's then it is nobody's, and a duty that is nobody's is a duty that is heard by nobody until it is too late to hear it.*

---

He copied it out three times that night at the kitchen table, because a thing written once in a hand people know is a thing that can be talked out of, and the copies went out on the morning post.

The first went to the Mosswake Roll Court, addressed to the magistrate, because the court was the body whose docket it was and because a magistrate is allowed to be shown arithmetic.

The second went to Upper Cut and the third to Millrace, because those were the other two water offices in the district and because a district is not a district if only one of its three offices has heard a number, and because he had learned that in the fifth year, in a way he could not have explained to a person who had not sat on a file for four days.

The fourth went to the office of Examinations in Auremar, on the ground that the thing it described was being decided there, and the card said *district water office, Reed Cut, flood district of Mosswake*, and under his name, because his name was the only part of it that could be called to account, and because he had put his name on a sheet nailed to a rail in the dark in the ninth month and had discovered that a name on paper is a different thing from a name on a nail and does not go anywhere, and he had decided that he would rather have the paper one.

At the foot of the copy that went to Examinations he put the two sentences that had cost him the most to write and that he would have to look at again in six months when somebody showed them to him.

*The officer signing this has a certification, and his mother's canal house is the security on the works schedule of the twentieth of the ninth, and neither of those is in this letter except as a fact about the writer. He has not read the file. He has read a column.*

*If it is a convenience to have one answer, say so in a document. A convenience that is not written down gets argued for six months by men who were not in the district.*

He sealed the four and took them down to the packet post himself at the seventh hour rather than send them with the works rider, and the postmistress at Reed Cut looked at the addresses and said that Examinations was a long way to send a water clerk's arithmetic, and Dren said that it was, and that was the reason he was sending it himself and not waiting for a season to be kind.

---

He walked home by the canal because it was a half hour longer and it was dry, and his mother's house is the second one past the second lock, and it is a small house with a shop-window that has not been a shop for nineteen years, and he had been raised in it and had signed for it in the eighth month of his apprenticeship, and it was the only thing he owned that was worth anything to anybody.

He stopped outside it, in the ordinary way, and then did not go in, and stood for a while on the towing path in the dark with the canal going under the lock and the water making the noise it makes in a cold month.

He had signed a general paper in the eighth month of this year because there were eleven weeks of backlog in the office and a flood memorandum wanted a filing to sit under, and he had known a correction would come in afterwards, and the correction had come in on the ninth, and the whole of the next ten weeks had happened because a clerk had put a name in a box on a form that asked for the district and the instrument and the ground and the person.

He could not prove a single link between that form and anything in the letter he had just posted to Auremar, and he was not going to say there was one, and he was also not going to sit in a warm room in the fourth month being told by a man he had never met that the question had been settled at the top and the district should get on with it.

His mother came out with a lamp because the lamp was lit in the front window and she had seen a man standing on the path who was not coming in.

"That you, Hallis."

"Yes."

"You've sent a post."

"I have."

She looked at the road rather than at him, in the way of a woman who has lived beside a canal for fifty-one years and knows what a long face is for.

"What's in it."

"The numbers," Dren said. "That's all. It's numbers, and I've said what happens either way, and I haven't said what should happen, and I've put my name on it."

His mother put the lamp down on the door sill and straightened up, which she did not need to do, and looked at him for a moment in a way he had not been looked at by anybody since the eighth month.

"Put the numbers on the wall as well," she said.

"The office doesn't have room."

"Not for want," said his mother, "and I'll not have you the only man in this district who can say what a month is going to cost. There's four hundred and sixty of you and every one of you is going to be told in the fourth month that the sentence is settled, and I would like there to be a piece of paper in this house that says a clerk put four columns on it in the eleventh month, before anybody knew, and said the numbers out loud, and did not say what ought to be done."

And Hallis Dren, who had a certification and a mother's canal house and nine men and a boy and a foreman and forty lines on a wall, stood on a towing path in the dark and could not think of a single thing to say.

So he said, "I'll bring you a copy in the morning," and she said, "You'll bring me a copy now, and I'll read it by candle, and you can tell me the parts I can't read out loud, because my eyes went in the second year and I'm not going to pretend to anybody that they didn't."

And they went in together, and the four sides went on the kitchen table under a candle, and Dren read the columns out loud to his mother in the order a foreman reads them, which is the order that has no feelings in it, and which was the only reason he had ever been able to say any of it at all.
