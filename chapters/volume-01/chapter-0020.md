# Chapter 20: Nine Hundred and Forty

The passage behind the third gallery of the Roll Hall was eleven feet long and had a window at the end of it that looked into the well between the second and the third galleries, and the well was the only place in the building where a person could stand without being asked what they wanted, and the rack was at the other end of the passage with its back to the wall, and there was no lock on the rack, and there had never been a lock on the rack, because a lock on a rack in a Chancery corridor says *this is worth stealing* and a rack with a lock on it gets audited and a rack without a lock on it does not.

It was called the Forms Received rack, and it held four hundred and some printed things that other offices had sent to the Chancery, and it had been in the passage since before the third gallery was built, and the index card on the side of it was indexed by the office that sent the thing, because the point of the rack was to prove that the Chancery had received them, and nobody had ever indexed it by what the things *were*, and a model circular of all things is not a form, and a form is not a model, and so the whole accumulated paperwork of an empire that has been keeping records for three hundred years sat in a corridor and could be found by anybody who happened to want a form and by nobody who happened to want a sentence.

Tamsin Rook had been in the passage for three days.

She had told Marek on the sixth that she was going to "look at the circular a clerk would have to have" and had not said more about it, and he had thought about it for three days and had come down on the eighth with his coat on and found her sitting on the floor of a corridor with a stool, a lamp, a tray of index cards, and thirty-one printed things fanned out on the flagstones around her like a hand of cards.

"Have you eaten," he said.

"Have you read paragraph nine."

"Sarn's note first."

"Sarn's note is in your coat. Read it there and come and sit down, because I have got to the eighteenth of the tenth month and I am not going to be a person who is interrupted on a floor."

---

Sarn's note was four lines and it had a stamp on it and it said: two documents admitted as exhibits in the Venn matter on the fourth, on the clerk's authority, in the ordinary form; copies made and issued to the parties the same day; the magistrate not yet informed; and then, in Sarn's own hand, at the foot, underneath the stamp, because a clerk who writes under a stamp is still a clerk: *The copies are out. I cannot pull them and neither can anybody. I would like it noticed that I put them in before I told her, and I have thought about that for two days, and I have left a piece of paper on her bench about it, and it is the first thing I have done in nine years that I would have to think about in a morning.*

Marek read it twice.

"She put them in before she told the magistrate."

"She wrote to me on the second and asked me whether I would still be sending them on the fourth, and I said I would, and she said then I am going to do it on the day they arrive rather than wait until the start of the week, and she said it in a way that I understood and I did not argue with." Marek folded it. "And she has written to Rell. She says she has left a paper on the bench. She has told her, in writing, before the magistrate asks."

"That is not a woman taking a risk," said Tamsin. "That is a woman who is not frightened of a bench she works at. It is the single most reassuring thing that has happened in this building in two months and it is in a footnote on a clerk's note, and if I were a kind person I would put it in a book and I am not going to, because it is hers."

---

"So it is nine hundred and forty," Tamsin said, "and I have been sitting on a floor for three days getting to a number, and I am going to tell you the number first because you will want to say something about the number and I would rather you got it over with."

Marek looked at the sheet on the flagstones.

It was a single printed sheet, cream, good stock, folded once, and it had a heading on it in a plain lettered face and a series at the left and a number at the foot. It was headed:

*Model Endorsements for Continuity Filings (Flood Seasons).* **MC/FS.**
*Issued under the seal of the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities to all district courts and landing offices holding a flood docket.*

And beneath the heading there were two columns, and the left column held a model endorsement in the form that a filing takes when a flood-season continuity is accepted in a district, and the model was short, and the model had no semicolons in it, and the model had blanks in it where the district, the instrument, the ground and the person went.

> *being a work of the ground and not of a person, shall be held against [the ground named], the present holder answering for the same until full review, the named signer having ceased.*

"Twenty-nine lines the whole sheet," said Tamsin. "And twenty-two of them are instructions about how a filing is to be numbered and where it is to be entered, and four of them are the model, and the last three are a form of words for the ground at which the sentence bites, and they are the words from the twelfth of the ninth month, in the plain wording the magistrate used, given out as approved model wording, with a note under them in a smaller face that says where it is from."

The note under them read: *Adopted from a provincial court, the ninth month. A court may adopt this wording or any other.*

Marek got down onto the floor of the corridor.

He read the sheet from the top. He read it again. He read the last four lines, which were the form of words, and the third of the four, which was the sentence, and the small note printed under it, and then he read the four again.

"Dated the twenty-second of the ninth month," he said.

"The twenty-second of the ninth month. Ten days. The ruling was made at twenty past three on the twelfth. On the fifteenth somebody in that office had a copy, because by the fifteenth the copyist at a trestle table in a lane had made nine of them, and the press in Greyfen had made six, and a woman with a lamp had made four, and an old man with a tin had made three, and two men off a bank had made four, and that is all twenty-six of them, in twenty-six houses, with a professional press in Greyfen taking work off a trestle table in a river lane." Tamsin was not looking at him. "By the twenty-second somebody in a warm office had read one of them, and had decided that the court was right, and had set it in type, and had printed nine hundred and forty of them, and had put them in a sheet with a number on it and sent it to every district court and landing office in the empire that holds a flood docket."

"Nine hundred and forty."

"That is the run at the foot. I have checked it against the base circular in the same series, which is of the second month of this year and has a run of five hundred and sixty, and this sheet is an amendment to it and the amendment has the bigger run, which means the office decided in the ninth month that this was the urgent one." She put her finger on the foot of the sheet. "And there is a distribution line. *To all district courts and landing offices holding a flood docket. To be filed with the district's standing instructions and not with its papers.*"

"To be filed with the standing instructions and not with the papers," Marek repeated.

"So it is not in the file. It is in the instructions. It is in the drawer with the flood memoranda and the gate schedules and the inspection dates, and the man who takes it out of the drawer is a water-office clerk like Hallis Dren, and it goes to a district court and sits in a rack that is not a file, and it is unobjectionable, and there is no way in the world for a person in a lane to find out it exists."

---

They sat on the floor of a corridor for a long time.

"The First Seat form does not say any of this," Marek said at last. "That is what I keep turning over. The form is four paragraphs and not one word of the sentence is in it, and its fourth paragraph says in terms that it is not a direction as to the validity of anything and gives no authority and takes none away, and if you showed it to a magistrate they would say: yes, that form is administrative. And they would be right. So where does it fit. You have been looking at that rack for three days and the form was in your hand before the form was in mine, and you have not put it in the same sentence as the sentence, and that is either very good method or a stonewall."

"It is both," said Tamsin. "And I have got it out. Sit up, because I am going to say a thing and then I am going to be wrong about part of it in front of you, and I would rather you were looking at me than at the floor.

"The form is not a rule about this. That is the part I had wrong for two days and I want it written down that I had it wrong. The form is a quarterly *return*. It asks a district office how many flood-season continuity filings have been itemised individually, and it asks whether any such filing came in from a person who asked to be heard, and it has four boxes, and it goes to a pile four hundred miles away, and it does not say what the answers are. It does not tell anybody to write a zero. It does not forbid anybody to write a one.

"Which means every honest clerk in this empire can fill it in truthfully, and has, and the truthful answer is nearly always a very small number, because of the first paragraph of the form and not because of the return. And a small truthful number is worse than a big lie, Marek. A lie gets caught. Small is what a form like this *aims* at. It does not have to be dishonest to work. It only has to be dull."

Marek had his hand over his eyes, and this time it was not fatigue.

"And Dren answered it honestly," he said.

"Dren answered it honestly, in his own hand, a month ago, and his answer was one and yes, and it went four hundred miles in a sealed sleeve, and he does not know what he did and never will." Tamsin put the four paragraphs face down on the flagstone, which looked like a small surrender. "The circular is the words. The form is the counting. They are not the same office and they are not the same document and I have no evidence on this earth that anybody in either office knows the other exists. A clerk in Glasswater got the sentence in a filing in the tenth month, and the sentence was in the circular, and the circular is sealed by a standing office of this Chancery and goes to every district and landing office holding a flood docket, and the form says that a filing like that need not be looked at, and the form is sealed with the old fire-and-water device of a dragon seat. And there is no document anywhere in the empire that says the seat issued the form because the office printed the circular. There is a permission in one building and a permission in another and four hundred miles of river between them, and what joins them is not a conspiracy. It is a practice."

"The Sallow file," he said.

"That is the part I cannot finish," said Tamsin. "The Sallow summary was made in this building in the fourth month of the fifth year, in this court's own tray, and it carries the four lines with the blanks filled, and the base circular in this series is of the second month of this year and is newer than that file by four years, which means the wording was in the district before the wording was in print here, which is the same finding as the Venn one and it is the fourth time and it is the finding of this whole case.

"And I cannot date the series. I have been through the rack twice. The oldest thing in MC/FS in that rack is of the ninth year before last. Of the thirty-one sheets I have pulled, twenty-two carry those four lines, and the four lines are in all twenty-two, and the oldest of them is older than the ninth year before last, and the series was not issued in the ninth year before last, and the first issue is not in this building." She sat back against the wall. "So the wording came first and the printing came second and both of them are older than this court, and nobody in this building can put a name on either."

---

He wrote to Magistrate Rell on the eighth of the eleventh and he did not send it.

He wrote it in the alcove at the seventh hour with the sheet in front of him and Tamsin at the next frame, and it was four paragraphs and it was accurate and it was the only sensible letter anybody could have written, and it said that a sentence stated in open court in this district on the twelfth of the ninth month had been set in type as approved model wording in a printed sheet of another series, and had a run of nine hundred and forty, and had been distributed to every district court and landing office holding a flood docket, and that the magistrate was entitled to know that a sentence of her court was in print in nine hundred and forty copies and that four of them were in a lane in her own province.

He read it back. It was four paragraphs long and it was correct.

He put it in the envelope and addressed it, and then he put it in the inside of his own coat, and he did not say anything about doing it.

Tamsin Rook, at the next frame with a reed in her hand and her hand not moving, watched a man address an envelope and put it in his coat, and after a moment she said, "You are not going to send that."

"No."

"Because if Rell knows, then the district knows, and if the district knows then Examinations finds out that the sentence has been traced, and if Examinations finds out that it has been traced before you have asked for anything then somebody in Examinations decides what the answer to nine paragraphs is going to be before you have asked it." She did not look up from her frame. "And then you spend a fortnight waiting for a scope decision that has already been made, on a file that has been quietly reduced while you were writing a letter to a river magistrate."

"Yes."

"That is the right reason and it is the reason you gave yourself, and I want you to hear the other one read out, because you have not said it and I have." She put the reed down. "It is that you would rather send it after you have the certification, so that she finds out from you at the moment it helps you, and not four days before, in a letter, from a man who has not yet got anything, when there is nothing she can do with it except be angry on a Tuesday."

Marek did not answer that, and the silence went on long enough to be the answer.

"I am not going to argue with you about it," Tamsin said. "I have a thing of my own in a book with a time on it and I sent it up the river because I wanted it, and you have read that in a book, and I do not get to be the only one who is allowed to decide things. I am telling you that I have noticed, and that I noticed it in about two seconds, and that I am not going to use it against you later unless you use it against me, and I am going to go back to my frame now."

And then, at the end of the same hour, with the letter in his coat and the sheet still on the flagstones downstairs, Marek Kest wrote a supplementary request to the office of Examinations in his own hand, and dated it the eighth of the eleventh month, and it was two pages, and it did the one thing he had wanted to do for a month and a half.

It named the document. It gave the series, **MC/FS**, and the title, *Model Endorsements for Continuity Filings (Flood Seasons)*, and the issuing seal, the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities, and the base issue of the second month of this year, and the amendment sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month, and the run at the foot of each, five hundred and sixty and nine hundred and forty, and the distribution line, *to all district courts and landing offices holding a flood docket, to be filed with the district's standing instructions and not with its papers*.

It asked five things. That the Chancery produce every sheet of the series in its custody, from the earliest issue, with the names of the offices the series has gone to. That it produce the office's own record of how the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month came to be written, in a flood season, in ten days, out of a ruling read aloud in one lane. That it say in writing on what authority a sentence stated by one district court becomes approved model wording for all of them. That it preserve, pending that answer, every endorsement entered in the model form in every district since the sheet was issued, itemised or not. And that it name the person who signed the sheet.

The last of the five was the one he wanted and the one he did not expect. It was a single line, and he wrote it and then read it back twice and left it in, because a request that stops short at a series number is a request about stationery.

The request went to the Chancery intake desk at the eighth hour of the evening of the eighth, and the young clerk of about twenty-five read it, and found the seal, and then read the whole of it without hurrying, and stamped it, and stamped a second mark under it.

"Custody: Examinations," he said, and wrote a second number on Tamsin's copy in ink, and initialled it, and did not have to be asked this time.

---

Nevin Trost of Examinations was thirty-two and had been an examiner for six years and had a cold in his chest on the ninth of the eleventh, and he came into the Received Room on the second floor with his coat still on and asked, before he sat down, whether the room could be kept warm by somebody.

The Received Room had a barred door and a table and a stove and a shelf, and the stove had not been lit since the eighth month, and Marek lit it, and Trost sat down with his back to the wall in the way of a man who has been in a great many rooms with people who want something.

"I have three things," he said, "and I am going to do them in the order the file wants rather than the order I would, and the fourth thing I am going to leave to the end."

"Go on."

"Two documents from a Glasswater river court and a furnace town were placed on this file on the sixth of the month, in a fair hand with a true-copy tick in the margin, and they are not part of the request." Trost put a sheet of paper on the table. "They are exhibits in a district matter this office has never heard of, and they were admitted on a clerk's authority on the fourth, and the copies went out on the fourth, and we would like them back."

"They are the only copies."

"They are the only copies in the sense that the provincials have the originals, which is a thing I am aware of and which does not help this office, because the originals are four hundred miles away and this office has one file and it is on the third floor." He put his hand flat on the sheet. "I am not being difficult. I am telling you that I have a request with a number on it and a scope decision pending, and if I cannot say what the material in front of me is, then when the decision comes I will be writing a reply to a file that has two provinces stapled into it that the file does not mention. And a thing like that gets an application refused by a person who has never heard of either province and who is entirely within her rights."

Marek looked at the sheet. The sheet was a form. It had four boxes, like the other one, and one of the boxes had already been marked by a clerk in Examinations in a small firm hand, and the mark was the mark a person makes when they have been handed something they are going to have to explain later.

"If you send them back," he said, "what happens to them."

"What happens to them is that they go into an envelope with a reference on it and they go down to the applicant's file, and the applicant's file is in your flat, or in your chest, or wherever a roll-keeper of a provincial court keeps a case while she is in the capital, and they are no longer material before an office that is deciding a scope." Trost said it without any relish. "And I will tell you honestly that I would rather not send them back, and that I have been told to send them back, and that the person who told me to send them back is right on the law and wrong on the work, and I have a wife and I am not going to be the man who lost this over it."

"Then do not send them back."

"That is not in my gift."

"What is in your gift."

"That is," said Trost, "an extension. Thirty days, on the ground that the material has been added to it since the request was lodged and the office must not decide a scope on an incomplete file. Thirty days is the most I can do and I have done it twice this year."

Marek looked at him for a moment.

"Take it," he said. "And write the reason in the form rather than in a note, because a reason in the box is a document and a reason in a note is a thing a man said."

---

The second thing was the supplementary sheet, and it was one line, and it was Bryn Mawer.

"There is a supplementary application on the file," Trost said, "made on the eighteenth of the tenth month by the applicant, requesting that a person of seventeen employed in a copying room in this city, two streets off the Kiln road, on the seventeenth of the ninth month be examined in the copying. And there is a second one on the file dated the eighth of this month, in your own hand, about a printed series called MC/FS, which I will come to, because I have been told it is the only document in the last six weeks that anybody in this building has actually read." It has no bearing on the request. It has no bearing on the form series, or the circular, or the two districts, or anything in this office. I have read it three times because it is well written and it has kept coming up in my mind, which is not a reason to keep it."

"Leave it in."

"That would delay the scope decision by as long as it takes to deal with it."

"Leave it in," Marek said. "And I would like it to be recorded that I have been asked and that I have said leave it in, and I would like the girl to be told by this office that it has been left in, because she has got no way of knowing whether a piece of paper she gave to a constable in a room above a chandler's shop is a piece of paper or nothing."

Trost wrote something down, and then said, without looking up, "You know that is the first time in six years that a person has asked me to leave something in that has no bearing on anything, and it is the first time in six years that the thing left in has had a use. Every other one has been a delay with a good reason attached to it. That one is going to be read by somebody in about four years and it is going to be the reason a person looks at a plate in a counting house. I have a strange job, clerk Kest."

---

The third thing was the second sealed page.

"There is an exhibit in your file," Trost said, "described in the inventory as a second sealed page, folded behind the memorandum, with a thread in the fold, not opened. It went into the chain on or before the sixteenth of the ninth month, in front of a court runner and a roll-keeper, and it has never been produced. I am required to ask for it, because an office cannot decide a scope on a file with an unopened thing in it, and I am required to record the answer, and the answer is going in the form whichever way it goes."

Marek sat very still.

"No," he said.

"I have to record that it was asked for and refused, and I am going to record that, and I want to say to you before I do that I have read the inventory entry and I have read Tamsin Rook's note of the eighth of the tenth, which is a good note, and the note says the page was looked at and not opened by two people and a constable and a boy at a door, and that is better than most things in this file." Trost's pen was not moving. "And I am going to tell you what the refusal does. The refusal goes in the form. And when the scope decision comes, and a decision on a file with an unopened exhibit in it is a decision this office will be pushed hard on, the person refusing it is you and not the exhibit."

"I know what it does."

"Everything has a price and this one's is a line with your name against it, and I would rather you had chosen to pay it than have it taken off you by somebody in a warmer office." He wrote. *Second sealed page, described in the inventory, not produced. Applicant informed of the effect on the scope decision. Refused.*

---

And then the fourth thing, and Trost put his hand flat on the table twice before he said anything at all, and Marek understood afterwards, from the sheet, that the man had been handed this in a corridor an hour before and had been told to put it last.

"Can I say a thing that is not mine to say."

"Yes."

"It is not mine to say and I am saying it, and I would like that noted, and I have put a note on it in my own hand on the sheet behind this one and I am going to give that to you and not file it, because if I file it then it is a thing this office has offered and I am a man who offered it." He took a folded sheet out of his coat and put it on the table and did not let go of it. "Read that. It is not signed. It is what a person in the Chancery said to me in a corridor this morning in about ninety words, and he is not the head of this office, and if I have put it in front of you in a room with a stove in it then I have made a decision I am not sure I was entitled to make, and I have made it because you are going to find out what it says in about three days from somebody who will hand it to you with a face on."

Marek took the sheet and read it.

It said, in substance, that the Examinations office was prepared to certify, for a period to be stated and with a review date in the fourth month, that where a flood-season continuity filing is made in any district holding a flood docket, the practice to be followed is the practice stated by the Mosswake Roll Court on the twelfth of the ninth month, including the public-necessity sentence stated in open court on that day; that upon such certification the request lodged on the eighteenth of the ninth month, and its preservation and protection paragraphs, would be closed; and that the form series and the model circular would not be pursued.

"Any district," Marek said.

"Any district in the empire that holds a flood docket, and not the districts named in your request, which is Auremar only since you narrowed it a month ago, and I want you to sit with how little that narrowing is worth to you here."

He read it twice.

"Six months," Trost said, "is what is on the table. Four would be shorter and harder. Six months means this flood season is not decided in a hurry by a hundred district offices separately, and a hundred district offices separately is how a district clerk ends up with a sentence in his hand at nine in the morning with a widow in front of him and no time to find out where it came from. I have seen four districts in this job try to work out on their own what a flood-season filing means, and three of them got it wrong in a way that hurt somebody, and the fourth one is this one." He took his hand off the sheet. "And I am obliged to tell you that it is the reason the request is closed, and not a side of it. If the certification is made, the request is closed, the preservation demand over four offices falls away, the thirteen protections are not affected, and the form and the circular go back into a drawer in this building, where they have been since the ninth month, and where nobody comes who is not looking for a form."

"And if I do not sign it."

"Then in the fourth month, when the docket closes, forty-six district offices are still deciding individually whether a widow is a guarantor, and the sentence I am offering to certify is the only sentence anybody has got that has been through a hearing, and the choice becomes: a rule that is bad and tested, or no rule at all and forty-six clerks with their own opinions." Trost put his hands flat on the table. "And I am going to say the thing that makes me the man who is not supposed to say this, and then I am going to hand you the sheet and let you decide what to do with it. Your request of the eighteenth asks whether a filed and accepted continuation may be copied into a summary and re-accepted as a precedent in another household's file. This offer says yes, in flood seasons, with a date on it, and it is offered to you by a man who is tired and who believes it, and I have watched your face in the last minute and you have worked out what it is, and I am not going to pretend to a man of twenty-nine that you have not."

Marek Kest put the sheet down on the table, and did not push it back, and did not pick it up again.

He had been twenty-four when the notice went up in the third gallery about the examinations for certification, and he had put his name down, and he had not been put on the list, and there had been a line in the clerk's note beside his name in a small firm hand that said *no completed audit of record*, and he had read that line about forty times in a week and had worked out over about a year that it was true, and that the true answer to *no completed audit of record* was a file and not a manner, and that nobody hands you a file.

Six weeks ago he had written nine paragraphs by hand and a young man in a cold room had read them twice and put a number in ink on Tamsin's copy and initialled it, and the number was the first thing in nine years that had been a door and not a wall.

And here was the door, with a table laid on it, and the price of the table was a sentence a frightened magistrate had said in a hurry on the worst afternoon of her life, which had then been picked up by a warm office in a fortnight and set in type in nine hundred and forty copies, and which was now being offered back to him, by an office, as a rule, with a review date, for six months.

It was the same offer it had always been. It had been a certificate on the twenty-fourth year of his life and it was a rule about other people's children now, and it had been the first one every time, in a different coat.

"Six months," he said.

"Six months, and a date, and a certification with your name at the bottom of it, which is a name that means something in this building in a way it has never meant anything before."

"What happens to the form and the circular if I sign."

"Nothing. They stay in the drawer. They are in the drawer in a corridor behind the third gallery, and the corridor is not locked, and I have walked down it four times in six years and I never once looked at the rack, and I did not look at it because I am not an auditor and it is not a file." Trost stood up and did not put his coat on. "Clerk Kest. If you refuse, the sentence is still in nine hundred and forty copies and there is no longer anybody in the empire who is responsible for what it does. That is what an uncertified sentence is. It is a sentence with no one under it. And the people who are under it at present are forty-six clerks, and they are not bad men, and they will be deciding, in the fourth month, on their own, in nine hundred and forty copies' worth of districts, what a widow owes."

---

The fourth month.

That was the date, and it was the date the flood docket closed, and it was the date on the Dunn review, and it was the date on which the Venn term was five months from ending, and it was the date in Tamsin's letter from the lane about Odile Fenn and a canal seat and a girl who would be examined if anybody ever decided anything, and it was the date on Orra Dunn's register, and it was the date on the fifth term's arithmetic on a sheet nailed to a rail at a gate, and it was the date on Rennie Sallow's tally, and it was the date by which two pounds and five a quarter comes out of the one wage in a house at the end of a lane in Greyfen, and it was the date on a levy sheet with four houses' names on it and one woman's condition written on the back in her own words.

He had until the fourth month to answer.

Answering no meant that a house with a child in a surety line went to a factor, and a lane went without a rate relief, and forty-six clerks decided, and nobody in the empire was under the sentence.

Answering yes meant that the sentence became the rule, with a date, and his name on it, and a review in the fourth month, and the form and the circular left in a drawer in a corridor, and a seventeen-year-old girl from Candlewright Lane left in an office where nobody would read her line.

Two of those things was the thing he had wanted when he was twenty-four, and one of them was the thing he wanted now, and they were the same offer in two coats, and he had known that for about a minute and a half and he had not been able to look away from it.

"Mr Trost."

"Take it to the fourth month. You do not have to answer today and I would take it as discourtesy if you did."

"I am not going to say no today," Marek said. "I am going to tell you that I am not going to say yes today either, and I want that in the form as well as the extension, because in about nine days somebody in this building is going to hear that I have been offered a certification and is going to assume the worst about me, and I would like there to be a date and a sentence in a file that says I have not answered."

Trost wrote it, and then he gave him the sheet, the unsigned one, and made him promise — and made him say it out loud, which was a formality and was not a formality — that he would return it.

Then he stood up and pushed the sheet across the table, and said, "Take it with you. I am not the man who offers this and I am not the man who holds it, and I have been sitting here for an hour deciding whether to put it in front of you at all, and I have decided to, and I would like that on the record in a room with no witness in it, which is the only kind this one is going to get. For what it is worth, and it is worth very little because I am the wrong man to be saying it: I have a file in front of me with a girl's name in it and a form with a run of nine hundred and forty in another building, and the sentence in it was said by a good woman in the worst month of her life, and six months of a certified bad rule is better than four months of forty-six clerks, and I think you know that, and I think you have known it since the twelfth of the ninth month."

"Yes," said Marek. "I have known it since the twelfth of the ninth month."

"Then you are going to spend a fortnight trying to find a third thing," said Trost, "and there may not be one, and if there is not one then you will have to choose, and I would rather you chose in the fourth month with the arithmetic in front of you than in a fortnight in a panic."

He went out. The door was barred behind him and Marek Kest sat in the Received Room on the second floor of the Roll Hall with a stove he had lit himself and an unsigned offer on the table and a letter to a magistrate in his coat, and he did not answer, and the request was alive in Examinations with an extension of thirty days on it and a refused exhibit against it and a seventeen-year-old's line left in on purpose.
