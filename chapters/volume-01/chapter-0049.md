# Chapter 49: Nine Days in a Furnace Town, and the Fifth Question

The coach fare up the pass and back is eleven shillings, and a woman on the vats in Greyfen is paid four shillings a day and there are twenty-six days of work in a month, so eleven shillings is two days and a bit — and it is six months of what Nell Kest has ever been able to put by, because there is nothing in a dye-house to put by. She had it in a cloth in a drawer in Greyfen since the second week of the second month, and she had not told anybody she had it, and the reason she had not told anybody is the reason a person does not tell anybody about a sum like that, which is that a person who has decided in the second month to go to a furnace town in the seventh is not yet a person who can say that out loud in a room with her brother in it.

The first of the two pairs of boots came off on the twenty-second of the seventh month at the sixth hour in the morning and the second pair went on at the seventh, and the road over the pass is shut by snow for about five weeks a winter and is four days in the eighth month, and there is no other way of getting to Emberwatch that a woman with no money and no authority can afford.

She told the lane nothing. She told Marl Crenn's successor on the rota that she would be off, because a rota is a promise about an afternoon and somebody has to be there. She wrote two lines to the trestle and put them under the stone in the middle of the table, which is where a letter goes when it is a fact and not a question.

> *I am going to a town four hundred and forty miles off to find out what a person does who is owed an answer, which is what a woman in a kitchen asked us in the fourth month and nobody has found out yet. I will not be able to read anything anybody gives me. I am not bringing a paper in and I am not bringing the four questions, and if anybody asks me what they are I am going to say the lane, and if anybody asks me what I am I am going to say I am off the vats.*
>
> *The Caudles' quarter is due on the eighth and I will not have it before you do. N.K.*

And the second line was the whole cost of the month, and it was the first thing she had ever written on that table that was not about anybody else.

---

Bettin Rahl's room is over the weigh-house office on Sillrow and his hours are the second bell to the sixth, and a person cannot get in before the second bell and cannot get in after the sixth, and he has been the only person a house in that trade may write to for thirty-one years because the trade wrote down in the second leaf of its own book that a house in a trade of dyers may not write to a court, a guild, a factor's office, a district office, or a person it has not dealt with before about an instrument without the assessor's leave in writing first.

So Nell Kest stood on the weigh-house steps in a bad hat at the second bell on the twenty-sixth of the seventh month with nothing in her hands, and a man of about sixty came down with a book under his arm and did not open the door.

"You have come to the wrong building."

"I have come to the right building and I am not going to ask you to do anything, and I know that I am the fourth person to stand on these steps in four months and three of them wrote to a lane, which is my fault, and I have come about that."

"You have five minutes and then the weigh goes."

"Take all five," said Nell Kest. "I am not going to your door without a reason and the reason is a question and you have got five minutes, and if you say no at the end of it I will get back on the coach and I will not come again this year and I will not write, and I will say so in a book in a lane and it will be the only thing anybody ever knows about you, and I would rather you had it than not."

Bettin Rahl put the book down on the step.

"Who read this to you."

"Four questions," said Nell. "Four. Whose name is on it. If you stop, whose name answers. And who read it to you, and do they get paid if you sign."

"And I am paid to read instruments to houses, and I have been paid to read instruments to houses for thirty-one years, and there are about two thousand of us in this trade and there is one of me in four hundred and forty miles, and yes, they get paid, and no, I am not paid for the answer, and you may have the whole of that on a stone in your town in writing if you would like it, and I would rather you had it as a piece of paper than as a woman on a coach."

"I would like it as a woman on a coach," said Nell Kest, "and I am not going to be in your room for a minute, and I am going to tell you what I want and then I am going to stand on your step and be quiet."

---

She wanted to sit in Emberwatch for nine days and advise nobody.

That was the entire plan and it lasted nine days and it was not the plan. What she did was sit in the room over the weigh-house office from the second bell to the sixth, in a chair, and read out whatever was put in front of her, and be asked nothing, because a woman off a lane who is asked nothing is a reader and a woman off a lane who is asked something is a visitor, and the four questions were put there because a person who will not tell you a thing may be told it, and it is only a person who will be told a thing who is asked a question.

In nine days she read out, in a room in a furnace town, two hundred and some papers belonging to about forty houses, and eleven of those papers were in the model form with a box in them, and nine of the eleven had the box filled in with a house and two had it filled in with a person, and the two were in the same trade and had signed in the same week and had never spoken about it.

The two were a woman of thirty-eight and a woman of twenty-two who worked the same bench in a different house, and the twenty-two-year-old's name was in the box headed *the person who holds* on her own house's amendment, and the box on the other was the name of a house.

"I did not do it on purpose," the twenty-two-year-old said, on the fourth day, in the room, with her own amendment in her hand, and did not cry and did not lower her voice. "I asked. I asked the man who brought it and he said the box takes whoever is on the bench and that is the person, and I believed him, and I did not ask anybody whether a box takes a house."

"Who has it."

"Me. It is in my own box at the house and nobody has asked for it and I have not put a thumb over the line that says he is not in it, because there is no line that says he is not in it." She handed the paper over and did not let go of it for about a second. "I have thought about it every day since the second week of the second month and I have a bad chest and I have not been to a doctor about it, and I have wanted to say that out loud in a room with somebody in it, and there was no room, and there has been no room in this trade for thirty-one years, and the reason there has been no room is the rule on the second leaf, and the rule is a good rule."

Nell Kest read it out in the plain words, twice, and read the box last, and did not say whether it was right.

Then she wrote the two sentences she has written about every paper in eight months and which are the only two that have ever been any use, in a book of her own, in her own hand, in a furnace town, in a language four hundred and forty miles from any court: *I have read this out. I was not asked what it means. I am not saying whether it is right. The rule of this room is that a paper may be brought and read and no advice is given.*

The four women from the fourth house past the weigh-house came to see her on the second day, all four, and one of them asked her the fourth question about the amended exit clause and got the answer out of the assessor in about a second, because she already had the answer and had had it since the fourth month and had been carrying it around a kitchen with nowhere to put it.

Then the fourth thing happened, on the sixth day, and nobody in the room had asked for it and nobody had arranged it.

The house had written to a second trade in a third district, and the second trade had written back, and the second trade had asked a question that the first trade had not thought of asking, which was the fifth question, and the first trade had written the answer down and sent it, and by the time Nell Kest arrived in Emberwatch a thing that four women in a kitchen started in the fourth month was in four buildings in three districts, and there was a copy of the amended clause with four questions in a schoolroom hand pinned to a wall in a town nobody in Auremar has a letterhead for.

Nobody put it there. That is the whole of the finding and Nell Kest wrote it in her own book on the seventh of the eighth at about the eighth hour on a road with a bad harness and it is nine lines long.

> *The thing that travels is not a form and not a question and not a ruling. It is a person who turns up.*
>
> *A trade with one door, told by a woman with a bad hat and a carriage clock that the door is optional, will find out in about a year that it has four doors. That is the whole of the institutional change available to a trade of two thousand people. It will cost nothing. It will not be a rule and it will not be a form and it will leave no series and no run and no office and no record in any bay of any rack, and in nine years there will be a woman in a furnace town who cannot tell you which of the four doors was opened by her, and the answer will be that she does not know either, and the answer will be the truth, and it will be the only good thing anybody finds in the ninth month of next year.*

---

The fifth question is what a house does when the assessor says no.

Nobody in the whole of the eight months had asked it. Not the four questions, not the process, not the reading table, not the four in a kitchen in a furnace town who had sent the first letter a trade has ever sent to a court and had asked what a person does who is owed an answer. The fourth question is who read it to you and are they paid. The first three are about a paper. The fifth one is about the person who is standing in the room when the paper is wrong, and it has no answer, because the person in the room is a trade's own rule.

Bettin Rahl had been waiting for it for thirty-one years and could not answer it and said so in one sentence and then wrote it down.

"I have been the door since the year the rule was written," he said, "and I have never once been asked what a house does when I say no, and the answer is that it does nothing, and has done nothing since the second leaf, and that is why we have never had a dispute, and that is also why four women in my own trade have been sitting on four copies of a court ruling for nine months."

"Then I am not going to answer it either," said Nell Kest. "I have not got an answer. I have got a woman in a lane who is not you and I have got a boy off the Reed Cut bank, and between the two of them I could build you a second door in about a year, and it would be worse than yours, and here is why: yours is thirty-one years old and it has kept a trade whole and every house in it knows exactly where the door is. A door that opens both ways is a door that nobody can point at."

Bettin Rahl looked at her for about eleven seconds.

"You are telling me not to let a woman in a lane into my trade."

"I am telling you that you have been the only door for thirty-one years and you have done it in a room over a weigh-house office with your hours on a door, and I have watched a trade in another district this year where the only door is a woman on a ladder reading a pad, and it broke in five weeks, and I was the one on the ladder." She put her hands in her coat. "And I am going to tell you the other thing, which is that I would give four pounds of my own money for a second door and there is not four pounds, and there is not a year, and there is a levy on a lane in Greyfen that has gone against four houses in writing with reasons and the Caudles' share of it goes on the eighth of this month, and I am going to Emberwatch on a coach fare that is two days of work and six months of saving, because I put it in a cloth in the second month and did not tell anybody, and I would not be telling you that if I was not going to be a liar about the rest of it."

She left the step and Bettin Rahl called her back at about the sixth bell.

"What is your name."

"Nell Kest. I am on the board in a lane in Greyfen and I am off the vats and I am the one who gave the last of the four questions away, which makes me the last person in this trade you should ever let in, and I am telling you my name anyway because you are the only person in four hundred and forty miles who asked me."

He wrote it down on the wall of the weigh-house office, on the second leaf of the Combination's own book, in his own hand, at the foot of the rule that is thirty-one years old. Not the name of a house. A woman's name, and beside it the fifth question in full, and the answer, in his own words, which is that there is no answer, and that the reason there has been no dispute in forty years is that there is no answer and there has not been four hundred and forty women in four houses who wanted one.

The rule is still in force. It is still at the second leaf. It is still the only door a house in that trade may knock on, and it is still true, and now there are four lines under it, and the last of them is a question with no answer written by the man who has to live with it.

And the coach came back down the pass on the seventh of the eighth with a woman in it who was twenty and had been sitting at a vat since she was nine, and sixteen days away from a desk, and who had come home with nothing she could carry except a page of a book in a furnace town with a woman's name at the bottom of it and four lines under a rule that thirty-one years old is now the second rule in that trade, and the second one has no answer written under it, and the second one is the one people will read.
