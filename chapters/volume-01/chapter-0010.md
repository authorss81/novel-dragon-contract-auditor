# Chapter 10: The Form

By the time they came off the packet boat at Auremar the headache had gone from Marek's left eye into the base of his skull and stayed there, and his mouth tasted of a lamp that had been blown out days ago. It had been five days. He had slept nine hours across them. He had told himself each morning that the reading had been an accident of exhaustion and each afternoon that this was what an accident of exhaustion felt like from the inside.

He had stopped at the Mosswake landing on the way down, on the second day, because Perrin Ashe kept a room over a chandler at the river stair and charged a penny, and had put two fingers on the outside of his eyes and told him that he had read something and it had not let go of him, that this was not his fancy, and that if it happened again he was to come to a person before he came to a book. Eat, in the meantime, and do not read anything else.

He had not gone to a book. He had gone to a boat.

On the quay in Auremar a customs clerk asked him, cheerfully, in the way of a man who has asked the same question eleven thousand times, whether he was carrying any written matter relating to water, seed, inheritance, or the Reed Cut.

"Four documents," Marek said, "a roll-keeper's copy of a court order, a certified chain of custody, a page of a broker's ledger, and a form with a First Seat seal on it."

"All right," said the customs clerk, in a voice that meant all right, and went back to his book, and Marek stood on the quay in the wind and understood that the Empire was not concealing anything from him; it simply did not ask.

Then the customs clerk looked up again and said, "You're the clerk from the hearing. The one who read the thing."

"Yes."

"My wife's brother's boy takes the low field at Reed Cut. He says the holding's clear." He went back to his book. "That's all. Clear. I only wanted to say it out loud to somebody."

Marek said thank you, and got up the quay steps, and the thing that came off him in the street was not relief. It was the sense of a man who has just been told, kindly, by a stranger, that a decision he made has reached a person whose name he has never had and that the person thinks the thing is over.

He had told the woman in his own head: *nothing more in this file touches you.*

He opened his mouth to say it to the customs clerk, on the quay, for no reason at all, and nothing came, and he stood there for a second with his mouth open while a man with a barrow went round him.

That was the fifth day. It was not going to improve by itself.

---

The joint order named a room above a chandler's shop in Candlewright Lane, because a broker's office could not be entered by a constable and could not be inventoried by a provincial court without a room to put the inventory in, and the Auremar Chancery had a way of taking rooms above chandlers for exactly this purpose. The room had a long table, four chairs, a cold hearth, and a window that looked out at the backs of the Crown Kiln's sheds.

Ruhl Dunnet came at the ninth hour with the Mosswake order in a sealed sleeve and a spare pair of gloves in his coat, and he was a broad, unhurried man of fifty-two who had been a constable in the Chancery district for nineteen years and had the hands of a man who has handled a great many things he did not want to be responsible for.

"You'll be the clerk," he said.

"Marek Kest. That's the roll-keeper, Tamsin Rook. She's the copyist of the court record for the Mosswake matter, and the court has named her."

"The court has named her." He put the sleeve on the table. "I'll tell you what I can do, because you're going to want to know before we start. I have a joint order from the Mosswake Roll Court and countersigned by this district. It lets me enter, it lets me hold, and it lets me take a man out of a room. It does not let me take a shop, and it does not let me take paper out of a shop, and it does not let me decide that anything in there is evidence." He looked at Tamsin. "It does let me take a person, once the paper is safe."

"How much paper," Tamsin said.

"That's the question, and it isn't mine."

"Do you have any idea?"

"I've had one of his offices before. Four years back, a lease in Salt Teeth. Eleven boxes and a chest." Dunnet took his cap off. "So. Plan. We do not put a hand on anything until there is a copy of it, because if I put a hand on it and it turns out that half of it is privileged or a client's private paper, then I have breached a thing in a room with thirty people in it, and the thirty people are the reason I am still a constable. What I want from the two of you is a hand that never stops writing."

"How long," Marek said.

"Two days. If I can do it in two days, do it in two days. If I cannot do it in two days, do not do it in two days. Do it in nine." He put his cap back on. "I have a boy outside. If anybody tries to burn a page in there, my boy is not a hero and he will not go in after anybody. He will put the wet down. That is the whole of my heroism and I would rather say it now than have you find out."

---

Corvin Dray's office was on the ground floor of a stone house two streets off the Kiln road, and it was the best-looking room Marek had ever entered in his life.

That was the first surprise, and it was a large one. He had expected something like a counting house. Instead: a long table of good ash, a fire, a lamp, a window with a proper curtain, a bookcase with real bindings and a set of scales on a bracket, and eleven people waiting on benches along the wall, all of them holding paper, all of them dressed for a court.

There was a boy with a ledger at the near end and a girl with a copying frame at the far end, and the girl was doing fast, competent work with a document sleeve and a weight on it, and Marek watched her for a moment before he could stop himself, and thought: *she is good at this, she is better than the copyists in my gallery, and she is copying a thing she has copied a hundred times.*

Corvin Dray himself came out of the back room with two cups and gave one to Marek, which was so smooth and so entirely without hostility that Marek understood he had been assessed in the time it took to walk eight feet.

"Clerk Kest," he said. "Sit down. You look like a man who has been reading."

"You know who I am."

"I know your name, which is not the same as knowing you, and I know you are the reason a good afternoon got longer in my lane. Do have the tea. It is not a bribe, it is tea, and if it were a bribe I would want you to have seen me put it down."

Marek put the cup on the table un-drunk.

"Corvin Dray. I have a joint order. There is a copyist from a provincial court who is going to write down every sheet in this room, in order, with the hour, and I am going to ask you to let her."

Dray looked at the girl at the copying frame. He looked at the eleven people on the bench. He did not look angry, which Marek had been braced for.

"I will let her," Dray said. "I will also tell her, politely, that I have thirty-one years, and that a woman with a pen and a free afternoon is not an inventory, and that if this is done wrongly the damage will not fall on me. It falls on the eleven people on that bench. Two of them have a hearing on Thursday. One of them has a widow's house in it. Look at them."

Marek looked at the eleven people on the bench. Two of them were looking at him with an expression he recognized, because he had worn it himself, in a courtroom, when a broker's man had put a certificate in front of a widow before the widow could speak.

He had wanted the inventory. He had not thought about the eleven.

"Wait in the yard," he said. "All of you. With your paper. And if it takes two days you will be fed, and I will put that in writing and sign it, and I will put it in the minute book of the court that ordered this, so it is not a promise from a clerk but a line in a record."

A man of about sixty got up from the bench and said, "You'll be wanting the deed box as well."

"Which box is the deed box?"

"Green, under the window." He said it to Marek and not to Dray, which told Marek that this man had been in this room before and had opinions about its management. "He'll tell you the box is archive. It isn't. It's the working box and it's the one that gets read."

Corvin Dray smiled at the floor. "Brammel," he said. "You could have been an excellent lawyer and an appallingly bad witness."

"Been told," said Brammel, and went out into the yard.

---

Tamsin worked for two days and it was the hardest thing Marek had ever watched a person do.

She wrote in a fair hand and a separate book, and she never once wrote in the fair hand what she thought. She counted the room before the man — benches, window, curtain, the scales on the bracket, the fire, every book on the shelf by title and number, the copying frame, the ink, the water, the hour. Then the table, sheet by sheet, face up, face down, docketed, loose. Then the boxes, one at a time from the floor up, naming each one out loud before she touched it and having the boy at the door call the name into the yard, where the eleven people and the constable's boy and a knot of neighbours could hear it.

On the afternoon of the first day, at the fourth box, she stopped.

"Two sheets are not in this stack," she said.

"There are no two sheets missing from that stack."

"Corvin Dray, you have forty-one leaves in that box and the docket says forty-three."

Dray came and looked, and did not touch anything, and said, "Then the docket is wrong."

"You docket by your own count?"

"I docket by what the boy brings up."

"Then the boy brings up two sheets that go somewhere else." She wrote it down and read it back. "Box four, forty-one leaves against a docket of forty-three, discrepancy noted at the second hour, and the count was made in the presence of the proprietor."

Dray's face did not change, and he said, "You are going to be very good at this, and I want to say that it is a compliment, and I want to say that in three years you are going to be doing it for somebody who has bought this office from me and will be worse at it than me."

At the ninth hour of the second day, in the presence of the constable and the roll-keeper and eleven witnesses from the bench, Corvin Dray unlocked a drawer in the back room and produced a bound ledger of ninety-one leaves and put it on the table and said, "There. There it is. I have been waiting for you to ask for that and I have been dreading it for thirty-one years, and there it is, and I would like a copy of it made in this room before I am asked a single question about it, because I have not slept in two days and I would like to have one thing in my life be done properly."

Tamsin copied the ledger in the room, all ninety-one leaves, and it took until the second hour of the afternoon, and when she came out of it she had the face of somebody who has been handed something and knows it is heavier than the box it came in.

---

It was not a book of secrets. Marek had expected secrets. What it was, was a price list.

There were six columns and the headings were: *certificate*, *sequence*, *witness*, *entry*, *carry*, *sundry.* And beneath them, leaf after leaf, three hundred and six lines, each with a date, a district, a reference number, a fee, and a short description of the work.

*Certificate* meant a copied seal. There were sixty-one of them, and against each one a note of the device: a loop and a bar, a loop and a crossed bar, a loop and a triple bar. He read the loop and the crossbar and had to put his hand on the table.

*Sequence* meant something else, and it took him an hour to understand, and when he understood it he went out of the room and stood in the yard in the dark for a while.

*Sequence* meant the witness line. It meant the placement of a third mark in a summary, on a copy, or in a file, at a set height, for a set fee, performed by people who had worked out what a witness mark was for and sold the use of it. Sixty-one certificates and ninety-eight sequences. Twenty-two of the sequences were in the Reed Cut district. Six were in the Venn file. One was in the Dunn file and it was dated the fourth month, and it was the third mark on the summary, the exact one Tamsin had put under a lamp in a gallery eleven days ago with a mismatched edge and a wrong height.

And the two provincial entries, in the fourth column, in his own hand's summary of it, read *Glasswater, spring* and *Emberwatch, second* — districts Marek had never been to, districts four hundred miles off, with dates in the same year and the same six columns and the same fee, which was four shillings, which was not a great deal of money, and which was the price of a mark in a witness line.

"Four shillings," Marek said.

"That's the price," Dray said. He was sitting in the chair by the fire, unaccompanied, with a constable in the room and the door open. "Four shillings is the price. Not forty, not four hundred. It is four shillings because it is not worth doing properly, and it is not worth doing properly because the price of doing it properly is the whole of a season's audit and no clerk in a district office will ever be able to prove that anybody meant it." He turned his cup. "I did not invent the mark. I want that said in whatever room you end up saying it in. I did not invent the third witness. It has been on the water office forms since before I had this room. What I did was find out where a predictable error sits in a docket, and how long it takes to reach the summary, and then sell the reach."

"Then the flood memorandum."

"The flood memorandum is the most beautiful document in this empire and I have never written one." Dray said it without any particular relish. "A general paper with a date on it that a district clerk signs to clear a backlog, and then anything particular filed under it for the next twenty-four hours inherits the general paper's standing. That is a service. I did not build it. Somebody, in a warm office, ninety years ago, built the space, and I moved into it."

"And the substitution. The language. *Being a work of the ground and not of a person*, and *the present holder*."

"That is not mine either." And for the first time Dray's face did something, and it was not a lie, and it was also not an answer. "I sell a mark and a date. I do not write courts' sentences. I have four clients who write courts' sentences and I have never been able to find out who they are, because a client who wants a sentence does not give his name, and a man who does not give his name pays cash, and I have not refused a cash customer in my life because I did not like the weather."

Tamsin had not moved. She had her book closed on the table in front of her and her hand flat on it.

"Mr Dray," she said. "In box four you docketed forty-three leaves and there were forty-one. You told us the boy brings up the sheets. I would like you to look at the index to the ledger in front of you and tell me whether the index has any gaps in the fourth month of this year, in the eighth month, and in the second."

Dray looked at the index.

He did it for a long time, and Marek watched a man of thirty-one years' standing look at a book he had written, and understand that a copyist of twenty-four had done in two days what he had relied on nobody ever doing.

"There is a leaf in the second month," Dray said, "which is a receipt for a different matter, entered in a hand that is not the boy's."

"Then that leaf exists."

"That leaf may exist."

"It is in your office, in a box, under a docket, in a district where six sequences went in, in a room with a man in it who wants to sell me the Venn file back for nothing." Tamsin did not raise her voice. "I am going to write down that you told us a leaf may exist. And then I am going to write down that you produced ninety-one leaves on request, without a fight, and that you told us you had been dreading it for thirty-one years. Both of those go in the book, and I am not going to leave one of them out to be kind to you, and I am not going to put them in a different order to make it look worse. You will get both."

Dray looked at her for a moment, and then he said, "You are wasted on a district."

"Yes," said Tamsin Rook. "I know. It has been mentioned."

---

The chain came open at the fourth hour of the second day, in the room above the chandler's, with the constable standing at the door and the boy outside and the two provincial entries copied and the exhibit sleeve laid on the table.

There were two folded pages folded into the chain behind the Reed Cut memorandum, and both of them carried a seal, and the seal was the First Seat's: a disc of fired scale with the old fire-and-water device, pressed cold, with a number in a ring.

Marek read it twice.

It was a form. It was not a message and it was not an order to anybody in particular and it did not bear a name. It was headed *Continuity Filings, Flood Seasons, Categorisation* and it carried a form number and an issue series, and it instructed the recipient court that a continuity filing accepted under flood authority in the season in question should be *entered in the routine column and not itemised in the individual column, for the purpose of the quarterly return*, and that such filing should not be itemised, scheduled, or heard individually except on the quarterly review.

It was four paragraphs long and it was, in the most complete sense, the most powerful document in the room, because it did not do anything. It changed nothing and it created nothing. It only arranged that a particular class of correction would arrive in a district court already inside a rule, so that nobody would ever have to look at one of them again, and so that the reason a widow had lost her winter would be a line in a quarterly return in a building four hundred miles from the field.

"A form," Marek said. "With a series number and a run at the foot." He turned it over. "How many?"

"I don't know, and I have had a day and a night with nothing to do." Dray turned his cup. "Put money on it that it is not a form for one. A form printed once for one court is a form about one clerk's hurry. This isn't about a clerk's hurry. Somebody has printed this and put it in every court that files a flood docket in the empire, and I would have bet a house on it in a week."

The room was very quiet.

"Marek," said Tamsin.

He looked at her. She had her own book open at a page where she had drawn, in careful lines, the running stitch out of Ivo Harker's tin, and beside it a bar with a dot, and a bar with two, and the gap.

He said, out loud, in front of a constable and a criminal and a roll-keeper, the thing he had told her on the levee not to say.

"I am not going to put those two in the same sentence," he said, "and if either of you puts them in the same sentence I will have it struck from the record and I will say why. There is an old thread in a tin under an old man's bed, tied up before his mother was a woman, and there is a thread in the fold of a form that was printed in a year nobody in this room was born for. That is two things that are the same colour. It is not a finding. If I say it is a finding I will be telling this room what to conclude about a man I have never met, and that is the trick, and it is the trick I have been staring at for six days, and I am not going to do it in a room with a constable in it."

"Struck from the record," Dunnet said, writing. "Go on."

"And the other thing." Marek put two fingers on the form. "This is not a person. I have been waiting six days for a person. There is no person in this. There is a run, and a form, and a quarterly return, and somewhere a body that prints them. And the reason I cannot use this to help that family is that it points outward, and outward is a place where a clerk from a district gallery with a headache cannot go and be believed."

"Then file it," Tamsin said. "Point and all. File the form itself as an exhibit and let the thing that points outward be somebody else's problem for a month."

"No," Marek said. "I am not going to hand it to a lawyer. I am going to file a request that names it, in writing, and asks the Chancery to preserve every source file that has been itemised or not itemised under a form of that series, in every district, for twenty years. And I am going to ask them to say no, or yes, in writing, with a name on it, because a man in a warm room who has never heard of me will say no to a request in a day and will take four months to say no to a request that has a form number on it."

---

The arrest happened at the sixth hour, and Dunnet did it in one sentence and did it well.

"Corvin Dray, I hold a joint order of the Mosswake Roll Court and the Chancery district of Auremar, made on the seventeenth day of this month on the matter of the Venn and Dunn files, and I take you now to the district house. You may have your coat. You will be searched. Your paper stays here and it is copied and it is sealed and a copy is left with your people at the addresses on the bench outside."

Dray stood up. He did not argue. He buttoned his coat, and it was a good coat, and Marek found that he minded that he minded.

"Gentlemen," Dray said, "and the copyist, who I think is going to be a problem for somebody. One thing, freely given, and I want it in the book exactly as spoken."

"Go on."

"I sold a mark and a date. I have never written a sentence and I have never known who writes them, and there are four of them, and I have wanted to give you one name for thirty-one years and I have not got one, and I would rather say that to a court than have it found later and said I hid it." He looked at Marek. "I will say it. I will say all of it. Not for a reduced plea, I am not a fool. I will say it because in about four years somebody is going to be able to prove I hid it, and I would rather be the one who said it."

"After counsel," Dunnet said.

"After counsel," Dray agreed. "In a cell, with a magistrate, with a clerk, and with the roll-keeper present, because she will write it down and it will be the same whichever room I am in." He looked at Tamsin. "You will be there?"

"If the court allows it and if I am assigned," Tamsin said, "I will be there."

Dray went out between the constable and the door. He went out the way he had come in, at a walking pace, and the boy at the door said the hour out loud into the yard as he passed, because Tamsin had told him to, and eleven people on a bench watched a man walk out of a room he had built over thirty-one years and did not say a word.

---

The hearing room had one chair in it that was not for the court. Marek asked for it to be emptied, and the constable emptied it, and Marek sat down in it and put the form on the table in front of him and wrote.

The request was nine paragraphs, and it was nine because nine was the number it took to say everything he wanted and nothing he did not want. He wrote it by hand, because the Chancery's practice was that a formal request to audit a file was made by hand and signed, and a hand-signed request could be refused by a person, and a typed one could only be refused by a rule that had to be written down.

The first two were the file, by name and by number: the Venn packet, the source leaf and the witness copy, the correction of the eighth month, the water office's memorandum, the ruling of the twelfth, the exhibit as sealed. Then the copyist who had made the comparison, and the receipt number of the Dunn summary, and the date on the register, because a request that cannot be answered by a person cannot be answered at all.

The third was the ledger. Ninety-one leaves, six columns, three hundred and six lines, and the two provincial entries.

He put the reed down at that point, and asked Tamsin the thing he had been putting off asking since the yard in Candlewright Lane.

"If I name them, it is an accusation against two districts I have never stood in, on the word of a price list a man wrote for himself and got copied by a woman who is now sitting in a cell. If I do not name them, this is a request about one lane and one family, and it is answered in six weeks."

"You did not have that ledger copied to be careful with it," Tamsin said.

"No."

"Then name them, and name the hand you copied it from. You have spent six days telling a lane that this is not one household. Do not make one concession for four hundred miles."

"They will be the first two things somebody in Examinations throws out."

"They may be. And if they are, they are the first two things any court has ever had to look at, and the throwing out will be in writing with a name at the bottom of it, which is the whole of what you have taught yourself to ask for this month." She was not looking at him. "And if you leave them out, then the two women in those files are the two women you did not name, and you will spend the rest of your life being a man who told a widow on a step that nothing further in this file would touch her."

He picked the reed up.

So the third paragraph asked the Chancery to inquire into the sale of copied witness marks as a practice and not only as an offence, and named the two provincial entries in the fourth column as *Glasswater, spring* and *Emberwatch, second*, and named the hand they had been copied in, which was Tamsin Rook's, with the date, the room, and the eleven people on the bench who had watched her do it.

Then the form, in full: *Continuity Filings, Flood Seasons, Categorisation*, series as on the seal, print run as at the foot, and five questions about it that all wanted the same answer, which was what it was, who issued it, to whom, on what authority, and since when. Then preservation, in the plainest language he could find, of every source file in every district that had filed a continuity correction under that series or a similar one, for twenty years back, pending a decision on scope. Then four offices by name, with a request that no source leaf in any of them be returned, re-filed, corrected, or re-categorised until the Chancery had answered. Then the eleven people on the bench at Candlewright Lane and the two who had agreed to give evidence, and a definition of protection, because the bare word would not carry: no seizure of their papers, no attendance at a broker's office without a court officer, and no exclusion from a hearing.

The eighth paragraph was the one he had most trouble with and the one he wrote twice, and it was the paragraph that said what he was not asking for. He did not ask for the Venn file to be reopened; it had been heard. He did not ask for the correction to be declared void; the court had dealt with it. He asked whether the same four words were in other files, and who else was selling the mark, and he put in that paragraph, in terms, that nothing in the request asked any court to turn a family out of a house before the fourth month.

The ninth was the request proper, and it was the only paragraph he read aloud twice: that the Chancery examine whether a filed and accepted continuation may be copied into a summary and re-accepted as a precedent in another household's file, and if so by what authority, and if not, by what process it is to be stopped, and if the stopping is beyond the Chancery's own power, then to say so in writing, and to say whose power it is.

He signed it, and dated it the eighteenth, and did not write *yours obediently* or anything like it, because he was not asking a favour.

The Chancery intake clerk was a young man of about twenty-five who read the first paragraph and then found the seal, and after that read it all the way through without hurrying, twice, and Marek watched him do it and understood that this was the whole of the protection on offer: a young person in a cold room who reads carefully.

"Clerk Kest," he said. "This is a request for a formal audit of a file and of a form series, with a preservation demand attached to four offices and a protection demand attached to thirteen named people."

"Yes."

"It does not go to the Inheritance Desk."

"No."

"It does not go to your supervisor."

"No."

The young man turned the request over and stamped it, and the stamp was *Received*, and a second stamp, in a different ink, said *Custody: Examinations*.

"Examinations is a higher office than the Table," he said. "It is not the top. It is where things go when nobody wants to be the one who has them. I am going to file it in Examinations, and Examinations will decide in three months, or six, or a year, whether its scope includes the form series and the other districts, and they will do that without telling you, because you are not the office."

"Will you tell me it has arrived?"

"I have stamped it. That is the arrival."

"Will you write the number on my copy?"

The young man wrote it on Tamsin's copy of the request, in ink, in the middle of the last blank page, and then had to be asked whether he would initial it, and did, and then looked up with the expression of a man who has just done something irregular and is not yet sure it was wrong.

"That is my number," he said. "If you come to intake and ask for the file by that number, they cannot tell you it does not exist without writing it down."

---

They came out of the Chancery at seven in the evening into a city that had gone on being a city.

Marek had a stamped request in his hand and a copied ledger in a case under Tamsin's arm and a head that had been going for six days, and the ache had settled low and stayed, and there was a taste behind his teeth that no amount of water had touched.

"Examinations," Tamsin said, walking. "Not the top. Where things go when nobody wants to be the one who has them."

"Three months. Or six. Or a year."

"Possibly the whole thing, and the four offices never told, and the form never named."

"Possibly." He stopped walking at the corner of Candlewright Lane, where the chandler's window was still lit, and where the eleven people had gone home to eleven separate kitchens with paper of their own that would still be their own in the morning. "Then we do the slow part. Which is: everybody who is holding a piece of this goes home tonight and tells somebody, and the somebody is in a different lane, and there is no form for that, and it is slower than a quarterly return and it is the only thing that has ever actually worked."

Tamsin shifted the case.

"I need to send four copies to Mosswake tonight," she said. "One to Sarn for the sleeve, one to the widow, one for my own book, and one to that woman in the seed barn who will not give her name, because a person who will not give her name is a person who intends to be hit, and a copy in her apron is the only weapon she has ever had."

"Do it. And then sleep."

"Sleep," Tamsin agreed, "and tomorrow you will write to Nell, and you will tell her what the request says and what it asks for and what it will cost, before I do, and you will tell her the same thing you told me, which is the whole of it and not the version that suits you."

"I will."

They got as far as the quay. The customs clerk was still on the steps, packing his book away for the night, and he recognized Marek and lifted a hand.

"Clerk." He waited until Marek was standing beside him. "One thing and then I'll let you go. The thing I said on the quay yesterday. The low field. He says the holding's clear." The man closed his book. "That's all. I'm not asking you for anything. People in my trade ask for things. I'm not asking."

Marek stood on the quay steps in the wind with the stamped request in his hand and the ash taste at the back of his throat.

He wanted to say: *there is more in this file that will touch your wife's brother's boy, and one day somebody in a hurry may ask him to sign a paper that makes him answer for a dead man's work, and I cannot stop that from this building.*

He wanted to say: *I have a form with a run number on it that points four hundred miles from here, and I have been told by one person, twice, and in a levee in the dark, that I am not to put two facts in the same sentence, and I have obeyed, and obeying is going to be the thing that costs me most in this.*

He wanted to say: *I read a page on the twelfth and it has not let go of me since, and there are three lines in a book in a copyist's hand that I could not have written, and one of them says whoever stands in that field after everyone I love is dead, and I have not told my sister any of that.*

What he said, because it was the only one of the four that was true in the part he was permitted to say, was:

"I'm glad it's clear. I want it to stay clear. And if anybody ever brings him a paper with a duty on it, he should make them read it to him out loud before he signs, and he should be allowed to say no."

The customs clerk looked at him for a moment.

"That's more than I've had off the court in eleven years," he said, and went down to his shed.

Marek stood on the steps a while longer. Somewhere behind him, four floors up in a building he had never been inside, ninety-one leaves of a broker's book and ninety-one leaves of a copyist's were being sealed into a sleeve in a room above a chandler's shop, and somewhere behind that, on a file in an office called Examinations that he had no right to enter, nine paragraphs of his own handwriting were lying in a drawer with a number written on them by a young man who had been careful, and a form with a run on it was going to keep being printed, and he had not found anybody.

He had found a mark sold for four shillings. He had found a warehouse of paper that did not know whose name was on the paper. And he had a woman on the quay, a stranger, who would go home and tell a person on a low field at Reed Cut to make a broker read a paper out loud.

That was the whole of the eighteenth. It was not a victory. It was three hundred and six lines of a price list, one arrest, one stamped request, and a sentence in a widow's coat pocket that had been a letter to about sixty households and had already found the first person it could be done to.

Marek went to find his tea, and could not taste the tea, and the headache started again somewhere behind his left eye about a quarter of an hour after the sun went down, and it stayed.
