# Chapter 1: The Summary

The third gallery of the Roll Hall had one window facing west and eleven desks arranged around it as though the afternoon light were a wage worth dividing fairly. By the sixth hour the Crown Kiln's chimney had taken the light away, and the copyists were trimming their lampwicks early, the way people do when they have decided to be seen working.

Marek Kest had nine packets and one courier.

The courier was broad-backed and worked the eastern districts and the river road, and she would take whatever the last clerk handed her without once looking at it. That was the point of her. The Roll Court at Mosswake did not want a courier's opinion. It wanted a packet.

He worked in the order the papers were written rather than the order they were filed, which is not a thing the Chancery teaches. He did it without deciding to. If a page is filed in the ninth month and written in the third, those are two facts about it, and only one of them is the clerk's business. The distance between them is where the work actually lives.

The Venn packet was the ninth.

He laid it out in front of him in the order he wanted rather than the order it came. A packet is four things and a clerk's summary that stands in for all four. The source leaf was not there. The source leaf was never there; the source leaf sat in the locked cabinet along the north wall, in a climate the Chancery paid for, and it came out only for a hearing, a claim of fraud, or a written authorization from a supervisor. What arrived on a junior clerk's desk was the witness copy, any filed corrections, the filing docket, and the summary.

The witness copy for the Venn file was three sheets of good flax paper, copied in a neat chancery hand, dated four years back. The docket was a strip of thin stuff with three entries on it in three different hands. The first correction was a single leaf, sewn on with red thread, and the red thread was the correct color, which meant somebody had wanted it to look official.

The summary was four lines long.

Marek read the summary first because that was what the summary was for.

> *Aldren Venn, of the lower field at Reed Cut, entered a seven-year water-and-field instrument with the water keeper of that district, in the fourth year of which he is deceased. The field-side upkeep and the winter-seed repayment named to him are recorded against the field and continue, the present holder being bound to the same until full review. Use of the channel and the lower field permitted to the household for the term. Certified to Mosswake Roll Court, this docket.*

It was good work. Whoever had compressed it had understood the instrument, or believed they had. He found the phrase *the present holder being bound to the same* and turned it over in his mind the way you turn over a coin you have been given too many of.

The original, as far as he knew, named Aldren Venn.

He went to look at the witness marks, because the witness marks were cheap and because he liked them. A witness mark in this province is a small pressed notch in the paper's edge, made by a thumbnail or a wax pin, usually two, sometimes three. Two meant two people had heard. Three meant a third had been called in, usually a guild assessor or an irrigation witness, and the third notch was always cut at a different angle because the third person was standing differently.

The witness copy carried two notches, low on the left margin, both of them old and both of them slightly ragged, the way paper goes when it has been handled in a wet season for years.

The summary carried three.

Marek sat with that for a moment. The third notch on the summary was shallow and even and its edges were clean, the way an edge is when a mark has been copied rather than made. Somebody had taken an impression of a third notch that had never existed on the witness copy and put it on a clerk's summary, and had done it well enough that the summary would pass down the hall without anybody stopping.

He put down the reed he had been sharpening and did not pick it up again.

"You've gone quiet," said Tamsin Rook from the alcove behind him.

She worked the copying alcove, which was not a promotion and paid less, and she had the particular calm of a person who could copy a page in ninety seconds and still know who had been standing in the room when it was written. She had been assigned to his evidence three weeks ago, which meant that in the Chancery's peculiar phrasing she had been assigned to his failures.

"I'm reading," he said.

"You're staring."

"Both."

She came and stood at his shoulder with her arms folded, which she did instead of leaning, and he understood the difference by now. Leaning is a friend. Folding arms is a colleague reserving her opinion.

"Third notch," he said. "On the summary."

She looked. It took her perhaps four seconds, which was slow for her.

"It's copied," she said.

"Yes."

"Copied from what?"

"That's the question."

She reached out and turned the summary a quarter turn toward the lamp without touching it, the way people examine things they intend to argue about. "The other two are ragged. This one is crisp. It was made from a good impression, not from a pressure in the original paper. And it's a hand's width below where a third notch would go if the witness had been standing in the line with the other two."

"Which is?"

"Which means whoever copied it was looking at a different page. Or a different edge. Or a different file entirely."

Marek turned the docket strip toward himself. Three entries, three hands. The oldest was the original entry, four years back. The second was a notation in the Venn family's own hand, a note that the keeper's schedule had been amended, with no date to speak of. The third, in a chancery hand he had seen ten thousand times, read: *Correction entered on the docket under flood authority, eighth month. Referred to the district water office.*

There was no attached authority. The page it should have been attached to was not in the packet.

"Pell," he said.

Pell Vey was coming down the aisle before he said the name, which suggested Pell had been coming to look at the docket for a while and had chosen the moment. Pell moved through the third gallery the way a good clerk moves through a gallery, without hurry, stopping at desks long enough to make each clerk feel looked at, and never long enough to be accused of it. He was fifty and greying at the sideburns and had been at the Notaries' Table for nineteen years, and he had trained Marek, and he had a way of asking a question that made it sound like he had already reached an answer and was waiting to see whether you would keep up.

"Evening docket," Pell said. "Nine packets."

"Eight and a half."

"Marek."

"It's the Venn. There's a problem with the witness marks."

"Which witness marks."

"The summary's. It carries three. The witness copy carries two."

Pell took the summary, looked at it, and gave it back. His face did not change. That was the thing about Pell Vey: his face was not a wall, it was a door, and it was always slightly ajar, and you could never tell from the gap whether the room behind it held a coat or a body.

"Third notch," he said. "Filing clerks add a third notch when a summary follows a correction that came in with an authority behind it. It's a marker so the next clerk knows the summary isn't working from the original leaf alone."

"That's not in the handbook."

"It's in the practice." Pell glanced along the aisle, at the last row of desks, where two copyists were pretending not to listen and failing. "Marek. Flood season. The Mosswake court has a flood docket the width of my table, and every packet I hold back to satisfy a junior clerk's curiosity is a packet that reaches that court in the sixth week instead of the first. The Venn summary is clean. The terms are legible. Certify it and let the provincial court do the work of reading a widow's circumstances, which they are far better placed to do than either of us."

"Certify a summary with a copied witness mark on it."

"Certify the summary of a packet whose correction was filed under a flood authority I can see from here. If you believe the mark is a forgery, that is a different matter and a different form. If you believe the clerk who made the summary was tired, that is a different matter and no form at all."

"I believe the mark is a copy."

"Then be sure of it before you spend your winter on it."

He had gone. He did that too: answered, and left, so that the argument had to be finished alone by whoever was left standing in it.

Marek sat for a while. Then he took a fresh sheet of copying stuff from the drawer, and set the summary face down beside it, and did the thing he had been trained to do and had never been praised for, which was to make a second record before making any decision about the first.

"Tamsin."

"I know." She was already unfolding the soft graphite pad she kept in her apron for exactly this. "How close?"

"Not close. Before the packet closes. Before the courier. I want a comparison copy of the summary against the witness copy, page for page, in your hand, and I want it in your keeping, not in the desk drawer."

"You want me to keep it."

"I want someone to keep it who does not think I am right."

She considered this. The lamps made small adjustments to the light as the copyists trimmed them.

"Say the second part again," Tamsin said.

"I want someone to keep it who will still be keeping it if I am wrong."

"Right." She took the soft pad and the two pages. "Then give me the witness copy first and do not touch the correction while I work, because if you move a thing while I am comparing it, I have to start over, and I will be annoyed in a way you will hear about for a week."

He gave her the witness copy and then took up the correction leaf, and read it, because there was one more thing in it he had not looked at properly.

The correction was a paragraph. A correction is a small animal. It is supposed to do one job: change a term, add a term, or fix an error of arithmetic, and it is supposed to say what it is doing, and it is supposed to arrive with the authority that allows it to arrive. This one did none of those three things. It arrived with no authority attached, and what it said was this.

> *That the field-side upkeep and the winter-seed repayment named in the said instrument, being a work of the ground and not of a person, shall be held against the field, and the present holder of the field shall answer for the same until full review, the named signer having ceased.*

Being a work of the ground and not of a person. He read it twice. Whoever wrote that had understood that Aldren Venn was dead and that a dead man's name does not sue anybody, and had solved the problem in the way that a filing room solves problems: by moving the debt off the person and onto the thing, and then off the thing and onto whoever is standing in the field at the time.

The named signer having ceased. Four words that turn a widow and a daughter into a category.

And the phrase, sitting in the middle of it like a nail head: *the present holder*.

Marek put the correction down and looked at the four-line summary beside it, and understood, with a clarity that arrived not as excitement but as a cold place at the back of his stomach, what a summary was for.

A summary was what the next file copied from.

The witness copy was evidence. The source leaf was evidence. The summary was the door. Every later packet that came in over this one — a district correction, a seed-barn lease, a guild term, whatever a clerk needed to make fit in four lines — would not read Aldren Venn's instrument. It would read four lines written by somebody who had been in a hurry, on a page with a third notch on it that nobody had ever pressed into the original.

A whole district could be copied out of that, and the district would think it was being reasonable.

He looked up. Tamsin had the comparison half done and was not looking at him. Outside, over the Kiln's shoulder, the last of the light was coming off the river in a long dull sheet, and the courier was crossing the yard toward the eastern door with her satchel open.

"Send it," Marek said.

Tamsin's pencil did not stop. "Send what? I've not written a line."

"Send the packet without the summary. Send the witness copy, the docket, and the correction. And put a slip in saying the summary is withheld on a question of witness marks and will follow under separate cover."

"That is not a thing I'm allowed to do on my own."

"No. So write the slip and I'll sign it, and take it to Pell, and he will either countersign it or say no, and either way it will be a thing on a record instead of a thing I thought of." He was already standing. "The second he countersigns, we take a boat."

"You have no authority to send a clerk to Mosswake."

"I have a packet with a copied witness mark and a correction with no authority behind it, and there's a widow at the end of it." He pulled his satchel closed, and the flat weight inside it — his own file, his mother's name gone out of the family record, the claim nobody would let him touch — shifted against his hip and said nothing, the way it always did. "I have until the courier crosses the yard. After that the summary goes to Mosswake in front of a magistrate and a woman hears it read to her, and it will be too late for the answer to be worth anything to her."

Tamsin looked up from her pad for two full seconds, which was the most time she had ever given one of his decisions before deciding against it.

"All right," she said. "But I am copying the summary in full as well, and I am putting my name on the copy, and if this turns out to be a tired clerk then you will owe me for a week of evenings and I will collect in tea."

"Collect in tea."

"I know what you drink. I have been in this alcove for three weeks."

He went to find Pell, and the door at the end of the aisle was cold under his hand, and the courier was already in the yard with the satchel open and the light going.
