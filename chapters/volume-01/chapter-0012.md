# Chapter 12: Until Full Review

The river courtroom on the twenty-third was the Roll Court with its fixed benches carried out from the courthouse and roped into the shape of a hearing room, which meant that the front row was where the court sat and the second row was where the public sat and there was no arrangement at all between the two, so that anybody who came to the second row could see the backs of the magistrate's shoulders and anybody in the first row had to turn round to look at them.

Marek got there at the ninth hour and the room was already filling, and he understood in about four seconds that the reason Orra Dunn had been afraid was that the court had not been built to hide anything and had never once pretended to be.

They were in the second row. He counted them. A man of about fifty in a factor's coat with a stick across his knees. A miller with flour still in the creases of his wrists and a boy with a satchel. A woman of about sixty in good black who did not move her hands at all. And a clerk of some kind of fund with a portfolio on his knees and a pen already out, which meant he had come expecting to write something down.

Nine other people. Sarn at her table with the minute book open. Wend the lamp-trimmer going up and down the aisle with a stick, checking the lamps, working, because lamps need trimming and it was still the ninth hour. Pella Osk at the copying table with her sleeves rolled, awake, which Marek noted and did not think about.

And Orra Dunn, with Nessa beside her in the shawl, in a season that did not need a shawl, with her hands in her lap and her feet flat on the floor.

Marek went down the aisle and stopped in the front, which is what an applicant's man does, and Orra said, without looking up:

"He was here on the eighth of the month and he told me I did not understand it and now he is here again."

"I did understand it," Marek said. "I understood it and I could not stop it, and I have said so to three people this week and I am saying it to you, and it is not going to get better this morning."

"Then sit down," she said. "There is a woman in the second row who has been writing since we came in."

The woman in good black was the miller's creditor or the miller's mother or something worse. Marek had a piece of paper from the eleventh that listed who had written to the Mosswake court about the Dunn barn since the twelfth, and it had four names on it, and three of the four were in the room, and the fourth was a factor out of Greyfen named Osric Lave, who was not in the room and had told a seed factor in Glasswater that the barn was coming.

---

Rell came in at the eleventh hour and the room stood up and sat down again in the way of a hundred hearings.

"This is a separate sitting in the Dunn matter," she said, "and I am going to say what it is before anybody stands up, because I have sat in this court for nineteen years and the words *separate sitting* have been used in this province to mean four different things and I am not one of them.

"It is not a second hearing. It is not an appeal. It is not a review. It is a sitting at which the court must decide one thing, which is whether a guarantor duty against the household of Orra Dunn may attach before the full review of the sentence this court stated on the twelfth. That is all. Everything else in the matter is not before me today and I will not hear it, and if anybody tries to hand me the whole file I am going to hand it straight back.

"Clerk Kest has asked for the sitting to be taken in a particular order. I have allowed the order. The household will speak first, without interruption. Then the registrar will read the summary that was filed. Then the parties who intend to ask questions. Then the applicant. Then me. If I am going to refuse something I would rather do it at the end of a morning in which everybody has said their piece, and I would rather it be on the record that everybody said their piece first."

She sat down.

"Orra Dunn."

Orra stood up and did not have anything in her hands, which Marek had expected, and which turned out to be the thing that undid him, because he had come braced for a woman with a board.

"Magistrate, I have got four people to."

"You have four creditors."

"I have got four creditors and a floor and a daughter and I have said out loud in this room and in my own doorway to four different people in the last ten days that I did not want to be named anywhere. I am going to unsay it and I am not going to be able to tell you why, and I have been thinking about why for three nights and the best I have got is that a clerk in the second row has been writing since we came in and I would like there to be one piece of paper in this matter that somebody has written the true thing on, and that it is not in his book."

Rell looked at the second row for a moment and then back.

"Go on."

"My daughter signed nothing. I know the court knows that. I have come to be told something and what I have come to be told is that the answer I want is not available, so I am going to ask for the next thing and I am going to be honest that I am asking for it because I have no choice and not because the court owes it to us.

"Here is what I lose. If it attaches, the barn goes, and I have got seed in it that was paid for, and the factor has been told it is coming, and it will be sold at less than it is worth because a thing that is sold under a duty is not sold, it is disposed of. And my daughter goes to her aunt at Salt Teeth, who has three and cannot feed four, and I go to the district works for the winter at a wage that is not ours and that you have all got in front of you in the book.

"Here is what I lose if it does not attach. Nothing. I keep my floor and I keep my daughter and I am a woman who came to a court and asked to be named and was thanked for it in front of a room. I have thought about that and I would still rather have the floor.

"Here is what I will refuse." She stopped, and Marek thought she was going to lose it, and she did not. "I will not sign anything in this room. Not the summary, not a continuation, not a guarantor line, not a new obligation for the bank works. Nobody has ever come to this barn and asked me for a mark. I have heard what happened to the woman at Reed Cut, the one whose husband died and whose field they nearly took, and I have thought about it every night since the twelfth, and I have sat here three nights waiting for the man to come up the lane, and he has not come, and now a piece of paper says I owe it anyway."

Nobody said anything. In the second row, the woman in black put her pen down.

"Nessa Dunn."

The girl got up the way a person gets up who has been told to do it in advance. She did not have anything in her hands either, and Marek found himself wishing she did.

"I am fifteen," she said. "I have signed three things in my life. I signed a school paper, I signed for a hoe, and I signed the seed-share paper at the barn, which is a paper about forty quarters of winter rye and it is mine and it is not a debt and nobody has ever tried to make it one except a man in a good coat at the gate who told me I was going to owe something before I was here.

"Ma says you have to say what you want."

"I said what I want," Nessa said. "I want to be examined for the seed trade. I have been sorting since I was nine. I know the damp in the middle bin better than the man who teaches it. I have never been examined and I have never been asked, and everybody keeps saying a hearing will be in the fourth month, and I have thought about it every night since the twelfth, and I have come to the conclusion that I would rather be asked than safe.

"I am not going to sign anything. I want that written down. I am not going to say yes to a thing I do not understand, and I do not understand this, and I have been told the words for it, and they are not the same as understanding it."

Her voice went at the end of that, and she got through it, and the room let her.

Rell said, "Read it back," to her clerk.

---

The registrar read the summary. It was four lines long and Marek had read it eleven times and could have said it in the dark.

It set out that on the fourth month of this year a clerk of the Mosswake Roll Court prepared a summary of a seed-barn term instrument in the name of Orra Dunn, sole signatory; that on the twelfth of this month the summary was entered on the flood docket on the authority of a sentence read in open court on the same afternoon; that the summary carried, in the surety line, a minor of the household; and that the present holder of the barn might be joined as guarantor until full review.

"That is the whole of the document," the registrar said. "It is four lines. It was not drafted by me. It was prepared in this building in the fourth month and it sat in a tray for five months and I entered it on the docket at the second hour of the twelfth in the ordinary way, under a sentence read at the second hour of the twelfth, and I have entered it in the minute book and I will say to this court that I did not know whose hand was in the surety line before today and that I have had my own looked at since Tuesday, and it is mine, and I would like that noted, because I do not want it said afterwards that somebody else put it there."

Rell said, "It is noted, and thank you, Mr Feave, and I am going to come back to the fourth month in a moment."

The parties who intended to ask questions.

The factor in the good coat stood up first and gave his name as Anselm Grice, of the upper district, seed factor, and asked Orra Dunn one question, which was whether she was disputing that the barn term instrument existed.

"No," Orra said. "It exists. I signed it. He signed it and I witnessed it and I have never disputed it and I am not going to start now because I am frightened."

"And whether she was disputing the summary."

"Yes."

The miller asked whether the family had a guarantor. Orra said no. The woman in black asked, in a voice of great patience, whether the household had any other security. Orra said, and Marek had to write it down because he could not remember hearing it, "I have got a daughter and a floor and forty quarters of rye and I have told you what they are worth and you know it better than I do."

And the clerk of the Seeders' Aid Fund asked one question, and it was the only question in the morning that was about anything other than paper, and he asked it to the magistrate and not to the witness.

"Magistrate, does the postponement carry a bar on enforcement?"

Marek felt it arrive before Rell did.

"No," Rell said. "It does not. I am coming to it. I have not decided it yet and I am not going to have a man in a portfolio put the shape of my order in the record before I have made it. Sit down, Mr — "

"Parrow. Hallow Parrow, of the Seeders' Aid Fund."

"Sit down, Mr Parrow, and you will get your answer in the same words I give it to the household, which is the practice of this court and which I am not changing for a portfolio."

---

Marek stood up.

"Magistrate, I am the applicant's man and I am going to be as short as I can be, and I am going to start with the part that is against my side, because if I do not say it nobody in this room can use it later.

"I have no offer. I want to be plain about that in a room that contains four creditors. I have come in here with a date and a signature and nothing else, and I have known since Tuesday that those were the only two things in the building. The sentence that put this summary in motion was stated by this court on the twelfth. I have read the file. I have got a copy of the form that came out of that file with a series number and a print run on it, and it does not say the sentence is lawful and it does not say the sentence is unlawful. It says a court is not obliged to look at these filings one at a time. And I have got two districts four hundred miles from here where a clerk put a mark in a witness line for four shillings, and I have not got the file, and I have not got the name of anybody who wrote anything, and I have not got a single thing in this room that I can put in front of these four people and say: this is what the mechanism is, and here is the person, and therefore you will hold.

"So I am not going to insult this morning by pretending I have brought an answer. I am going to ask this court for three things and tell the room in advance that I expect to get one of them, and I would like the creditor and the fund and the factor to hear all three asked for, and not only the one I get.

"First: that the guarantor duty against the present holder of the Dunn barn be postponed pending full review, and that the postponement be dated. I have no argument that it should be anything other than that, and I am not going to make one, because the file before the court is the court's own file and the court knows what is in it better than I do.

"Second: that the child's answer be taken, and read back to her, and entered in the minute book, and that the copy go to her mother and not to the applicant. I am told that is irregular. I do not care. A record of a child's answer that nobody has read back to the child is a copy.

"Third: that the postponement carry a bar on enforcement until the review, so that a duty which has not attached cannot be used as security and cannot be named in a demand."

He sat down.

Rell was quiet for a while.

"The applicant has told this court to his face that he has no case, and I am going to record that, because in nineteen years I have had four applicants tell this court that and I can remember three of them and none of the three had read the loan to anybody in a good coat.

"Mr Parrow, you asked me the question. The answer is no, and I am going to give you the whole of it, because you are entitled to know what you are dealing with in two months' time.

"The court postpones the guarantor duty against the present holder of the Dunn barn pending full review. It does not attach. It is not decided. It is not a finding for anybody.

"A duty that is pending is a duty. It exists. It has a number and a date on it. Any creditor of that household may name it in a demand, may hold security against it, and may go and tell a factor in Greyfen that a floor is coming, and the demand does not wait for the review, and the factor does not wait for the review either, and I cannot prevent that, because the alternative is that this court has decided something it has not heard.

"I know what that does. I said it out loud in the recess on the twelfth and I am saying it out loud again. A postponement is worse for that household than a refusal would have been, and a refusal would have been better for them, and I cannot give them a refusal, because the sentence that put them here is mine and the file they are in is this court's, and if I refuse it today then somebody reads the refusal in the third month and says: the court will do it, it only wants somebody to push it.

"So I am going to postpone it, and I am going to date it, and I am going to read the date out twice, and I am not going to put a bar on it, and the reason I am not going to put a bar on it is that the bar you are asking for is a thing a court can only give where it has decided the duty is not going to attach, and I have not decided that, and if I write a bar on it today then in the fourth month somebody will read my bar and say that a bar was available, and why not in the case after it, and why not ever."

Marek understood what had just happened and it was not the thing he had come for and it was not the thing he had expected either, and he sat there with his hands on the front of the bench and let it happen, which was the hardest physical thing he had done in three weeks.

"Nessa Dunn," Rell said. "Your answer has been taken and read back to you. Do you want to say anything to it."

"No," Nessa said. "It's right."

"Entered," Rell said. "Copied to the mother. And Mr Feave, I am going to direct the registrar to enter this in the minute book in these words: that this court will not again apply the sentence read in this court on the twelfth of this month to any summary, filing, or household, upon its own motion, in any matter, without a separate hearing, and without the signature of a named person who has been heard. I have had that in my hand for eleven days. I should have written it on the twelfth. I am writing it on the twenty-third, in front of the people it is about, and I would like the registrar to enter that it is written on the twenty-third and not on the twelfth."

Feave wrote it, and read it back, and Rell said, "Good. That is the first thing this court has done in nine days that is worth the paper."

---

They came out into the corridor at half past twelve and the room behind them was still full, and Orra Dunn was in it for a while after, giving the same four answers to four more people, patiently, in the voice she had used for the seed factor.

Marek was at the end of the aisle with his hat in his hand when she came out.

"Magistrate." She had the shawl round Nessa's shoulders and she was not looking at Marek. "A word."

Rell stopped.

"You told me on the second day that a hearing would cost me the whole household."

"I did."

"You were right," Orra Dunn said. "I went in there and I gave you the names and the figures and the four quarters of rye and my mother's house in a note, and the man from the fund asked me what security I had and I said my daughter, in a room, in front of a court, and I said it because you were going to ask me the same thing and I would rather it was me that said it."

"I am sorry for that."

"Do not be," she said. "If I have said it out loud in here then the man in the portfolio has got it in his book and it is out of my mouth, and a thing that is out of my mouth can be looked at, and a thing that is only in his book cannot. I did not do it for the court. I did it because the only copy of it that matters is one somebody else is holding."

Rell looked at her for a moment, and then she said, "Then you have done one thing this morning that the law will not do for you, and I am not going to take it away from you by praising it. Go home. The review is on the fourth month's docket and you will have the date in a week."

---

The clerk of the Seeders' Aid Fund was standing by the window with his portfolio open when Marek got to him, and he did not look friendly, and he was not unfriendly either. He was a man about thirty-five in a coat that had been made for a warmer office.

"Clerk Kest."

"Mr Parrow."

"You told that room the sentence is on its way to a printed form." He said it without heat. "I want to ask you a question and I am going to ask it here and not in there, because in there you would have had to answer to the magistrate and I would rather have the answer without a magistrate in it."

"Ask."

"Is this the end of it. For that household. Is there a fourth month, or is there a month after the fourth month, or is there a printed thing that comes out of a drawer in the fifth year and finds a household that has never heard of a court in Mosswake."

Marek opened his mouth to say *for that household, with that file, this morning, there is nothing further that can reach her without her name on a paper or a court saying it in a room she is standing in.*

It did not come. It was not there. It had not been there for a month, and he had stopped expecting it, and he had just been about to say it in a corridor to a man with a portfolio because it was the polite and sufficient answer and it was the true answer as far as the morning went.

What came out instead was: "There is a form with a series number and a print run on it that has gone to every court in this empire that files a flood docket. I have read it and it is four paragraphs long and it does not do anything at all. It says that a court is not obliged to look at these filings one at a time. It has already been in two other districts that I know of, and one of them is four hundred miles away, and I have not got either file, and I do not know the name of anybody who wrote a word of the sentence that put it there, and I have said all of that in open court this morning where four people heard it, and I am now saying it to you in a corridor because I have just found out that I cannot say the other thing, and because you will get the other thing out of me or you will not, and I would rather you had the true one for your own reasons than the comfortable one for mine."

Hallow Parrow closed the portfolio.

"That," he said, "is worth more to me than the comfortable one, and I am going to be honest with you about why, because we are two men in a draught and neither of us is going to be thanked. If it is one household then I write one line and my committee reads it in the third month and it goes in a drawer. If it is a form that goes everywhere then my committee has to decide whether this fund is lending against a term that a court has published and a form has distributed, and that is a different instrument, and I would have to put it to them, and the fund is small and I am its clerk and I would rather be the man who brought them the true thing in the twenty-fourth than the man who brought them the comfortable thing in the twelfth and was corrected in the fourth month by somebody else."

"I am not asking you to do anything with it."

"No," said Parrow. "You are not. That is why I am telling you what I am going to do with it."

He opened the portfolio on the window sill in the draught from the embankment door.

He wrote for some time. He wrote with his coat off. He did not hurry and he did not look up at anybody who went past, and when he had finished he turned the sheet round and looked at it, and tore the carbon off, and folded the top copy twice and put it in the inside of his coat.

Then he put the portfolio under his arm and went out to the steps, where the packet boat was loading, and down the steps with the same unhurried walk, and got on it.

Marek stood in the corridor and watched the sheet go into a coat, and understood the whole of the finding he had argued for.

The court had given Orra Dunn a date. The date was a number. The number was on its way to a factor out of Greyfen at the speed of a river boat, and it was going to arrive in a place where nobody had to ask whether a duty was decided, because everybody in that trade knows that a duty with a date on it is a debt with a countdown on it, and the question the trade asks is not *is this true* but *how long have I got*.

And the second thing, which was worse, because it was his own: a fund that had believed him would have written one line. A fund that had been told the truth had to put a form in front of a committee, and a committee that is told there is a distributed form does not put its money away for a month. It puts its money somewhere safer, faster, and it does not matter to it in the slightest whose floor it is standing on when it gets there.

Marek had spent eleven days trying to get one sentence into a widow's mouth.

The sentence had gone into a fund's book instead, and it was going to cost Orra Dunn her floor anyway, and the only thing he had bought for her was four days of certainty about when it would happen.
