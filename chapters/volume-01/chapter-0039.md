# Chapter 39: The Fourth Question in a Room of Eleven

She did the Caudle house first, on foot, an hour down the road, on the fifteenth of the fourth month, with the four questions on a sheet in her coat and her own book in her other hand, because there is a thing you do about a number on your own lane before you do anything else with a number on somebody else's.

Nery Caudle had the paper out from under the cloth. It had been out since the second of the month and it had been on the bench in the same place for a fortnight, face up, with a cup on the corner to keep the wind off it, and nobody in that house had turned it over.

"Have you looked at it."

"I have looked at it every day for a fortnight," said Nery Caudle. "I want that written down first, before you ask me anything. I have looked at it every day and I have not read it, because I cannot read, and I have been ashamed of that for a fortnight, and you are going to come in with four questions and I am telling you now so that you do not ask me the first three slowly."

"Who is it from."

"That is the thing." Nery Caudle moved the cup. "It is not a letter. There is nobody at the top of it. It is a sheet with our number on it — the same number as the amendment, the one he said was the same paper — and a line at the top that says what it is, and the line at the top says: *Notice of the person who holds, entered under the second instrument in this house.*"

"Read me the top."

"There is a box." Nery Caudle put her thumb beside it without touching the ink. "It is the ordinary box. It is the box that says *the person who holds*, which is on everything anybody has ever sent me, and there is a name in it, and the name is Mike."

Nell Kest did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Mike."

"Mike Caudle. My husband. Nineteen years on that bench. And there is nothing else on the sheet, Mike, that is his name, and no signature, and no date except a date at the top which is the second week of the twelfth month, and I have had it on this bench for a fortnight, and I have not been able to work out what it is *for*, and you are the first person I have asked and I have asked you because you were in my kitchen in the second of the second month and I did not have anybody else."

"Then the fourth question."

"Who read it to you, and do they get paid if you sign."

"The man on the rack." Nery Caudle said it flatly. "And I have got a different answer this time and I have been sitting on it for a fortnight, so write it down before I lose my nerve. He read it out. All of it. Twice, and the second time slower, which is the same as last time, and I asked him about the box and he read the box out twice as well. And then he put his thumb over a line, and it was not the same line as last time."

"Which line."

"It is under the box and it is a small line and it says that the person named in the box is not a party to the amendment and is not liable under it and holds no duty under it and enters into it of no obligation." Nery Caudle looked at her own hand on the cloth. "And he put his thumb there. Not over Mike's name. Over the line that says Mike is not in it. And when his thumb was there I could not see whether it said what he read out or whether it said the other thing, and I have thought about that for a fortnight, and I am not going to be able to tell anybody, and I would like it written down that a reader put his thumb over the only line in the room that was in Mike's favour."

Nell Kest wrote it in her own book, at the kitchen table, with the children coming in and going out, and read it back, and Nery Caudle made her read back the small line twice.

"And the other thing," said Nell. "What is the figure on it."

"Two pounds five and a quarter, and a second one, and the second one is eight pounds." Nery Caudle turned the sheet over. "It is dated the second week of the twelfth month and it is payable on the twenty-ninth of the tenth, which is the date I signed, and Marl Crenn told you in front of me in the second week of the twelfth that she could not put a levy in force until the next quarter was struck, and could not strike a quarter in arrears, and would not."

"So this asks you for the second quarter before the quarter that carries it has been struck."

"Yes. And the figure is not the figure in the book. The book says two pounds five and a quarter and I have paid two pounds five and a quarter out of my own wages off a cloth, and this says eight pounds, and there is nothing anywhere in this house that I have ever agreed to eight pounds."

Nobody in that kitchen could find out who had filled in the box, and that was not because anybody was hiding it. It was because the sheet carries a guild's number and a number is all a sheet carries, and the number was the same number as the amendment, and there are, as of the fifteenth of the fourth month, two documents in that house with that number on them and neither of them is an instrument that anybody in the house has signed twice.

Nell Kest wrote one more thing in her book that evening, under the eight pounds, in a hand that got worse as it went.

*There is a line in the fourth term of a ruling I read out loud in a lane to about two hundred people that says nobody may be made to take on a continuing duty at a place without signing for it. I read it out on a table and I thought it was the best thing in the whole case. In this kitchen, on a bench, under a cloth, there is a sheet with a man's name in a box and no signature and he has not been asked, and the line under the box says he is not a party and owes nothing, and I cannot see the line because of a thumb.*

*I am not going to say those are the same thing. I have been in this four months and the one thing I know is that a person who sees two shapes and says they are the same shape is how nine hundred and forty copies of a thing happen. I am writing down that I saw it. I am writing down that I saw it and cannot prove it and I am going to have to sit with that.*

*And the difference, which is the whole of the difference and which I would put in front of a court if there were anything to put it in front of: in the lane, the man signed.*

---

The room with the assessor in it was the guild's assessment room on the second floor, and it was hot, and eleven women from the next province had come sixty miles in a cart and a hired carriage to be in it.

The assessment is the fourth month's business and Marl Crenn runs it, and before she struck anything she did the thing she had said in January she would do: she read the next province's decision out in her own hand, and the woman from the next province read out her own sheet, and the fifth line on the back of the craft exemption form was read aloud in a room for the second time in four months.

Then Nell Kest asked the fourth question.

She had planned it for eleven days and she had planned it the way she planned everything, which was by writing out what she was going to say, and she had written four versions and thrown three of them away, and what she had left was the four questions on a sheet and the sentence *I am not here as the assessor and I am not here for the guild and I am here because I copy clauses into schedules for a wage and I am going to ask you the same four things I ask anybody, and the last one is the one that decides the other three, and I am not going to apologise for asking it in your own room.*

She asked the first three and got ordinary answers, and then she asked the fourth, in front of eleven women who had come sixty miles.

"Who read this to you, and do they get paid if you sign."

The assessor from the next province did not look at her. She looked at the copy of the amended clause that was in the leather case on the desk, which is the same copy she had brought in a cart in the first month, and she took about four seconds.

"Yes," she said. "I am paid to read instruments to people. It is nine-tenths of the work and it is the whole of what an assessor is. A woman of fifty in a small dyemasters' association in the next province reads instruments to about two hundred people a year and is paid for the reading and is not paid for the answer, and the reason she is not paid for the answer is that the answer is not hers and never was."

"And do you get paid if they sign."

"No." She shut the leather case. "And that is your whole answer and I am going to say one more sentence and then I am not going to be asked anything else in this room, and I would like the sentence written down, and then I would like the meeting to go on."

Nell wrote it down. "Say the sentence."

"You have asked me in my own association's room, in the presence of eleven houses, whether I am paid to read and whether I am paid for the answer. Everyone in this room has now heard me say that the two are different, and the eleven of them came here for a decision about a certification and are going home with a piece of information they did not have at noon, and that information is not worth my seat to me and I have had my seat for nine years." She put her hand flat on the case. "You are not going to build anything out of that. I know what you are. You will write it in a book and you will not put it in front of a court, because there is nothing in front of a court, and I want to say now, in front of these eleven, that I know that, and I am not offended by it, and I would do it again."

"I was not going to build anything out of it."

"You were going to write it down," said the assessor, "and that is the whole of what you were going to do, and it is the correct thing to do, and I have just made a fool of myself in front of a guild for no reason at all except that I have spent nine years reading other people's papers out loud and nobody has ever once asked me whether I get paid for it."

Nobody else in the room said anything. Marl Crenn wrote *paid to read, not paid for the answer* on the corner of her own sheet and did not say she was going to use it, and eleven women looked at each other, and the meeting went on, and nothing was decided, and everybody in the room had heard it.

Nell Kest wrote the yes down in her own book, in her own hand, with the date on it, and did not write anything under it. That was the whole of what she took away from a room of eleven houses: a sentence that is on the record as having been said out loud, and no case built out of it, ever.

---

She walked back up the road with an hour of the day gone and three-quarters of a shilling in her pocket, and she did the arithmetic on the road, in the cold, which is the only arithmetic in this that is not somebody else's.

Four hours after the sixth hour. Twenty-three houses to walk before the assessment. Forty in the two districts that have written, and about a hundred that have not. The dyers' lane is a mile and a half end to end and half of it is not a house anybody is at, because the front doors face a yard and the people are in the back rooms behind the vats. A knock at the front of four houses in an hour is not four conversations, it is four door-openings and four sets of the same four sentences said quickly to somebody with a vat going.

She had already said that out loud to a woman at a kitchen table in Greyfen on the second of the second month, and she could not unsay it, and it had been true then and it was truer now, and the difference between then and now was that in February it was a thing she was complaining about, and in the fourth month it is the shape of the whole job.

One woman. One room. Four hours. Every name on every list in this case — the hearing slate, the levy sheet, the four in chalk, the eleven houses, the twenty-three doors, the forty copy-holders, the ten who have not answered — is one of those rooms. There is one of her. She is twenty, she is at a vat from the sixth hour, and the four questions cost three and a half hours each done properly, which is a day's pay to ask a person one thing.

Which is why the thing that has actually spread, in four months, is four short sentences on a board that anybody may say to anybody, and not a woman. She put the board up on the sixth of the twelfth month because she was frightened of being the only one who knew the questions, and she had been right about the danger and was completely wrong about the size of it.

---

She did four doors on the lane and then stopped, because the name of Wenna Sallow was in chalk on the board at the trestle, in her own hand, put back in the morning after the twenty-eighth of the first month as she had said she would, and it had been in chalk for eleven weeks.

She did not go to the Sallows' gate.

That was a decision and she wrote it down afterwards as a decision, because it was one, and because the reason for it was that a girl of nineteen had told her at a gate in the dark that she had put her name back on a board for three months being the person who helps and had not been asked a single question about her own house, and that going up to that gate in the fourth month because a name was in chalk would be the exact thing that had been asked for.

So she wrote in her book: *Wenna Sallow's name is on the board and I have not gone, and the reason is that being on the board is the only thing I can give her that I have not got somebody else's permission for, and going would take that away.*

And then she did the two doors she had left, which were the two that had been on her list since Michaelmas and which she had never got to, and the people in both of them had read the amended clause and signed it and did not know it existed.

---

The letter came to the table at about the eighth hour on the sixteenth of the fourth month and the boy from the lane brought it up off the post and left it on the flat of the trestle, and nobody read it for an hour because there were two women in front of the table.

The address was in a hand that had been taught in a schoolroom and had not been taught out of it, and it was four lines, and it was addressed the way a letter is addressed when the person writing it has been told that there is a lane with a table on it where a paper may be brought and read out loud and nobody will tell you what to do about it, and who believes it, and has written the address accordingly.

> *To the table at the mouth of the lane. From a house in Emberwatch, and nobody has to know which one, and my name is Osa Ferren and I am one of the four who did not answer and I am sorry about that.*
>
> *I have read all three papers you sent. There are four of us and we have read them out loud to each other in a kitchen and we understand them and we have four questions and nobody to ask.*
>
> *Our assessor has read them too, and he says a house in our trade may not write to a court without his leave in writing first, and he has given us leave, and this is the first thing this trade has ever written to a court, and it is yours, and I do not know what to do with it.*
>
> *Tell me what a person does who is owed an answer. Not what I should sign. What a person does.*

Nell Kest read it four times, which was more of one woman's handwriting than she had read in a month, and then she did the thing she had been doing all month, which was the only thing she had found that works.

She wrote it out on the back of the copy, in full, and read it back to herself under her breath to see whether the words were the writer's, and then she put the copy on the stone in the middle of the trestle where anybody who came to the table would have to move it to get at anything else, and she wrote on the board in chalk, under the four questions and the four names and the two lines of arithmetic and the four lines about the fifth line:

*Emberwatch. Four hundred and forty miles. A trade that has never had a dispute. It is not a question and it is not a paper. A person down there has asked what a person does who is owed an answer, and nobody in this lane knows, and I am going to find out and I am going to be the one who goes.*

And she was gone up the road at the ninth hour, in her coat, on foot, to find out whether there were four hours in the day.
