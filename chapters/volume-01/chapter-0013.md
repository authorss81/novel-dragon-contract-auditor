# Chapter 13: The Register

Orra Dunn could not remember a sentence anybody had said to her. She could remember a number in a way that other people found unnerving, and she had worked out years ago that this was not a strength, it was a partition wall, and that what lived on her side of it was a woman who could look at a floor and tell you what it would fetch in the second month whether or not anybody had asked her.

On the morning of the twenty-fourth she got up before Nessa and did the figures on the board.

The board was the flat back of a seed tally that had belonged to the barn before it was hers, and she kept a charcoal stub in a loop of string round her wrist, and she did not keep it for sentiment. A board can be wiped. A page is evidence and a page gets carried into rooms.

She wrote the four debts down the left side in a column, in the order she would have to pay them, which was not the order she owed them.

*Grice, seed, commission and paper against floor. Four hundred and eleven.*
*Kell, mill, seed bought on my note. Ninety.*
*Kell senior, the actual money behind the mill note. A hundred and forty.*
*Tale, the forty quarters. Ninety-two.*

Then on the right she wrote what the floor was, and what the floor was worth, and what the floor was worth with a duty standing on it.

*Forty quarters rye, in and sound, worth at the open market four hundred and eighty, if it is sold by a keeper who is not in a hurry and nobody has a duty on it.*
*Same forty quarters with a pending guarantor duty in the second month, sold on, in a hurry, to a factor: two hundred and ten.*
*The barn itself I do not own and cannot sell and am not going to try.*

She looked at the two numbers for a long time and then wrote under them, because she was a woman who wrote things down:

*The difference is two hundred and seventy. The difference is not a market. It is a court saying a number out loud on a Friday.*

Then she wiped the board, because the two hundred and seventy was not a figure she was going to have lying about in a house with a girl in it, and she put the four debts back on it and nothing else, and made the tea.

---

The four of them.

The first was Anselm Grice, and going to see Grice was not going to a man, it was going to a building with a stove in it and a boy who wrote down the name of everybody who came in. Orra had been going up to the seed office since she was nineteen and she knew exactly how much of a morning Grice could be made to give a person, and she had known on the Friday, in the corridor, watching the portfolio, that she was not going to get a morning out of him now. She got eleven minutes.

Grice was not cruel. That was the thing she had never been able to explain to anybody. He was a middle-aged man with a bad knee and a good memory for faces, and he asked her to sit, and he sent the boy out, and he let her finish, and then he said:

"I have got a paper on you and I have told the man in Greyfen that I have got a paper on you, and he has not lied to me, and I am going to tell you what that means for the price of your floor and then I am going to stop talking about your floor, because you have been in a court and I have got a boy to feed."

"Go on."

"Free and clear, on Thursday, before anybody knows, at four hundred and ten. With the duty on it, on the twenty-third, at two hundred and thirty, and I have to be able to show a man in Greyfen a date and not a hope." He turned his knee. "That is the whole of the difference between you being paid and me being paid, and I have got a bad knee and a boy at home and I am not going to pretend to you that I would hold it for the shape of a hearing."

"Then hold it for the shape of a date," Orra said. "There is a date now. The review is on the fourth month's docket. I have got it in writing in a week. If you hold to the fourth month I will not have to sell to a factor at all and you will have me at the open market in a barn that is not half emptied, because a seed factor who has had to buy a floor off a keeper does not get the same price for the next one as a man who has waited."

"You are asking me to be paid in April."

"I am asking you to be paid in April at four hundred and eighty instead of in January at two hundred and thirty," said Orra, "and you can count that in your own head, because you are better at it than I am."

Grice looked at her. He did the thing she had watched him do to a hundred people, which was to move his eyes as though he were reading a column of figures on the far wall, and then he said, "I will hold to the first week of the fourth month and not one day past, and I want the date in writing and I want your daughter off the paper."

"She is not on the paper. She was put on the paper in April by somebody in this building."

"Then I want her taken off it in front of me," said Grice, "and after that I will hold."

---

The miller was easy, and being easy was a relief and a warning.

Bodo Kell owed ninety and had been told to say so in a room, and he said it in the yard without being asked, and then he said, "Ma will hold. Ma holds anything I tell her to hold and I have not yet told her what to hold, and when I do she is going to ask me what it is worth to her to be wrong, and I am going to have to say less than the truth, because the truth is that it is worth about eight pounds to her and about my whole life to me, and she is sixty and I am thirty-one."

"Then tell her what it is worth to her," Orra said. "Do not tell her a number. Tell her the floor is not named. If the floor is not named then the duty is not on the floor, and a duty that is not on the floor is not on your note, and Ma is lending to you and not to me."

"You have had a court day," Kell said. "I can hear it."

"I have had a court day and I have come out of it with a date and no bar, and the no bar is the part I keep going back to at night," said Orra. "So I am going to use it. I am going to ask the mill for its paper to say in terms that the floor is not security. If Ma can see that written, then you have a paper and I have a paper and neither of them is a promise."

That took two days and it took Kell's mother writing her own sentence into a document at the end of it, which she did with a steady hand and which cost her nothing, and which Orra understood would be the last time in a long while that Ilsabet Kell signed anything for anybody.

---

The fourth was Emrys Tale and the fourth was the whole of it.

Tale lived four doors down the lane with a wife who did not speak to Orra any more, which had started in the fourth month and had not been about anything Orra could remember. He held the second paper on the floor, the one she had given him in the winter of the sixth year for the forty quarters of rye when she had had nothing and he had had a cart, and he had been decent about it for four years, and he was the reason she had a floor at all.

She went to his door on the second day and he opened it and did not ask her in, and she had expected that.

"Emrys."

"Orra." He had his hands behind him. "I am going to say it in one go and you are not to interrupt me, because if you interrupt me I will stop and I will start again tomorrow and it will be worse tomorrow.

"I have sold the paper to Grice. Nine days ago. He gave me a hundred and six for it and it was worth ninety-two and I want you to know that I have known what it was worth every day since the day I signed it, and I sold it anyway, and the reason I sold it is that on the Thursday when the man in Greyfen was in the town asking about floors, I was told what a floor goes for when it has to go quick, and I did the arithmetic on the ninety-two and I thought: a hundred and six now, and the certainty of it, and I have got a boy at the end of this lane who is nine and I have not been right about a thing in eleven years."

Orra Dunn stood in the mud of a lane in the upper district and looked at a man who had done the one thing in eleven years that he was probably going to be judged on, and she found that she was not angry, and that she was not glad either, and that the thing she felt instead was a kind of professional respect for a sum done correctly at the wrong moment.

"Right," she said. "When does he serve it."

"He will not serve it. He will not serve it on you and he will not serve it on the girl, because he has said so, and I have made him say it in the yard in front of the miller's mother, which was not asked of me and cost me a good dinner and a bottle." Tale shifted. "What he is going to do is serve it on the *seed-share*. The forty quarters in the barn are the girl's, and it is the only thing in this lane with a name on it, and the paper he bought off me names the floor, and a floor is the ground under the forty quarters, and he has been to two lawyers about it, and one of them says the seed-share cannot be taken and one of them says it can be taken if it is under the ground, and he is going to use the second one, and he is going to do it in the second month, and he has told me he will do it in the second month."

"Then the second month," said Orra, "is the number that is going to kill us, and it is not the court's number."

"It is not the court's number," said Tale. "It is his, and he has had it since the twelfth, and everything the court has said since the twelfth has been a door for him."

---

She went home by the low path and stopped at the seed barn and put her hand flat on the wall of it, which was not sentiment either, it was habit, she had been checking that wall for frost since she was nineteen.

Bettany Skail was at the low end of the lane with a basket on her arm and a paper bag in it.

"No," Orra said.

"I have not said anything."

"You have got a paper bag."

"I have got four pounds of winter tilling seed that you have not got any use for," said Bettany Skail, "and I have got a bag to carry it in, and I have walked all the way down here in the rain, and you are going to take it, Orra, because there is four pounds of it in that bag and you have got forty quarters going into the fourth month and no tilling seed, and the fourth month is when the fourth month is."

"There is a floor full of tilling seed," Orra said.

"There is a floor full of *rye*," said Bettany Skail, "and rye is not tilling seed, and I am a woman who has bought seed for forty-one years and I know the difference, and you know the difference, and you are going to stand there and tell me that you are too proud to take four pounds off a woman who feels sorry for you, which is a thing I would rather you did not do in front of this barn."

Orra took the bag.

That was the day. Not the court, and not the number on the board, and not Tale's hundred and six. The day a neighbour of forty-one years' standing walked half a mile in the rain to be sorry at her, and handed her something she did not need, and would not be able to refuse it, and which could not be given back.

---

On the third day she went down to the court.

The clerk's window was open and there was a bench outside it, and the window had a board on the side of it with a slate hanging on a nail, and the slate had the day's attendance on it in a hand that had been going thirty years and had got very small at the end of a line.

Orra had been to that window four times in her life. Three of them had been about the seed-share.

"Clerk. I want to ask for a thing and I am going to tell you why it is a stupid thing before I ask for it, so that you can turn me down with a clear head."

Sarn put her pen down, which Orra had not expected. "Go on."

"I told a court on Friday that I did not want my household named anywhere. I said it out loud, to a magistrate, in front of nine people, and I meant it, and I have been thinking about it for three days and I have got two answers to it and I do not like either one and I am here anyway."

"Both of them?"

"The first is that I am a coward about a name, which is true, and my name in a book is a name somebody can use, and I have spent four years building a floor out of nobody owing anybody anything and I do not want to be findable." She put her hands flat on the sill. "The second is that a duty which exists in one man's book is a duty that only that man can say is false. Grice has got it in Greyfen and Lave has got it in Glasswater and Tale has sold it and the fund has got it and there are five people in this province who can tell me the state of my own household and I am not one of them and my daughter is not one of them and neither is the girl who cut the ditch. The only place I cannot get to is a room where anybody can walk in and look."

Sarn wrote for a while, in a small hand, and then stopped and read it back to herself and then said:

"You are asking me to publish you."

"I am asking you to write down that there is a duty standing against the Dunn household of the upper seed barn, what it is, what date it was made, when it is next looked at, and where a person can come and read it. And I am asking you to write down that I asked, and that is the part I would like."

"That is a register of pending duties," Sarn said. "There is no such thing."

"Then make one," said Orra Dunn. "It costs you a slate and a book and it costs the court nothing and it is not law and it protects nobody, and I am not asking for it to protect me. I am asking for it because a thing nobody can look at has only got the people who want it to exist holding it, and there are five of them and I am the one it is on."

The clerk of the window looked at the slate on the nail, and at the board, and at the queue behind her, of which Orra was the fourth, and then she said, "It will take a shilling for the copy, and a shilling is a shilling, and I am not going to waive it in the first month or I will be waiving it in the fifth."

"Charge me the shilling."

"Orra Dunn," Sarn said, writing. "Household of the upper seed barn. Duty: guarantor, present holder, arising on a summary filed on the docket on the twelfth of the ninth month on the authority of a sentence stated in open court the same day. Person named in the surety line: minor, heard separately on the twenty-third, answer taken, read back, no signature given. Status: postponed pending full review. Next review: the fourth month, date to be entered. Source: this court. Copies at one shilling. Any person may come to this window and read it and any person may write to me about it and I will answer in writing, and anybody who puts a name to a request will be told that a name was given."

She tore the leaf out and put it on the sill between them.

"That is what it looks like," she said. "It is not very grand. It is the only thing I have got in this building that nobody has to ask my permission to read, and I am starting it today, and I am going to keep it in my own hand, and I am telling you now that it is the last thing I am going to start in a month in which I have found out that the last thing I started was four days that a household in the upper district paid for."

"Which household," said Orra Dunn.

Sarn looked at her. She did not look away and she did not answer it, and Orra, who was thirty-six and had a bad memory for words and a very good one for faces, watched a woman of thirty-eight decide in about two seconds not to say a thing, and filed it away, and did not push, because she had a girl at home and had learned in three days exactly how much pushing a person can afford.

"You will have the date in a week," Sarn said. "And the register will be here on Monday, and the register will be here whether or not anybody else in this building thinks it is a good idea, and I would like it noticed that I have written your daughter's refusal in it, because if it is not in the register it is not a fact, it is a thing you said."

---

She got the shilling's worth of paper and put it in the front of the board, folded, behind the four debts, where it could not be wiped.

Nessa was in the barn when she got back, sorting the middle bin, with her sleeves up and her hair tied, and she had done it since she was nine and did it the way everybody does a thing they have done too long, without looking.

"Ma."

"I got the date."

"What did they say."

"They said the review is in the fourth month and they said it out loud in a room, in writing, and I have got it on a bit of paper, and I have got to pay a shilling for a second one because I want it in a drawer and not in a coat." Orra hung the bag of tilling seed on a nail. "And there is a slate at the court window now with our name on it, and anybody can read it, and I asked for that and I would like you to be angry with me about it for a minute and then I would like you to tell me what you think about it, because you are fifteen and you have got a better head on this than I have."

Nessa put down the sieve she was holding.

"You told the court on Friday that you didn't want us named anywhere," she said.

"I did."

"And now there's a slate."

"A slate with a date on it and a review and my daughter's name and the word *no* in it," said Orra, "which is a different thing from a name in a book, and I have not worked out yet whether it is, and I am telling you that I have not worked it out instead of telling you I have."

The girl looked at the board on the wall, at the four debts in her mother's upright charcoal, and at the forty quarters of rye she had sorted since she was nine and would not be examined for until the fourth month, and then she said:

"Put it on the wall over the bench," she said. "Not folded in the board. On the wall. The seed office sends a boy round every Monday and he'll see it on the wall and he'll carry it back to Greyfen, and that's the point of it, isn't it. That's the point of it."

Orra Dunn stood in the barn with a bag of tilling seed she did not need on a nail behind her.

"That is the point of it," she said.

---

The packet boat came up on the fourth day and landed at the Mosswake stair with the ordinary muddle of sacks and passengers, and among them was a woman of perhaps thirty-five in a river court's grey coat who had travelled, by the state of her bag, a very long way, and who stood at the top of the stair for a quarter of an hour looking for somebody to ask.

She had a bundle of papers in a sacking wrapper and she had a shilling in her hand and she had been told, by somebody, that there was a slate at the court window where a person could come and look at a duty without anybody stopping them.

She told the boy on the stair that she was not looking for her own file, and that the file in her bundle was not hers, and that she had carried it four hundred miles because there was a copy of it in her court and a copy of it in a lane in Mosswake and neither of the two had the whole of it.

And when the boy, who was thirteen, said that the window was shut for the middle of the day, she said that was all right, and she would wait, and she sat down on the bottom step of the Mosswake stair with the bundle on her knees, in the rain, and would not give her name to anybody who came down.
