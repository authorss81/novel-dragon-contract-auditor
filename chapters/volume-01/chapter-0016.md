# Chapter 16: The Permission

The copy of the First Seat form in the alcove was made on the second day of the inventory in a room above a chandler's shop in Candlewright Lane, by Tamsin Rook, out of a chain that had been opened under a joint order in front of a constable, and it was nineteen lines long and four paragraphs and it had a series number and a run at the foot, and it was the only page in her case that she could not put in a drawer without writing down where it had been.

She had copied it in fair hand. She also, on the second evening in Auremar, in her own rooms over a laundry, with the door shut, copied it out again in a large plain script with the four paragraphs numbered, because there is a point at which a person stops being able to read a thing and starts being able to see it, and it had not happened to her with this page yet and she wanted it to.

Then she read the second copy, which is the one that undid her.

She had gone in expecting the same three things she had expected in the room above the chandler's: a sentence that said something was lawful, or a sentence that said something was unlawful, or a person. The first copy gave her none of them. The second copy gave her something worse, which was that it gave her nothing to object to at all.

*Continuity Filings, Flood Seasons, Categorisation.* Series as on the seal. Print run at the foot.

*1. A continuity filing accepted under flood authority in the season in question shall be entered in the routine column and not itemised in the individual column, for the purpose of the quarterly return.*

*2. Such filing shall not be itemised, scheduled, or heard individually except on quarterly review.*

*3. The recipient court is not obliged to examine such filings individually, and no request by an affected person for individual hearing shall of itself require the court to depart from this categorisation.*

*4. This form is not a direction to any court as to the validity of any filing, instrument, correction, or summary, and confers no authority and takes none away.*

Paragraph four. Tamsin read it eleven times over two days and then wrote in the margin of her own book, in her own hand, the sentence she would later say out loud in a court anteroom and mean:

*It is not a crime. It is a permission. The correct thing to do with it is administrative.*

Nothing in the four paragraphs was wrong. Every one of them was an ordinary thing for an office to want in a flood season, when a district has four hundred and sixty rolls and three weeks to certify them and a clerk who opens one more file is a clerk who does not certify the four hundred and fifty-nine. That was Rell's argument on the stair in the ninth month and it had been true, and it was still true, and the woman in the boat that had carried Tamsin to Mosswake in the seventh month had said much the same thing about her own relief in a harder voice.

What the four paragraphs did was remove the last person in the chain who might have looked.

A duty passed from a file to a summary because somebody compressed it. A summary was copied because that is what a summary is for. And then a form arrived that said a court is not obliged to look at these one at a time, and the answer to a person who says *I have heard something about my own barn* is no longer *you are mistaken* or *here is why*. It is: *the court is not obliged to examine this individually, and your request does not of itself require one.* And there is no lying in that. There is no one in the building who has done anything a person can be shown.

That was the discovery, and it came to her on the second evening, in her rooms, at about the second hour of the morning, and she sat on the edge of her bed and understood that she had been arguing against the wrong thing for a month. She had spent four weeks trying to prove that somebody had written a sentence on purpose. And nobody had. Somebody, ninety years ago, in a warm office, had built a space where a hurried thing would fit, and then somebody had printed a form that said the space need not be examined, and the whole of the machinery was in the fit and in the printing, and the printing had a run on it.

---

What she had to do about it was the part that took nine days, and the reason it took nine days was that there were two possible actions and both of them were wrong.

She could put the form where anybody could see it. That was the public act, and it was the one that was right on any measure she could construct in her own head, and it would have gone into a hundred hands in a day because a printed sheet with a run number on it is the easiest thing in the world to pass round. And it would have said, on the front of it, in the mouths of the people who put it out: this court, that court, the Mosswake Roll Court, has been told that it need not look.

Mosswake Roll Court, which had given a widow her field in five numbered terms and had refused to write a broad sentence out of its own head even when it wanted to, which had put a limitation on its own practice on the twenty-third in open session, and whose magistrate had been dragged up a river on the strength of a girl's iron bar and had asked not to be thanked for it in a lane.

Publishing the form was not a public act. It was an attack on a specific court by a copyist with no standing, and it would be read as an attack, because that is what it would be, and because the form did not name a court and would be made to name one by the person who sent it round.

Or she could say nothing, and then the people the form was about would find out what the form said from a district office, on a quarterly return, in the ordinary course, in a room with a slate on the wall.

Tamsin went round that second option nine times in nine days and each time she came back to the same fact, which was that the form was already in two districts she knew of and probably in nine, and that it was in print, and that the people it was about were in three districts, and that a warning she sent would arrive in a household before a filing did, and a warning she did not send would arrive after.

She could also — and this was the door she went near on the fifth day and closed again — tell Marek Kest first.

---

She saw Pell Vey on the fourth day, in the yard behind the Chancery, at about the ninth hour, and he was being helpful in a way that had become a temperature she could measure.

"Copyist Rook."

"Master Vey."

"You have been at Candlewright Lane three times this week." He said it pleasantly. He had a rolled paper under his arm that he did not need, in the manner of a supervisor on a walk. "I am not asking you for anything. If you would rather I did not know, I would find out from a boy, and I would rather be told by you, and that is the whole of my interest in it."

"I have not been at Candlewright Lane three times this week."

"You have been twice," said Pell, "and you took the case out of the building, which I notice, and which I would have noticed, and which I would have asked about if you had taken it to a public room. You are not stupid, and neither, apparently, is the constable."

Tamsin said nothing, and Pell Vey, who had been a supervisor for nineteen years and had taught more than half the clerks in three galleries how to keep a minute book, did the thing that she would think about afterwards, which was to change the subject himself, smoothly, like a man opening a second door so that the first one does not look shut.

"There is a form," he said. "I am not asking to see your copy. I have not seen it and I do not expect to. I am asking a professional question." He shifted the rolled paper. "Of the eleven people on the bench at Candlewright Lane, and the other two who gave evidence — how many of them have it?"

"How many of them do you have?"

"That is not the same question."

"It is the same question with the words turned round so that it does not sound like it," said Tamsin. "You have asked me how many of thirteen people you do not have. That is a count of what we have, not a count of what you have, and you did that on purpose in a yard, and I do not know whether you did it on purpose to find out or on purpose to be a man who asks, and I have watched you for a month and I still do not know which, and I am going to have to work as though it is the first one."

Pell Vey stood in the yard of a building where he had been the second most powerful man for nine years, and looked at the district's roll-keeper of twenty-four, and for the first time in the four weeks she had known him in this capacity he did not have an answer ready.

"Copyist Rook," he said. "I am going to say one true thing and then I am going to leave you alone, and you may write it down and you may not, and you should not believe me because I have said it nicely. I did not know about the form. I have been in this building for nineteen years and I did not know there was a form, and I found out about it three weeks ago the way everybody finds out, from a boy with a tray. If I had known about it in the eighth month of this year I would not have done anything about it, and you may put that in a book, and it is true, and it is the reason I have not asked you a single question about your copy. I am asking about your thirteen people because I have a worry of my own and I am going to have it in the wrong room, and I am too old to be interested in pretending otherwise."

"What is the worry."

"The run at the foot," said Pell Vey. "A form with a run on it is not a form about a court. It is a form about a business. And a business that prints four hundred things for four hundred courts has somebody in it whose job it is to know how many of the four hundred have complied, and that person is going to be asked, one day, in a room like the one your thirteen people are sitting in, and they are going to have to answer. And when they cannot answer, they will want to know which thirteen names are in the hands of a copyist. That is all. I am not asking you anything. I am telling you what I would be thinking about, and I am not doing it to be kind, I am doing it because a form with a run on it is a thing I should have seen, and I did not see it, and a man who did not see a thing in nineteen years is a man who has stopped looking, and I would like that on your side of the table rather than on mine."

He looked at her for a moment.

"You may tell Marek Kest what I have said," he added, "and he will not believe it, and I would rather you told him than have him find it out later and think it was a manoeuvre."

Then he walked off across the yard with the rolled paper under his arm and did not look back, and Tamsin Rook stood in the yard behind the Chancery for a good deal longer than she had intended, with eleven names in a list in her head, and understood that she had just been handed a thing that could help her and did not know yet how, and had also been told, in the plainest words anybody had used with her since the eighth month, that Pell Vey had a want and the want was the same one she had, and that wanting the same thing had not once made him a good man.

She did not tell Marek Kest. She wrote Pell's words down in her book, in full, with the hour and the place, and then she put a line under them, and the line was: *He says he did not know and did not act. That is one sentence too many for a man who had not read it. Ask him one day, not today.*

---

On the eighth day she went up to the room above the chandler's shop in Candlewright Lane and asked Ruhl Dunnet to open the chain.

She did it in a room with a constable in it and a boy at the door, and she asked for the chain and not for the contents, and she said the reason out loud before anybody could decide she was being clever.

"I am going to look at the second sealed page," she said, "and I am not going to open it. I am going to open the chain because a chain that is sealed can be described by anybody who has seen the outside of it, and a chain that has been opened in front of witnesses and not gone further has been described by a witness. And then I want it written down in a room that the page is there, that two people looked at it, and that neither of them opened it. Because the last time anything came out of a chain in a case like this, it came out because nobody had written down what was in the sleeve."

Dunnet looked at her for a moment and then said, "That is either the best reasoning I have heard from a copyist in nineteen years or the worst, and I cannot tell which, and I am going to allow it because a constable who will not allow a thing he cannot rule on has stopped being a constable and started being a person with a stick."

The chain was opened on the table in the middle of the room. The constable read the seal aloud and named the hour and the boy called it into the yard. Inside the sleeve were six things, and behind the Reed Cut memorandum, folded in, were two sheets with the old fire-and-water device pressed cold on the wax.

The first was the form. The second had a thread caught in its fold.

Tamsin did not touch either. She looked at both for about as long as it takes to read a line twice, and she did not put her hand within an inch of the second one's wax, and she said, out loud, in front of the constable and the boy, so that the words would be in the room: "There is a second sealed page. There is a thread in the fold. I have looked at it and I have not opened it and I am not opening it, and I want that entered in the inventory book, and I want it entered with the hour and with my name and with yours, constable, and I want a copy of the entry to go to the Mosswake court and one to Marek Kest's file whether he wants it or not."

The clerk's hand in the yard called the hour out loud again because the boy always did, and eleven people were not there that day but the neighbours were, and the entry was read back and initialled by three people.

Then the chain was closed and the seal went on and the hour went in the book, and Tamsin walked down the stairs with her case under her arm and thought, honestly, that she had done it because a rule of her own had been sitting on her for six weeks like a splinter and she had wanted it out, and that she had wanted it out at the same moment as it had become useful, and that those two things are almost never the same moment and she had got lucky.

---

She sent the copies on the ninth day.

Not published. Sent. There were thirteen names on the list: eleven from the bench at Candlewright Lane, and Brammel, and a twelfth who had given evidence in the market on the seventeenth, and a thirteenth who was a courier's clerk at the Mosswake court and had carried an inventory in a bad coat and had not been asked to do anything wrong. She had the ink and the wrapping paper and about four hours of a working day that belonged to nobody.

Each copy went with a sheet in her own hand, and the sheet said, in the plainest words she could find and then tried twice to make plainer:

*This is a printed form. It is four paragraphs. It is not about your file and it does not change anything in your file. It is a form that tells district courts that flood-season filings of a certain kind go in a routine column and are not examined one at a time, and it says a court is not obliged to look at them individually even if you ask. It says in its fourth paragraph that it is not about validity and gives no authority and takes none away.*

*You have been sent it because you are named in a record that somebody else may want to look at, and because you are the sort of person who would rather have a thing in the house than be told about it afterwards.*

*If you are ever brought a filing of that kind, in a flood season, with a number on it, you may take it to a court and ask to have it examined on its own, and a court that has received that form is not forbidden to do so; the form says it is not obliged, and does not obliged be the same as may not. Keep this copy. Show it to anybody who comes to your door with a paper. Keep a copy of anything they show you. If you are shown a paper and you are not sure, write to the number at the foot of the first sheet and somebody will answer you in writing.*

*This has not been made public, and that is a decision somebody has made for a reason, and if you are asked why, the true answer is that eleven people on a bench have lost their broker and cannot afford to be named in a public fight this month.*

*Your papers have not been taken and will not be taken. That is a separate promise and it is being asked for in a court in the capital this week.*

She wrote it thirteen times. She read it back thirteen times. And at about the sixth hour of the afternoon she put the thirteenth in the envelope and then did not seal it, and went and found Marek Kest instead, and told him.

She told him in one sentence, standing up, in the alcove, in front of the copying frames, with her hand on the back of a chair.

"I have sent the First Seat form to the thirteen named people with a warning and a date and an address for writing in, I have not published it, I did not ask you, and I decided on the ninth day and I am telling you afterwards on purpose."

Marek Kest put down his reed.

She watched him do it. She had been in the same room as this man every working day for a month and she had watched him work out a room four or five times, and she had watched him read a page the way other people read a face, and she had watched him fail to say a sentence to two women and a customs clerk and never once explain why, and she had known since the second of the month what it was and had not asked.

He opened his mouth.

He was going to argue. She could see the whole of it — the word *permission* arriving, and behind it *a court gave a widow her field*, and behind that a man who was going to try to tell her, in the most reasonable voice in Auremar, that a decision like this is not hers to take alone.

What happened instead was that nothing came out of him at all, and he shut his mouth, and then he said:

"No."

That was all. He said *no* and then he sat down again and looked at the frame in front of him for perhaps four seconds, and then he said, in a completely different voice:

"All right. Tell me the whole of it, from the beginning, in order, and do not leave out the parts where I would have been right."

Tamsin Rook sat down on the end of his bench and told him the whole of it, from the beginning, in order, including the parts where she was frightened and the parts where she had thought about asking him and had not, and including Pell Vey at the fourth hour in the yard, word for word, because she had promised herself on the sixth day that if she was going to do this she was going to do it in a way that could be read out afterwards by anybody.

It took fifty minutes. He did not interrupt her once, and when she had finished he wrote four things in his own hand, which were the things he should have written, and did not write the fifth.

When she had gone he put his hand over his eyes and said, to the empty room, "Three times. I have been outvoted three times in three rooms and this is the first one that was decided before I walked in."

Then he said, aloud, "I cannot even find out whether that is why I am not arguing, and that is the part of it I am going to have to sit with."

---

On the eleventh of the tenth month a clerk of the Reed Cut district water office received a return.

It was the ordinary quarterly return, on the ordinary form, with the ordinary number at the top, and the fourth question on it asked a district office how many continuity filings accepted under flood authority in the season then current had been itemised individually, and the fifth asked whether any such filing had been received from a person who had requested individual hearing.

The return went to Hallis Dren at the Reed Cut office, and it had come down on the ordinary distribution, and there was no reference on it to a court in Mosswake, and no name on it, and nothing in it that was wrong.

Hallis Dren sat in the water office with a stove in the middle of the room and a board on the wall with forty lines on it, and he looked at the fifth question for a long time, and then he did the thing that any competent clerk does, which is to look for the answer in his own books.

He had one line in his own books, because there was exactly one such filing in the Reed Cut office for the season, and it was the one he had issued the general memorandum for on the eighth of the eighth month, and the correction that had come in against it on the ninth had been entered and not argued, and had been carried on a docket that had been closed in the second week of the ninth month with a public rehearing and a five-term ruling and about forty people in a room.

The answer to the fourth question was one. The answer to the fifth was yes.

And a return is a form, and a form has to be answered by a person, and the person who answers it writes what is true, and what was true was that a court had examined a filing individually because a widow's daughter had asked, and the result of that was a copy of a printed form in a copyist's rooms, and the copyist had been asked how many of thirteen people she had it.

Hallis Dren did not know any of that. He knew a general paper, a filing under it, a docket, and a court. He did not know about the form with the run on it, and he had not heard of the First Seat's quarterly return, and he had no idea that the question on this return and the thing on his wall were two ends of the same piece of paper, and he never would.

He filled it in, honestly, in the ordinary way, in his upright hand, and sent it up, and then sat for a while with the pen in the box, in a warm office, in a district that had a fifth term nailed to a rail at the gate.

It was the correct answer. It was the only answer there was.

And it went, in a sealed sleeve on the ordinary distribution, to an office four hundred miles from Reed Cut that had been sent a form with a run on it and had been waiting, for two years, for somebody in a district to fill in a box.
