# Chapter 44: A Number Is About the Book

The answer came on the fourth of the sixth month from the Guild Assessors' function of the Craft Assessors' Guild, and it was two lines long, and it was the first thing in five months that came back to that lane with anything on it.

> *The notice described is not issued by this guild. This guild issues no notice of a person who holds. A notice of that kind, if a notice of that kind exists, would carry a number, because a document entered in a book carries the number of its entry in the book. The number on the notice described is the number of the second instrument entered in the Greyfen dyers' schedule for the quarter in question. The person who keeps that book is the schedule assessor for that lane.*

Nobody had asked who typed it. It had not occurred to the Guild Assessors' function that anybody might, and the answer they gave was about the book, and it was the answer to a question about the book.

---

Tamsin Rook read the two lines three times in the copying alcove of the third gallery of the Roll Hall and then went and stood in the passage behind the gallery for about a minute and a half without doing anything, which is a thing she does when she is about to be pleased with herself and has decided in advance not to be.

Then she copied out the whole of it in a fair hand, dated it, and went down the passage to the Forms Received rack.

The rack is unlocked. It has been unlocked for the whole of the eight months since a junior clerk learned in a corridor in the twelfth month of the ninth year that the answer to the question everybody was asking was in a drawer. It is eleven feet of deal shelving against a wall, in a passage that goes nowhere anybody wants to be, and it is indexed by the office that sent the thing and by nothing else, and it is the answer to this volume's question and it is also the answer to this question, and it will be the answer to the next question, and the reason is that the index card is the only key anybody has ever built.

She did not take anything out of it. That was decided before she got there and it was decided for a reason that took her about four seconds to work out standing in the passage: if she took a card out of the rack, then a card was out of the rack, and a card out of the rack is a thing a person can be asked about later by somebody who has an interest in the answer, and Tamsin Rook had spent four months in a copying alcove learning that a thing you can be asked about is a thing you have to be able to answer, and she could not answer it, and the not being able to answer it was the entire finding.

So she copied, standing up, with the card in place, leaning over the edge of it, and she copied three things, and she wrote the shelf and the bay and the hour on the back of her own hand and not on the card.

The first was the index card for the sending office.

> *Greyfen Dyers' — Schedule Assessor's Office. Instruction to assessors on the entering of a second instrument in a house. Series AS/2. Third shelf, fourth bay, behind the fourth year's returns.*

The second was the distribution line, which was not a distribution line at all but a filing line, and which said the thing the whole of this case is standing on:

> *Issued to the schedule assessor of every lane holding an assessment and filed with that assessor's standing book and not with the assessor's papers. Printed forms of this series are issued under the same authority and no further authority is required for their use.*

Which is a sentence Tamsin Rook had read before in the twelfth month of the ninth year standing on a step-ladder in the same passage about four feet from where she was standing, on a different card, in a different bay, from a standing office that had no person at the top of it, and she had read it then as the answer to a question about dragons' paperwork and a widow's winter, and she read it now as the answer to a question about a foreman's wife and a cloth with a vicar's sum in it.

The third card was the one the notice was claimed to belong to.

And the third card is where the whole of the fifth month went, and it took her a day and a half to be sure of it, because a date on the back of a card in a bay is a date a clerk wrote at some point in any year, and the only honest way to read one is against the run at the foot of the sheet, and the sheet is on the shelf under the card, and the sheet is not the notice.

> *AS/2. Instruction to assessors on the entering of a second instrument in a house. Second year of issue, being the third year of the sixth year back. Run four hundred. Issued under the authority of the Guild Assessors' function of the Craft Assessors' Guild.*

The Venn correction was received by a district court on the ninth of the eighth month of this year.

This was four years and four months older, and it is not a copy of anything, and it is not a fraud, and there is no wrong party in the room for a minute book to rule on, and it says this:

> *Where a second instrument is entered in a house in this trade after the sixth year, the box in the model endorsement headed the person who holds is to be completed, and the entry is made in the lane book under the number of the second instrument, and the person entered in that box is the person answerable under the first instrument for the term of the second.*

The box on the Caudle notice was not a word that walked about.

It was a box that a standing instruction in a guild's own office had told assessors to fill in, four years before a dragon's file in a river province was corrected, for a reason that had nothing to do with dragons and was about tool debts in a dyers' trade, and which was, as far as she could tell, ordinary and correct and the reason assessors are given a box and not a paragraph.

---

She took her hand off the shelf and stood in the passage for a while.

Here is the whole of what four hundred printed sheets of a guild's own instruction had done, and it was not a conspiracy and it was not a crime and there was nobody in it to be angry with. A box was drawn in a model form because a trade wanted to know who owed for a bench. A district court's correction put a word into a box that already existed. A man on a rack carried a sheet from an assessor's lane book into a kitchen in Greyfen and put a cup on the corner of it to keep the wind off, and a woman could not read it, and a man whose name was in the box had not been asked.

And the number on the notice was the number of the second instrument in the lane book, because that is what a number is.

A number on a document is not a fact about the document. A number is a fact about the book the document was entered in. If two documents carry the same number, one of them is in the wrong book, and the person who can tell you which book is the person who keeps the books, and the person who keeps the books is a schedule assessor of a dyers' lane in Greyfen with eleven years in a room and a certification to lose and no reason at all to have been in it.

The Caudle notice is not in the Venn series. It does not carry the four substituted lines. It is not the amendment sheet and it is not the base issue and it has nothing on its face that came out of a Chancery corridor or a dragon's office or a broker's bench in a lane off the Kiln road.

It came out of a guild instruction about how to enter a second instrument, and it is four years older than the Venn correction, and it is about something else entirely, and the box in it is a box that instruction created.

---

She wrote it out in her own book on the sixth of the sixth month, in a copyist's hand, with the date and the hour on it, and she read it back four times, and then she took a leaf out and wrote the heading at the top of it twice, because the first heading was a sentence and a sentence is a claim.

The first one she threw in the stove. It began: *This proves that the notice on the Caudle bench came from the same source as the Venn correction.*

The second one is what is in the book:

> *Filed by a roll-keeper of the third gallery, on the sixth of the sixth month, in my own hand, in a book of my own, with no seal, no office and no authority, and sent to a dyers' lane and to a schedule assessor and to nobody else.*
>
> *A number on a document is a fact about the book the document was entered in and not a fact about the document. Two documents in two houses in Greyfen carry the same number. One of them is a second instrument entered in a lane book. The other is a notice. The Guild Assessors' function of the Craft Assessors' Guild states in writing on the fourth of the sixth month that it issues no notice of a person who holds, and that a document entered in a book carries the number of its entry in the book, and that the book in this case is the Greyfen dyers' schedule.*
>
> *The sheet the notice was claimed to belong to was taken from the Forms Received rack in the passage behind this gallery, standing up, and was not removed from the rack and is not in this book and cannot be produced by me. It is indexed by the office that sent it. Series AS/2, second year of issue, being the third year of the sixth year back, run four hundred. It instructs assessors to complete the box headed the person who holds and to enter the filing under the number of the second instrument. It is a guild's own instruction about its own trade. It is four years and four months older than the Venn correction. It is not the Venn series and carries no substituted wording and no public-necessity sentence.*
>
> *Nothing has been produced that connects any two documents. I have looked for it in a corridor for two days and I have not found it and I do not believe it is in the rack, because the rack is indexed by the office that sent a thing and a series does not know what else was filed under the same shelf in a different year by a different person for a different reason.*
>
> *I therefore record a number and a date and a box, and I record that the assessor for that lane holds the book and does not know who filled the box in, because an assessor's office does not keep a hand, and I record that this is a fact about a book and not a finding about anybody.*

She read it back. Then she copied it out twice more, and sent one copy up the road to the lane for the stone in the middle of the trestle, and one copy to Marl Crenn at her own address and not to the guild, with a covering line of four words: *Read by nobody but you.*

And she sent nothing at all to Marek Kest, and she did not send it to him out of caution, and she wrote that down in her own book as well, because she had learned in the twentieth of the eleventh month that a decision not to say a thing is a decision and not a silence, and a decision gets a date and a reason and goes in the book.

> *Not sent to K. It is a finding about a number and it is not yet about a clause, and the day I put a number and a clause in the same sheet to a man in a corridor is the day that sheet is read by two people as a story about a dragon, and the answer we have had for eight months is a Chancery standing office's drawer and a line in a district's flood standing instructions and nothing else, and I will not put my hand to the join. He will hear it when the four questions are asked in Emberwatch in a trade, or he will hear it in a year, or he will not hear it at all, and all three of those are better than being the second document in an envelope.*
>
> *And the true reason, which is worse. Everything in my book now has my name at the foot of it in the sense that my hand is the only hand that made it. The four questions have nobody's name on them, and the copy list has a street in Glasswater and not an office, and the register at Millbrook says on its first page that nothing in it is evidence. In eight months I have built three things that are worth exactly as much as the number of people who could be got at by an officer with a subpena, and I have been proud of that, and I have now written a finding about a guild's own index card that is only any use if somebody is prepared to say in a room that they read it, and there is no room in this that has me in it, and there never has been, and I have spent eight months calling that a virtue.*

She posted the two copies at the seventh hour on the sixth and came back up the stairs and put the ledger open on the frame, and did two hours' ordinary work, and at about the ninth hour she took out four addressed envelopes that were not addressed at all — they were four leaves of her own copy paper with the corners squared — and looked at the drawer for about a minute and did not open it, because the third of the sixth was tomorrow and there had to be a day on which she put them in the post, and a thing done on the day it was decided is not a rule.

Then she went back down the passage and stood in front of the rack again for about four minutes, and the four minutes were spent looking at a card that was not the one she had come for.

*Greyfen Dyers' — Schedule Assessor's Office. Instruction to assessors. Series AS/2.*

She had been copying cards off cards since the twenty-eighth of the twelfth of the ninth year. That was the whole of her method and it had never once failed: take a card off the shelf, copy it, send the copy somewhere a person could act on it, and the action is never hers. A card copied off a card in a corridor at the seventh hour on the sixth of the sixth month is still a card copied off a card in a corridor, and the only thing that has ever made it different is that the person it arrived at understood it.

The note came back on the ninth, in Marl Crenn's own hand, and it was four lines, and the first of them was the whole of it.

> *I have been the standing schedule assessor of that lane since the year before that instruction was issued. I have signed a book out of a drawer eleven times with that in it and I have not read it. I have read it this morning, in my own office, at the second hour, and I have read the fifth line on the back of the exemption form against it on the same page, and I sat down.*
>
> *I am not going to say anything about the fifth line. It is a form with a box on it and I signed it in fear in the tenth month of last year and I have been saying so in my own room since the seventh of the third month and I will say so in Greyfen's own room or not at all.*
>
> *I have written to two of my eleven houses this afternoon. I have not written to the other nine and I have written down why in a book of my own, and the reason is a paragraph I wrote on the seventh of the third month, and the paragraph says that if I am going to say a thing in a room I am going to say the whole of it, and the whole of it includes the sentence about a girl off the vats that I have been reading out loud in a room of a hundred.*
>
> *You did not ask me for this and I have not told the guild and I am not going to. Put it in your book with the date on it. — M.C.*

Tamsin Rook put it in the book with the date on it, on the ninth of the sixth month, in nine words, and then wrote under it, because it was the kind of thing that has to be written under:

> *A card copied off a card in a corridor, to a woman who had never read her own trade's instruction in eleven years, in a copyist's hand, with four words on the cover. That is the entire transmission mechanism of this case and it has no seal on it and no run and no office and it will not be found again in nine years, and it is the only one of the four I would bet a month of copying on.*



The fifth of the sixth, the eleventh hour, at the copying frame in the alcove, three of them went into a packet addressed to a lane in Emberwatch that has no numbers, and the fourth — the one with a district office on the head of it, the one to the standing assessor, the one that had stayed in her hand since the fourth week of the first month — stayed in the drawer, and that is the one still going to matter in nine years.

---

The answer to the first of the three came back on the nineteenth of the sixth month, on the packet, and it was a sheet copied out of a book by a hand that was not a copyist's and had been taught in a schoolroom, and it was the second leaf of the Combination's own book, and there was one line written under the copy in a good clerkly hand that said, without being asked, that the copy was made at the request of a person in a lane in Greyfen who was not a member of the trade and had not been refused.

> *No house in this trade may write to a court, a guild, a factor's office, a district office, or a person it has not dealt with before, about an instrument, without the assessor's leave in writing first. The rule is thirty-one years old. It is at the second leaf of this book. It is the reason we have never had a dispute in this trade. You may read it and you may copy it and you may take it to a person who has a paper. You may not take it to a person who is going to be asked to sign something, because a person who is asked to sign something does not need a rule, they need a witness, and I am the only witness in four hundred miles and I am not going to be turned into a stranger's errand boy by a lane I have never visited.*

Which is the plainest answer anybody had given Tamsin Rook in five months, and which told her the whole of what a trade's own door costs, and which had a second sheet with it, and the second sheet was a list, in the same schoolroom hand, of eleven women in four houses who had each written to her in the last month and not to the lane, and each of whom had said that nobody had ever told her she was allowed to write to anybody.

Nobody in Auremar knew that the four non-answers in a list of forty-one names had become eleven names in a book in a furnace town, and nobody in Auremar was going to hear about it for a month, because the fourth envelope was in a drawer and the drawer was hers and the rule she had made with a man in a coat pocket in the twelfth month of the ninth year said that neither of them moved a thing about this without a name on it, and the drawer was hers alone and had never had his name in it and was not going to get it.
