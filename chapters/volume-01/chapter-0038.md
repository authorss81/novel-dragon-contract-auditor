# Chapter 38: Nobody at the Foot of It

The decision came into the copying alcove on the ninth of the fourth month in a sleeve with a seal on the corner, and it took Tamsin Rook about ninety seconds, and at the end of the ninety seconds she put it face down on the frame and said the thing she had been saying to herself since the eighteenth of the ninth month, which was: *that is the answer of a drawer*.

It is one page. It has a file number on it, and the file number is the one a young clerk of the intake desk wrote in ink and initialled in September, and it has a seal on the corner, and it has the words *Scope: not granted* in the middle of it in a form so plain that Tamsin read the first line and understood the whole of the rest of it before she got to the end.

> *The applicant asked five things. This office answers two of them and does not answer three.*
>
> *The office states that its sheets are issued under standing authority and are not signed by a person, and the applicant is referred to the standing instructions of his own district, which are the authority.*
>
> *The office has no officer competent to state on what authority the amendment sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month was set in type, and no person is at the head of this office, and the applicant is therefore referred, as above, to the standing instructions of his district.*
>
> *The applicant is further informed that he has already been sent, on the ordinary distribution on the fourteenth of the twelfth month, a statement of the terms of a certification offered to him and declined by him, and that the offices of this court have no further statement to make on the subject of the practice, and that the practice is not a matter within the competence of any court.*

No name. Not a refusal — a refusal can be asked about again. Not *unknown*, not *not held*, none of the three words that an office can be asked about twice. A true answer, formally made, sealed, delivered, with nobody at the foot of it, answering two of five questions and referring the other three to a document in a drawer in a different province.

It is the exact sentence Pell Vey dictated to a junior clerk in a gallery on the sixth of the twelfth month, word for word, in the middle of it, because a man who dictated it then had said *I want you to have a thing that is true, that you cannot easily unask, and that will be read by every clerk who ever opens that bay*, and he had been right, and it had taken four months to come back through the building in an envelope with a seal on it.

Tamsin Rook read it four times and then did the thing she had been doing since the twentieth of the twelfth month, which was to write down what the document actually was before she wrote down what she thought about it.

> *This is not a refusal and it is not a lie and it cannot be proved wrong.*
>
> *A lie can be disproved. That is the only advantage a lie has, and it is a real one, and it is the whole of what an honest person in a bad position has got. A true answer that nobody signed cannot be disproved, cannot be refused, cannot be appealed, and cannot be asked about a second time, because there is nobody to ask. The machine does not have to be cruel. It does not have to be careless. It only has to be issued, and this is the proof that it does not need to be either.*
>
> *A finding that nobody is answerable for is not a rule. It is forty-six people each doing their best in a hurry, which is the thing we refused to certify in the eleventh month and were right to refuse.*
>
> *And the answer to the third question is that there is nobody to name, and the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth month asked to be answered by a person whose name is written at the foot of the answer rather than at the end of a date, and we got a seal, and the seal is standing in for the name, and a seal is not a person and cannot be embarrassed.*

Then she went and got her coat.

---

Before the coat there was the review, which had happened on the fourth of the month, two days after a dragon had sworn a statement in the river courtroom that the court had entered and could not use.

The magistrate had read Sarn's wall aloud in her own words and had not improved it, and had read the third paragraph twice, which was the one with the four people who said they did not understand and the eleven who said they did and then signed something else inside a month. And then the court had said in one sentence that a review is not a precedent, is not a rule, compels nobody, is not binding on any district including this one, and that this district may or may not run the process again next season and will decide that itself, and that the review was over at the first hour of the afternoon.

Which meant that the whole of the batch's work — the four-box form, the witness order, the hearing slate, the cost on the wall, and a sworn statement in scale form with a hole in it — added up, on the fourth of the fourth month, to a room in which a clerk and a magistrate had said out loud that it was a thing they had tried, in a place that had paid for it, and were not going to tell anybody else to do.

Tamsin had been in that room and had written it down and had felt, at the time, the specific satisfaction of a person whose method has been publicly described as insufficient. It had lasted about a minute and then it had turned into the thing she was writing on the ninth.

---

She took the decision up to the third gallery at the fourth hour and put it on Pell Vey's desk, face up, with her finger on the second paragraph.

"Did you know this sentence would come back."

"No."

"You dictated it on the sixth of the twelfth month, in the third gallery, to a junior clerk, in about nine seconds, and I wrote it down in that alcove as I wrote it down, and it is the middle of the second paragraph of a sealed answer that came into this building on Monday morning."

Pell Vey read it. He read it twice. He put his hand flat on the corner of the desk, which is a thing he does, and then he took it off again.

"You have made a mistake and I am going to correct it and then I am going to let the correction sit there, and the correction is this. I dictated a sentence for you to put into a reply, and the sentence was true, and I said it to a junior clerk because a junior clerk was going to send the reply and a junior clerk's reply is a document in an office's file and could be read by a man in a corridor in six months. I have never seen this page. I do not know who read it. I have signed nothing this year that is not in that folder." He turned the file round. "You will notice that I have not asked you what you are going to do about it."

"You have not."

"I want to be careful with you," said Pell Vey, "because you have a piece of paper of mine in your own book with a date on it, and I have not asked for it, and I have known about it since the sixth of the twelfth, and I have let you keep it, which is the single worst supervisory act of my career and I would do it again."

Tamsin Rook stood in the third gallery of the Roll Hall with a one-page decision on a desk and understood the whole of the machine in about four seconds, and the whole of it was: the man who taught her how to write an unanswerable answer is not the man who sent it, and the man who sent it does not know that his own sentence is being used, and the sentence is a sentence he believes in, and a thing that is true and believed and issued travels four hundred miles on its own and does not need anybody's permission at any stage after the first one.

That is why the wire between the two of them does not exist and cannot be drawn.

"Nothing," she said. "I am going to do nothing about it for another day, and then I am going to do something about it that is very small and is in my own name, and I am not going to tell you what it is until it is in the file, because if I tell you first then a person with a name on a piece of paper in this building is behind it, and the whole point of what I am going to write is that there is not."

"Write it," said Pell Vey, and went back to his bench, and did not ask.

---

The objection was in the inside pocket of Marek Kest's coat, folded once, with the corner of it turned over, and it had been there a hundred and sixteen days.

"I have come for this," said Tamsin Rook. "Not today. This afternoon. In five minutes, in this alcove, and you do not get to be surprised about it."

"You said you would collect it before I wrote anything else about a dragon seat's paperwork."

"I have said a great many things in this alcove and I have been wrong about some of them." She took the coat off the hook and turned the pocket out and the leaf came out folded once, in four lines, in her own hand, dated the thirteenth of the twelfth month, and it was not as faded as she had expected and it was not as damning as she had hoped. "Read it."

Marek Kest read it.

> *The first document carries the words and is a Chancery standing office's. The second document does not carry the words and is a dragon seat's. They are two offices, two buildings, four hundred miles apart, and there is no paper in either of them that mentions the other, and only two people in this empire know that both exist.*
>
> *You have left the second one out of the reply of the thirteenth. I think you are right about the envelope. I think you are wrong that it can be left out for ever.*
>
> *A reader of the reply in a district is going to be told that a sentence came out of a court and got into a form series, and there is no question asked and no answer expected. In about two years somebody is going to work out that the reason the honest answer is small is that something is counting for them, and they are going to be right, and there is going to be no document to put in front of them.*
>
> *If the second document is named, once, in a file that somebody in this building will be annoyed about, then in two years there will be a record that both halves exist and that only one of them was written about. A missing document is a hole. A document that was deliberately not named is a decision, and decisions can be asked about by people who are not frightened of offices.*
>
> *I am not asking you to put it in the reply. The reply has gone. I am asking you to put it on the record in this building, where the two of us can be wrong about it together, and I am asking you to do it before the fourth month is out, because in the fourth month the answer to the nine paragraphs arrives and after that every day you wait is a day that somebody could say the two of us sat on it.*

He read it twice.

"You are not going to get it," he said.

"I know I am not going to get it. I have known that since I put it in your pocket and I have spent a hundred and sixteen days waiting for you to ask me the argument in a room, in words, with a date on it, and you have not asked once, and I have let you not ask, and that is the second thing of mine that has come out of this coat's pocket after the copy of the reply." She folded the leaf back. "I am taking it back because I have a better place for it and I want you to know that I have known about a better place for it for about a month and I have not used it, and I want that written in your book as well as in mine."

"What is the better place."

"An answer that is true, sealed, and signed by nobody, which arrived in this alcove on the ninth of the fourth month, and which answers two of five questions and refers the other three to a drawer in another province. If I file anything about the second document now, in the fourth month, on the day an unanswerable answer has landed, then there is a sheet in this file that mentions a dragon seat's paperwork, and from that day forward every clerk who opens this file reads the two documents as one thing, and the first one stops being a Chancery standing office's and becomes part of a story about a dragon, and the whole answer we found in a corridor in the twelfth month comes apart on the page where it is strongest."

"Which is the worst reason in the world."

"It is exactly the worst reason in the world and I have had a hundred and sixteen days to look for a better one and there is not one." She put the leaf into her own book, between two pages of the Millbrook account, and shut it. "So I am keeping it in a book of my own with a woman's name in a surety line in it, on a page about a cooper's gauge, and on the day that is not good enough I will move it, and I will tell you the day I move it, and it will not be the fourth of a month."

She sat down at the frame and took a fresh leaf out of the pile and squared it to the edge of the board, and Marek Kest understood that she was about to do the thing that nobody in this building had done in nine provinces, and that she was going to do it on her own authority, and that no office in the building was going to hear about it until it was in the file.

---

She wrote it in four lines. She read it back twice. She did not soften it once, and she was not proud of how easy that was.

> *Received in this file on the ninth of the fourth month: a decision on the scope of the request of the eighteenth of the ninth month. It is sealed. It is true. It does not answer three of the five questions put, and it refers them to a document held in another province.*
>
> *I am a roll-keeper of this building. I have no standing, no office, and no authority in this matter, and I am asking in my own name and not on behalf of the applicant, and I am asking on the ground that a copyist is the only person in this room with nothing to protect.*
>
> *The first of the two provincial filings was entered by the district court at Glasswater as entry ninety-one. I name that district and not the office, because a district has a clerk and the office has no person, and a question put to a clerk can be answered by somebody who has to answer it, and a question put to a seal is answered by a drawer.*
>
> *I ask that district to state in writing, by a date, what its standing instructions say about the itemising of a filing made under flood authority, and I will pay a shilling for the copy and a shilling for the letter, and I do not ask that district to agree with me about anything.*

She signed it at the foot: *Tamsin Rook, roll-keeper, third gallery.* No office above it. No seal. No series and no number, because a document in an office's file that the office can find again is a document in an office's file, and this one was going to have to be found by somebody who wanted to find it.

Then she read it back twice, which is the rule, and then she did the thing that the rule was for, which is that on the second reading she heard the third line and did not like it and wrote it a fourth time before it was better.

*...because a district has a clerk and the office has no person, and a question put to a clerk can be answered by somebody who has to answer it, and a question put to a seal is answered by a drawer.*

On the fourth time it was: *...and I would rather be refused by a person in Glasswater than answered by a drawer in this building.*

That is worse for her. She kept it.

---

The list of the fourth month came out of the intake tray on the twelfth, folded in three, in a clerk's hand, because the fourth month is when the district's own business is what is left, and it had eleven days on it and about thirty names, and Tamsin read it standing up at the frame because it was not her list and she was not going to sit down to read somebody else's.

Tavi Venn, canal examination, to be arranged and paid, gated on a decision. Orra Dunn, review called and answered, second hearing to be dated. Ilsabet Kell, standing, in the fifth month, at a cost nobody can protect her from. Anselm Grice, first week of the fourth month, passed, and not bound, and not stopped. The Greyfen assessment, the twenty-third. A levy, four houses, one farthing. Twenty-three houses to be walked and no coach. Nery Caudle, a second paper, under a cloth since the second week of the twelfth, now out.

Oster and Son, Millbrook Reach: a register read aloud in a shop on the twenty-fourth of the third month, in the presence of a factor's man.

And at the bottom, in a different ink, in a different clerk's hand, because it had come up from the district on the morning post and had been added: a request for a witness order to be posted to a furnace town four hundred and forty miles away, sent through the standing assessor of a dyers' combination at the applicant's own cost, and the docket number of the order that had come back signed.

Two of the names on that list had been in Tamsin Rook's own book for a month and one of them had been in it in a cooper's handwriting on the eleventh of the second month, and neither of them knew there was a list, and one of them was going to find out in about a fortnight from a woman who could not read.
