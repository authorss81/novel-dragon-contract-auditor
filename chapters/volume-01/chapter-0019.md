# Chapter 19: Itemised

The copying alcove in the third gallery of the Roll Hall had eleven frames in it and nine of them were working, and on the third of the eleventh month Tamsin Rook had cleared the two nearest the window and put the two letters on the boards with weights on them, side by side, and had been looking at them for an hour and ten minutes before Marek Kest came down the gallery at the second hour of the afternoon and found her doing nothing at all.

"You have had those since the second."

"Since the second, this morning," said Tamsin.

"And you have not said."

"I have been going to say it four times since you came in and I have got as far as your coat twice." She took her hand off the left-hand board. "I have had a witness in a room in this building for two days who came up the river on the first and has been sitting in the alcove with the fire since because I would not let her go back down in November with what she is carrying. So you are going to hear the letters first and then you are going to hear who is upstairs, and I am not going to be a woman who has a witness and forgets to say so."

The letters were on two different papers. That was the first thing, and it took Tamsin about four minutes to explain and it was the thing that made her put them down and stand back from them.

---

The left-hand one was four sheets, written on a river court's copying paper with the counterfoil edge still on it, and it came off the packet boat at the Mosswake landing on the thirtieth of the tenth month addressed to *the reading table, Venn lane, and anybody there who keeps copies*.

The right-hand one was a single sheet, folded twice, on paper that was not a copying paper at all but a good cream laid stock, and it was the only piece of expensive paper Marek had seen in this building in six weeks, and it was addressed to *whoever is doing this*, and there was no name at the foot of it, and there was no letter, and there was nothing written on it except a copy.

"A copy of what," Marek said.

"Read it."

It was an endorsement. Four lines, and then a filing note in a different hand, and then a number. It was a copy because it was a copy — Marek could see the copyist's tick in the left margin, the small one, the one that meant *this is a true copy of a sheet in a book and the sheet is at the address in the filing note*.

The four lines read:

> *being a work of the ground and not of a person, shall be held against [ ], the present holder answering for the same until full review, the named signer having ceased.*

"There is a blank in it," Marek said.

"There is a blank in it because the thing is a form," said Tamsin. "That is not my sentence. Read the other one."

---

The other one had the same four lines, filled in, and beneath them, in a clear upright hand, a fifth thing that Marek had read in a courtroom seven weeks ago and had heard read aloud in a lane on four days running and had copied into nine books himself.

> *Where immediate public necessity requires a recorded continuation to prevent loss of food or shelter, the present holder of the burdened property may be joined as guarantor until full review.*

Neither of them said anything for a while. A frame went round in the gallery behind them, and somebody's hand asked for the second series of the ninth and was answered.

"Seven weeks," said Marek.

"Seven weeks and three days," said Tamsin. "The ruling was made at twenty past three on the twelfth of the ninth month. This is the third of the eleventh. And in between, that sentence has been written into a copy of a document in a town four hundred and forty miles downriver by somebody who has never been in this room and has never heard the name of the magistrate who said it."

"It is not the same words."

"It is the same words in the same order," said Tamsin. "Word for word, including *the present holder*, and including *until full review*, and including *the named signer having ceased*, which is the part I would have bet a year of my life nobody outside four provincial clerks had ever read, because it is a dead phrase, it is not English any more, it is thirty years old, and it is the phrase that has made it from a river court to a furnace town in seven weeks in a sheet of a book."

---

She had done the work before he arrived and she had done it properly, which meant she had done it in an order he would not have chosen and would not have improved on.

She had begun with the paper. The Glasswater letter was a river court's copying paper and its counterfoil edge was still on it and the counterfoil had been torn against the stub, which meant it had been pulled out of a book and not cut out of a pad, and it had come out of a book in a court where books are kept and not out of a bundle in a lane.

Then she had done the hand. The four lines and the sentence at the head of the Glasswater sheets were in the hand of a copyist and not the hand of a clerk, and it was a good hand, a fast one, the kind that gets a column finished in a morning, and there was a true-copy tick in the left margin.

"A true copy of a sheet in a book," Marek said.

"A true copy of a sheet in a book, and the book is a filing book, and a filing book gives an entry a number of its own when a person has bothered to enter it one at a time." Tamsin turned the sheets round. "Glasswater, the tenth month of this year, and it is the ninety-first entry of that month, and it is in the individual column and not the routine column, and it is the thing I have been sitting with for an hour and I still do not know what to do with."

"Tell me."

"It is itemised."

The word hung in the alcove.

"The filing is itemised," Tamsin said. "It has a number in the entry column, and a line to itself, and the line says a flood-season continuity was accepted at that landing on that date, and somebody in a Glasswater river court sat down and wrote that line on purpose, one at a time, in the ordinary course of an ordinary morning."

Marek put both hands flat on the board.

"Say that again."

"A flood-season continuity filing in the tenth month, entered individually, in a court four hundred miles from the one that wrote the sentence, in a book that is itemised by hand." Tamsin's voice did not rise. "Which means the form failed. That is what it means. That is the only thing it means, and there are two possible reasons and I do not know which one it is, and the difference between them is the whole of what happens next."

"Spell them out."

"Either the form never reached that court, or it did reach it and somebody in that court read it and decided not to obey it." She put a finger on the sheet. "And a person who has read a permission and refused it is a completely different thing from a court that was never sent the permission, and the second one can be fixed by a letter, and the first one cannot be fixed by anything at all, and I cannot tell you which one is on this sheet, and I have been sitting here since the second trying to make the paper say."

---

The Emberwatch copy had been itemised too, which was the second surprise, and the surprise was the good kind and nobody was allowed to enjoy it.

It had been itemised, and it had been given a number, and beneath the number somebody had written a word and underlined it.

*Itemised.*

"Not a note to self," Tamsin said. "A clerk does not underline a word in a filing book to remind himself. You underline it when you are writing for the next person, and the next person in a filing book is a reviewer, and the reviewer is the quarterly one."

"So somebody at Emberwatch is expecting this to be looked at."

"Somebody at Emberwatch looked at this, itemised it, wrote down that they had itemised it, and then sent a copy four hundred miles to a lane in a province they have never visited, on good paper, unsigned." Tamsin looked at the sheet. "Which is not what a frightened clerk does. A frightened clerk burns it. A frightened clerk does nothing. That is a person who has worked out what the thing is and cannot get anybody to listen, and has decided that the only way anybody will ever look at it is if it turns up in the place where everybody is already looking."

"Or it is somebody who cannot sign."

"Or that," Tamsin said. "And I have no way to tell, and I have looked at the paper for an hour looking for a man's hand, because a hand is a person, and I have got two hands on this table and neither of them is the one that wrote it."

And then she said the thing that had been sitting under the paper the whole time, and said it flatly, and did not look at him while she said it.

"The instrument is a craft one. It is a house of dyers. The filing note names the house and the trade and the term of an apprentice taken in the eighth month, and the four lines are on that instrument, and there is a second instrument in that house, and the second instrument names a person." She put her hand flat on the board. "Marek. A dyers' exemption in this empire survives a second instrument in the house only if that instrument names a person and the first instrument is read with it. And the first instrument in that house is a court ruling with four lines on it, and the second instrument has been entered on a craft term in a furnace town, and neither of the two people in this room can be told that from a document, and I have been able to tell it from a document since the second of October because a woman in Greyfen read the same form to me in a workroom and made me understand it."

---

The Glasswater household was the one on the left, and it took eleven minutes, and it was the reason Tamsin had not been able to put the letters down.

The instrument was a landing and a delta cut at Glasswater, in the name of a sole signatory, and the file was four years old, and the summary prepared in the ordinary way in the third month of this year carried the four lines, and it was in the spring that her husband read a copy of that summary at the top of the landing in daylight and could not read again afterwards, and it was in the tenth month that an endorsement was entered on the same file carrying the four lines *and* the sentence, and the surety line in that endorsement named a person of sixteen who was a member of the household and had signed nothing and had never been asked.

That was the third file since the ninth of the eighth month, and it was the same fact each time, and it was no longer surprising, and that was the part that frightened her.

The holder named was not the family. The holder named was a man who had taken the cut on a three-year holding two years ago, on a paper, from a man who had held it before him, and the paper the new holder signed was the paper that carried the four lines into his hands without anybody noticing, and he was the *present holder* now, and he was not related to anybody in that file, and he had signed a three-year holding in the ordinary way in a room with a fire in it.

"He has been told the cut is security," Tamsin said. "There is a filing note in the Glasswater book. A miller has asked for the answer to be: the cut is not security. And the miller has written, in the fourth month, to a factor in Greyfen, and the Greyfen factor is Osric Lave, who is the same factor that wrote to this court about the Dunn barn in the ninth month, so the two ends of it are talking to the same man, and whether he started it or picked it up I do not know and I have written the direction of travel on the back of the sheet in pencil because I do not want it in the front where it looks like a conclusion."

Marek read the pencil line.

"Write it again in ink and give it to somebody who is not us," he said.

"That is the first sensible thing either of us has done in an hour," said Tamsin, "and I wrote to Mosswake yesterday about it, and Sarn will put it in the minute book the day the application lands, and it will be a document, and if Lave is running on both sides of the country then a clerk with a minute book is the thing that is going to find it out, and it will not be us and it will not be in a lane."

---

Then she told him about the woman in the alcove, and it took ninety seconds, and Marek did not interrupt once.

Perry Ostrow, thirty-five, of the copying room of a Glasswater river court. She had put the four sheets in the packet and sent them to the lane on the twenty-ninth of the tenth month because somebody had told her in a court yard in the ninth month that there was a lane where a ruling had been read out loud four times.

She had told nobody at home. Not her supervisor, not the clerk of the filings, not the man who kept the keys. She had told nobody because she was the copyist who wrote the endorsement into the filing book on the third of the tenth month, and she had known when she did it that she was the reason there was a number in the entry column, and she had then gone on copying for a fortnight.

Her name was at the foot of the left-hand letter. *P. Ostrow, copying room, Glasswater.*

"She was not going to give her name to me either," Tamsin said. "She gave it to me on the second night in about four minutes, and she gave it because I told her what I did, which was write to Mosswake on the first and ask a clerk to enter her letter in the record with her name on it and write back and tell her, in writing, whether it had been entered, and she said that a woman who will not give her name is doing one thing and a woman who gives it and then finds out what happened to it is doing a different thing, and she chose the second one in four minutes, and I have not written about it in a book yet because I wanted you to hear it first."

"Where is she now."

"Upstairs, by the fire, in the copyist's room, with my spare blanket, and she has been there two days, and she is not a prisoner and she is not a guest and she is a person who has left her court without telling her court anything and has nowhere at all to go until the end of the week." Tamsin looked at the two boards. "And her husband is blind. He has been since the spring, when he read that summary in daylight at the top of the delta landing, and when I asked her about it she said that was a different thing and she was not going to be asked about it here, and she said the same words to Ivet Sarn at a window in Mosswake a month ago, which means she has been putting the same sentence down in front of two clerks in two provinces and neither of them pushed, and I would like that recorded as a thing about the two clerks and not about her."

---

The rest of it was the part Tamsin had been carrying since the second, and she said it standing up, with the boards behind her, and she said it to Marek and not to the papers.

"I can get them in."

"Get what in where."

"The two provincial files into the Venn matter at Mosswake, as exhibits." She had gone over it twice on the walk down from the river that morning and both times she had thrown it away and started again, and what came out of her now was the third version. "Not into your request, which is in Examinations and which nobody will read until somebody decides to. Into the Venn matter, which has a docket, which has a date, and which has a person in it whose holding is preserved until the second month. That is the ground. Tavi Venn is a person named in a ruling of that court, and on the fourth month's docket there is a question about what happens to her at the end of a term, and these two files are evidence about the reach of the sentence the court itself stated when it preserved her. That is not a trick of law. That is the ordinary use of an exhibit."

"That is true."

"It is true, and it is a stretch, and Rell will strike it in a week if I put it to her in those words." Tamsin's hands were flat on the board. "So I am not putting it to her in those words. I am going to make a written application to the clerk of that court for the admission of two documents as exhibits in a matter of record, on the ground stated, in the ordinary form, with the number of the exhibit and the number of the page and the address of the copy. And the clerk of a district court may admit an exhibit on her own authority. She does not need the magistrate. She has never needed the magistrate, and it is the oldest power in that room, and it is a power nobody uses because admitting an exhibit is not a thing anybody wants to do at nine in the morning."

"And the magistrate strikes it the moment she is told."

"Or in a fortnight, or in a month, and it does not matter, because of the second thing." Tamsin lifted the corner of the board. "An exhibit admitted to a file in that court is copied to the parties on the day of admission. The copy is made the same day. It goes out the same day, because the court's practice is that nothing waits for a week, and I know it is copied out the same day because I have watched the copying room do it for three years."

"Sarn can strike it. She cannot un-send the copies."

"She can strike the exhibit from the file and the order will say *not to be used*, and that is an order about the use. It is not an order that a copy which has gone to a party was never made." Tamsin let the board down. "There will be four copies of two provincial files in four buildings by the fifth, and one of them is in this gallery, and I have put it in your hands to be the one that keeps them, and I have not had to lie to anybody at all, which is the part I have been sitting with since the second. The whole of it is that a copy that has gone out cannot be recalled, and that is not a loophole. That is a filing system working exactly as it was built to work, by people who are not dishonest, for the benefit of a clerk who needs a paper in a court four hundred miles away."

"You are going to do it today."

"I have done the form. I have it in my book. I have not sent it, because it is made in your name and it needs your hand and your signature, and a roll-keeper of this court cannot apply in the name of the applicant, and I would have had to invent a reason why she could, and I was not going to invent one on a day like this."

Marek took out the reed and read the form, all of it, twice, in the way he read everything, and it took about four minutes, and at the third minute he put his finger on a sentence near the foot.

"This one."

"That one is mine and it is a stretch and I know it is a stretch."

"*In the absence of any contrary direction the court will treat the same sentence as a live question in any matter in which its reach is raised.*"

"Yes."

"That is asking the court to do it again in another matter, in advance, without a hearing, and the last time it did that to a sentence on the twelfth of the ninth month it put a child's name in a surety line in the same minute."

"I have been turning that sentence over since I wrote it," Tamsin Rook said, "and I have not been able to take it out, and I would like you to take it out, and I want you to know that I put it in because I wanted there to be a version where a district court could not do that again without somebody noticing, and I know exactly what I am asking you to refuse, and I have decided to ask you for it in a room instead of in a letter, so that you can say no to my face."

Marek struck the sentence out, and signed the form, and dated it the third, and gave it back to her.

"Send it tonight," he said. "And write to Sarn and tell her what it is, before it arrives, so that she is not reading it cold with a magistrate standing behind her."

"I wrote to Sarn yesterday," said Tamsin. "It went up the river yesterday afternoon with the first of the two letters in a second envelope, and that is the part I have not told you yet, and it is why I did not sleep."

"You enclosed the Glasswater file in a letter to a district clerk without asking me."

"I did."

"Why."

"Because it went down the river in an envelope yesterday, and if I had waited for you it would have gone on Thursday, and the fourth month's docket preparation begins on the sixth, and a letter from a roll-keeper that arrives in the gap is a letter that gets read and a letter that arrives after it is a letter that waits in a tray." Tamsin took the book off the frame and shut it. "I have made my decision and I have made it wrongly on purpose, in one thing, so that the other four things I decided this week would not look like a woman who cannot be trusted with a pen. Write it in your book. I would rather it was written down by you than be forgiven by you."

Marek wrote it in his book, in the ordinary way, with the date, and read it back, and the rule they had both been working under without ever having made it was a rule that had been made in this room in the first week of the month by two people who had not noticed making it, and it was the first time in seven weeks that something in this case had not been decided by one of them alone.

---

She was frightened and she said it out loud, and that was the part of the day she had not planned.

It was at the seventh hour, after the form had gone down to the river post and the alcove had emptied and the boards were bare, and Marek was putting his coat on with the reed still in his hand.

"Marek."

"Yes."

"I am frightened."

He stopped, because he had not expected it, and because she had said it the way a person says a thing they have decided to say exactly once.

"I have been frightened since the second and I have been getting better at it," said Tamsin. "I have a book, and a rule about what goes in the book, and I know what a copyist is for, and today I have put two provincial files into a district court in a form I wrote at a table, and if I am right then in about six weeks a dyers' house in a furnace town four hundred miles away has somebody in it who has read the line under the four, and if I am wrong then I have taken two people's private papers out of their province and put them in four buildings on the strength of a sentence a magistrate said in a hurry while the water was coming up the steps of her own court."

"The sentence was right."

"The sentence was right and I have known that for seven weeks and it has not helped me once this month." She put her hands flat on the frame in front of her. "That is what I am frightened of. Not the letter. This is bigger than a widow, Marek. It is bigger than a lane. It has got a mark on it that is in thirty-eight lines of a man's own book and it has got a form with a run on it and it has got a district I have never walked in, and I am a copyist of twenty-four with a book and a stool, and nobody in this building has the power to make anybody in Emberwatch do anything, and everybody in this building that I have spoken to in seven weeks has been careful, and careful is what we have, and careful did not stop a fifteen-year-old getting into a surety line, and I do not know what careful is *for*."

He did not tell her she was being dramatic, and he did not tell her she was the reason the copies existed, and he did not tell her that the shape of the thing was the shape he had been walking into for seven weeks, because she knew all three of those and would have taken them as comfort.

"You are not a copyist who is out of her depth," Marek said. "You are a copyist who has found out that the depth is not a thing that can be got to the bottom of, and that is a different thing, and it is the only difference there is, and I have been in it eleven days longer than you and I have not found anything else."

"Write that down."

"I have."

"You have not."

"I have not," he admitted, "and I am not going to, and I will tell you why: because it is the kind of thing a man writes in a book about himself, and I have had four of those in seven weeks and every one of them was a way of not being in the room." He put his coat on. "Send the second letter down with the register and tell the lane that if anybody writes back to it they are to write to Sarn and not to the lane, and get the woman upstairs onto the morning boat with a letter from this office in her hand saying she has been received and entered, because if she goes down that river as a woman who left a court without leave then she is a woman who left a court without leave, and if she goes down as a woman whose exhibit has been admitted she is a woman who was summoned, and it is the same walk and only one of the two is true."

---

The form went down the river on the third and was received at the Mosswake clerk's window on the fourth, and Ivet Sarn admitted the two documents on the fourth, on her own authority, in the ordinary form, in about four minutes, and the copies went out on the fourth, in the afternoon, with the afternoon post.

Nobody knew for certain then that it would hold. It was not supposed to hold. It was a filing clerk doing a clerk's power on a day she had chosen herself because a thing had been sent to her the day before, and a thing that has been copied out cannot be un-copied, and about four hundred miles away there were two documents in two books that somebody was going to have to look at now, whether or not anybody in this building ever got to see them.

And on the fifth of the eleventh, in the copying alcove, with the boards bare and the frames going round again, Tamsin Rook was going through the left-hand letter for the ninth time because she had not believed she had read all of it, and she put the Emberwatch copy beside it, and the two four-line sets lay side by side on the boards with the weights on them, and she read them both again, and then she read them both a third time without taking the weights off, and then she said something out loud in an empty gallery, which she was in the habit of doing and which nobody in the building had ever once said anything about.

Neither of them was written the way a court writes a thing.

The Venn correction was written the way a court writes a thing, badly, in a hurry, by a hand that was copying a general paper to fit a particular one, and it read like a court because it was full of *notwithstanding* and *save as aforesaid* and had a semicolon in it. The Dunn summary was written the way a court writes a thing and read like a clerk tidying up a sentence that had been written in a hurry.

These two were not written like that. They were short. They were flat. They had no semicolons and no *notwithstanding* and no *save as aforesaid*, and the words ran in the order that a person runs them when they are setting down in a box what is supposed to go in the box.

"Four shillings for a notch," she said. "And these two have been written in exactly the words somebody would write them in if they were being set into a box that about five hundred copies were going to be printed from, and I have been in this building three years and I have never once seen a document that was set."

Marek came and read it over her shoulder, and then he read the Emberwatch one again, and the Emberwatch one had been written by a different hand and in a different province and it was in the same words.

"Nobody in Auremar has ever seen the document that says so," he said.

"No," said Tamsin Rook. "And that is the difference between this and everything that has happened since the seventh of the ninth month. Everything else has been a thing that was somewhere, being copied by hand by somebody who could be caught. This is a thing that was *set*. And you cannot catch a thing that was set. You can only find out who set it, and where it is set, and whether anybody in this empire is obliged to tell us."
