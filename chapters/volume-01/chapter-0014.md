# Chapter 14: Four Days

Ivet Sarn had kept the Mosswake flood docket for nine years and in that time she had made exactly two decisions that she would defend out loud, and both of them were the same decision, and both of them had cost a household she could name.

The first was in the eighth month of the fifth year, when a clerk two desks down from her wanted to reopen a file and she told him it would cost eleven hundred households four months of eating, which was true, and she was the clerk who signed the refusal, and she has never in nine years told herself that the refusal was the only available answer. It was the answer she had.

The second was four days long and it had a name on it and she had never said the name out loud to a person.

She had a paper about it. It was in the second drawer of the desk in her own two rooms over the bakehouse, folded in four, and it was not a copy of anything and it did not have a name on the outside, and she had looked at it perhaps thirty times in six years and had never once taken it to anybody.

On the thirtieth of the ninth month the register was a week old and it had already been used by a person she had never met, and the person had come down the lane in the rain on the third day and stood at the window with a bundle on her knees and would not give her name, and that had made the paper in the drawer heavier.

---

The woman in the bundle coat came back on the morning of the thirtieth and Sarn let her stand at the window because the queue had gone and because the window had a sill you could lean on.

"You are the one who started the slate," the woman said.

"Yes."

"My name is not going in it."

"Then it does not go in it," said Sarn, "and I am not going to ask you for it, and I am going to tell you that I am going to look it up."

The woman did not move.

"You are going to look it up."

"Everything that comes through this window is in this building," Sarn said. "Not in my name. In the building's. It has been in this building for nine years and I have indexed all of it and I can put a hand on any file in this district that anybody has ever brought up this lane, and if I do not look it up then I have decided that it is better for you to be unknown to me than for me to be a clerk who cannot find things, and I do not get to make that choice for you and call it a kindness."

"You have not asked me a single question about myself."

"No."

"That is not what I asked for."

"It is what I am able to give you at this window," Sarn said, "and if you want the rest of it you will have to stand there while I look, and you can go at any point and I will not follow you, and if you go I will write *offered and declined* in the margin with the hour, because that is also a thing somebody might need to know about later."

The woman stayed.

Sarn turned round and took the fourth register of the fifth year off the shelf, which is not a thing she does lightly, because taking a register off the shelf in front of a member of the public is the oldest trick there is for making a person feel watched, and she did it facing the window with her back to the room, deliberately, so that the woman could not see the spine.

The bundle was a sacking wrapper with a tie on it and a wax string, and inside it were four sheets of a Mosswake Roll Court file, and they were copies, made by a hand that was not a clerk's hand, or rather made by a clerk's hand in a hurry, with the pen lifted in three places.

The first was a summary of a seed-barn term instrument. The second was a filing endorsement. The third was a district water office memorandum with a number in the corner and a date in the eighth month of the year four years back.

The fourth was four lines long, and Sarn read it standing up, and then she read it again, and then she put her hand flat on the sill and kept it there.

*...being a work of the ground and not of a person, shall be held against the barn, the present holder answering for the same until full review, the named signer having ceased...*

"That is not possible," said Sarn, in a voice she did not recognise.

---

She did not say anything for about a minute. The queue had gone. The boy from the copying room came past twice and went past twice again.

"Madam," she said. "I am going to ask you three questions and I am going to ask them in the order that I can answer the last one."

"Ask."

"Have you had this bundle copied anywhere else."

"No."

"Has anybody else read it."

"My husband read it, in the spring. He has not been able to read it since the spring and that is a different thing and I am not going to be asked about it here." The woman said it evenly. "Are you going to tell me whose it is."

"Not from the window," Sarn said. "I have told you that I will look it up and I am going to look it up, and I have looked it up, and I am not going to say the name at this window to a person who has come four valleys in the rain to be unknown. I am going to write to the address on the endorsement, and I am going to ask her whether she will come, and I am going to put your refusal in the book either way with the hour on it, and if she comes, she comes to this building and not to your door, and I will not be in that room unless you want me to be."

The woman looked at the slate on the nail for a while.

"I want you to write to her," she said, "and I want the four lines in your book, and I want the name of whoever put them in that file, and I do not think you have got it."

"No," said Sarn. "I have not got it. But I have got the file, and I have got a date on it, and I have got about forty other files with a date in that month on them, and I have got a man in Greyfen with a paper on a floor and a fund in this building with a portfolio, and I have not got anything at all that I can put in front of you, and I am not going to stand at this window and pretend to a stranger that I have."

The woman put the bundle back into the sacking and tied it and went up the lane, and Sarn wrote *offered and declined* in the margin with the hour and initialled it, and then she sat down at her own desk, in a building with a register on a nail and a queue she had sent away, and she wrote *offered and declined* on a sheet of paper as well, and put the sheet in her coat, and went down the corridor to the magistrate's anteroom to ask whether the magistrate would see her for twenty minutes about something that was four years old and was not her business.

---

Marek Kest was already in the anteroom.

That was the part of the morning that Sarn would think about afterwards, because it told her that somebody had been in the building since before six, and that somebody had not slept much, and that somebody had come to her office before she got there and had asked after her and had been told which room she was in.

"Clerk Kest."

"Clerk Sarn."

"I am not here about the file."

"Then I am glad, because I have not eaten and you look like a person who has eaten."

"I am here," Sarn said, "because I am going to put something in the record about the fifth year, and I would like you to be in the building when I do it, and I would like you to do one thing for me, and it is a small thing and you are not going to like it."

"Go on."

"I want you to sit down and not say anything for about twenty minutes."

Marek Kest, who had a headache behind his left eye and an ash taste behind his teeth and had been in this province for twelve days discovering that the correct procedure had a cost attached to it in a different household every time, looked at her for a moment.

"You are asking me not to protect you," he said.

"I am asking you to watch me not protect myself, which is not the same and is harder."

---

She had the paper out of the drawer in her own rooms, and she had brought it in her coat, folded in four, and it was not a copy of anything.

It was the fourth month's relief tally of the fifth year, in the ordinary hand of a clerk who had done four hundred and sixty lines in a fortnight, and against the entry for the upper district there was a short line in a different ink, in Sarn's own hand, in a hand she had written at nineteen and could still read.

*Seed allocation, upper district, held on the fourth day, docket open. File: Sallow, R., barn term, upper. Reopened by me. Held. R. Sallow came to the window twice on the fourth day and the third and was told the docket was not closed. Certification delayed. Told him so at the window. Did not tell him why.*

Four lines. It was the only place in nine years in which she had written down the number of days, and she had written it on a tally, which is a working document, and which went into the district file, and which is therefore not a confession at all, and is the only reason she had been able to keep it in a drawer for six years without being able to lose it.

"The third and the fourth month of the fifth year," Sarn said. "A file came up from the upper district. A barn term. It had been shut for two years, which is a long time for a term file, and the clerk who brought it up said it had come up because the holder had died and somebody wanted the floor. I opened it on the first day. I want you to have the shape of it before I have the detail: the file came up, and I did not close it in a day."

"How long."

"Four days," said Sarn. "I have got the number in your hand, because I gave it to a copyist in a yard in this building in the eighth month of this year and she wrote it on a board, and I have heard about it three times since from people who assumed it was a figure of speech."

"You told a copyist a number."

"I told a copyist a number because I was asked to write down my reason, and the reason was *everything*, and the number was the only part of it anybody could check." Sarn put the paper on the table. "Here is the other part."

The other part took about nine minutes. The file had been a barn term instrument in the name of a sole signatory. The summary prepared in the office in the ordinary way, in the fourth month of that year, had carried, in a surety line, a person who was a child of the household. And the endorsement, which was the routine four lines, ended with the words that Sarn had read standing at a window an hour earlier with a stranger leaning on her sill.

"I found it on the second day," she said. "Not on the first. On the first I did what I do. I looked at whether the file could be closed in a day, because the docket was three weeks from a relief certification and there were four hundred and sixty rolls in it. And then I found the endorsement, and I sat with it until the second hour of the afternoon on the second day, and I understood, sitting there, that this was not a filing error. A filing error does not put a child's name in a surety line with that sentence under it. Somebody had made a decision about that child and written it in the ordinary place where a clerk writes the ordinary thing."

"And you sat with it for two more days."

"I sat with it for two more days and I did the arithmetic, and I want you to understand that the arithmetic was not a comfort."

---

"The docket was three weeks from certification," Sarn said. "Four hundred and sixty rolls. I did not know then, and I know now, that this building had already done it once, in the eighth month of the fifth year, and got eleven hundred households four months of eating, and I have signed that refusal myself and I would sign it again.

"The arithmetic was this. If I went in and said, in writing, on the docket, that a summary in a five-year-old barn term file carried a substituted guarantor line with a minor named in it, then the docket was open. Not this file. The docket. Because that is what an examiner does with a line like that, and once he has started, the certification does not go out on the third of the month, and the fourth month's relief does not go out on the fourth month's day, and the whole upper district and half the lower one eat in the wrong weeks in the worst winter this river has had in nine years.

"And I had before me a district where a file had been shut for two years and a woman with a floor and a child, and I could not put the two sets of numbers next to each other and have both of them be right, and I was the one who had to hold both of them.

"So I did it. I held the file for four days, on the fourth day the relief rolls still could not be certified, and I told Rennie Sallow at this window that the docket was not closed, and I did not tell him why, and I have carried the reason for six years in a drawer in my own rooms and I have never said it to him, and he has never asked me, which is the thing that has kept me awake about it for six years. He never asked me. He came twice on the fourth day and once on the fifth and I sent him away, and he never once said *why*, and a man who has been told nothing will believe anything."

Sarn stopped. She was not looking at Marek. She was looking at the paper on the table, at her own hand from six years ago, at four lines on a tally that was a working document and had gone into a district file and was therefore the only true thing she had ever written about herself.

"That is it," she said. "It was not carelessness. It was competence. I knew exactly what the paper was and I knew exactly what it would cost and I chose the four hundred and sixty, and I did not tell one man the truth, and there is not a word in the record anywhere that says I did anything wrong, and that is the part I have not been able to put down."

Marek Kest said nothing for a moment, which was what she had asked him for, and then he said, "Say the last part again."

"Which one."

"The part where you are making yourself the villain, because in about forty seconds you are going to tell me that you would do it again, and I would like to hear you say it properly instead of arranging for me to say it for you."

Sarn looked at him.

"Yes," she said. "I would do it again. I had the fifth month's winter in front of me and that household did not, and I have been to the relief office in the eighth month of the sixth year and I have seen what a district looks like when the rolls do not go out, and it is not a thing you can put in a ledger. I would hold it four days again. And Rennie Sallow would lose his seed again, and I would tell him the docket was not closed and not why, and I would keep the reason in a drawer, and I would be right."

"That is an honest answer and I have no argument against it," Marek said, "and I want you to understand what it cost me to hear it, because I have come down here for eleven days telling people that if a clerk will only say the true thing in a room then the room will do the right thing with it, and you have just told me that a clerk saying the true thing in a room can cost a named household a winter, and that it should."

"It is not a case," said Sarn. "It is one file and a man and two hundred and forty pounds of seed, and it is not a case, and if anybody tries to make it into a case I will stop talking."

"That is where it stops," said Marek. "Both of us. This goes into the record as one file, one clerk, four days, one household, one loss, and it does not go out with the shape of a rule in it, because I have spent twelve days watching a rule go out of this building wearing a sensible coat, and I am not doing it."

"You are doing it now," said Sarn. "You have just told me it does not go out as a rule, which is a decision, and you have made it in an anteroom, and I am a clerk and I will do what you decide and I want it written down that I have said so before I have heard it."

Marek Kest put his hand over his eyes, which he had been doing about four times a day for a fortnight.

"Go on," he said, from behind his hand. "You are going to have the last word and I am not going to fight you for it, and I have never in three years been on this side of a conversation."

---

Here was the arrangement, and Sarn dictated it into her own book first and then into the magistrate's, and Marek Kest typed nothing and signed nothing and was not a party to it, and that was the price he paid for being in the room.

That the record should carry the reason and the consequence together, and neither without the other.

That the Sallows were to be told first, and by her, in her own words, and not by a clerk out of the Chancery and not by a copyist and not in a letter, and that she was going up the upper lane that afternoon with the tally in her coat and was going to tell Rennie Sallow what she had done and why and what it had cost, and that if he asked her for the number she was going to give him the number.

And Marek Kest said, "I will come with you," and Sarn said, "No," and Marek Kest said, "There is a thing I can do," and Sarn said, "You are a Chancery clerk in an unstamped coat who has been in this building for twelve days and the last man who came up that lane with a paper from the capital did not leave it. He is going to open his mouth in front of Rennie Sallow and say something about mechanisms, and Rennie Sallow has lost eleven pounds of seed and a winter's floor because of a mechanism, and if I bring the mechanism to his door then I am making him do the work of being angry at a thing I have brought him instead of at a thing I did."

"Then I will walk at the bottom of the lane," Marek said. "I will not come up. I will stand at the bottom of the lane where he can see me standing there, and I will not come up, and if he comes down to me I will answer whatever he asks, and if he does not come down that is a thing I have done and I will not tell you about it."

Sarn considered that for a moment longer than she needed to, and then she said, "The bottom of the lane."

---

She came back at the eighth hour of the evening.

Marek Kest had been at the bottom of the lane for five hours, in the rain, and had been spoken to by four people on the way down and had answered every one of them, and one of them had been a woman with a bar who had asked him whether the ditch was hers now and had told him no before he answered.

Sarn came down the upper lane alone, and she was carrying nothing, and she looked about forty years old.

"He has got the number," she said. "I gave him the number. He asked me for it about a quarter of an hour in, which is what I said he would, and he has written it down and he has asked me to write it down again on the copy of the tally that he has paid for out of a shilling I have not asked him for, and he has asked me to initial it, and I have."

"What did he say."

"He said: *I did not think about you for six years and I have thought about nothing else for a fortnight, and now I can stop.*" She said it without any expression. "And then he asked me whether the word was in his file or in mine. Not *who wrote it*. *Where it was.* He wanted to know whether it was a thing in his house or a thing in my building, and when I said it was a line in a summary made in this building in the fourth month of the fifth year and not a thing he ever saw or signed, he said: *then it is not mine and it was never mine, and I have been carrying it as if it was mine for six years, and I am putting it down on your doorstep where you left it.*"

Marek said, "And his seed."

"Eleven pounds," said Sarn. "He paid eleven pounds over the price in the fourth month to buy seed that the district was going to give him, and he has the figure, and it is on the copy, and it is a real figure and I have not asked him to soften it. He wants it recorded. He said, and I am writing it down, and I am going to read it to the magistrate tomorrow: *Let it be written that the eleven pounds was mine and not the court's and not the district's and that I am not asking anybody for it, and that I would like there to be a place where it says that a clerk in this building looked at a thing that was wrong and put it in a drawer, and I would like that place to be the same window I have been coming to for forty years.*"

She stopped in the middle of the lane.

"That is what he said," she said, "and I have had it in my hand since the fifth hour and I have not been able to write it into anything, and I have come down here to give it to you before I write it down, so that there are two people holding it."

---

The thing Sarn had read at the window an hour earlier was in the record by the first of the month, with a name Sarn had looked up and put in the register in her own hand, because a duty that can be read by anybody has to have the household on it.

And the fourth of the month, in the anteroom, with the magistrate in the chair and Sarn at the table and Marek Kest standing at the back by the door where he had been told to stand, the record took the reason and the consequence together.

And then Marek Kest, who had said for two days that he would not put a rule in it, stood up anyway, and said the thing that had to be said, because nobody else in the room was going to say it and it had to be said out loud by somebody or it would sit in a minute book being true and small.

"The wording in that file is the wording in the Venn correction," he said. "Word for word. *Being a work of the ground and not of a person*, and *the present holder*, and *until full review*. The same four lines. And that file was made in the fourth month of the fifth year, which is four years before the correction was filed in the Venn packet in the eighth month of this year."

He put his hand on the table and did not look at Sarn.

"So it was not written for the Venns," he said. "The Venns are not the first household in this district it was used on, and whoever filed that correction in August did not compose it, because it was already here in the fifth year, in a tray, in this building, and it had been sat on by a clerk for four days and then filed and then closed. Which means the thing we are looking at is not a fraud in a lane. It is a practice, and a practice does not have a day it was invented on, and it does not have a person who did it on a Tuesday, and it is the reason there is nothing in that minute book for you to rule on, Magistrate, because there is no wrong party in the room. There is only a form and a habit and a sentence that a court in this district found useful and put in its own practice and then, when a widow's file needed it, put in hers."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Sit down, Clerk Kest," said Rell at last, in a voice that was not unkind and was not kind either. "And write your last sentence down, because I want it in the record and I am not going to have you carrying it about in your head, and I have watched you do that for twelve days."

He wrote it down. Sarn took the copy and read it back, and the magistrate entered it, and the clerk's hand on the bench did not stop for a single moment of the whole of it, which Sarn noticed, and about which she was grateful in a way she could not have put into a record.
