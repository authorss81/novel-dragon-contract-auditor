# Chapter 2: The Work That Is Named

The boat from Auremar put them at the Reed Cut landing at first light with the cold still in the reeds, and by the time the channel banks were wide enough to walk, Marek understood that Mosswake was not a place so much as a set of decisions about water taken a long time ago and still being obeyed by people who had never seen the person who made them.

Tamsin came up out of the boat behind him with the packet wrapped in oilcloth against her back and her copy case slung across her shoulder, and she said, before they had gone twenty steps, "Say the thing you are thinking."

"I am thinking that this is a working channel and not a place where a person discusses their inheritance."

"Then say it to them, and they will be pleased, and we will get nothing."

They walked the levee. The water on the left had a smell Marek had learned to identify years ago and still could not explain to anyone: cold stone, iron, and something green underneath that was not quite a plant. On the right, the ground was already being worked in long strips by three men and a boy, and a fourth man was standing in the mud at the end of a furrow doing nothing at all, in a way that meant he was deciding something important.

"Which is Tavi," Tamsin said.

The girl at the far end of the strip was seventeen and had a length of iron bar over one shoulder and was walking the furrow line with her toes, checking something Marek could not see. When she saw two strangers in good coats coming up the levee she straightened up, and she did not come toward them, and she did not go away. She waited in a way that made it clear she intended to be spoken to but not to be helpful.

"Miss Venn," Marek said. "I'm clerk of the inheritance desk at Auremar. This is Tamsin Rook, the copyist assigned to the file. We'd like to ask about the channel work and about the instrument your father signed."

"The court's already asked." She spat into the furrow, which was either a courtesy or an insult, and Marek could not decide. "There's a notice. It says I owe seed. The notice came before you."

"I know."

"Then you can tell the court the same thing I told the man who read it to me. I never signed anything." She shifted the iron bar. "And I can tell you the gate linkage is two fingers out on the lower rod since Tuesday and if somebody with a bar and an opinion stands here all morning talking about my father's paper, the lower field goes under on Thursday and that will be a real debt, and it will be mine, and I will not enjoy it."

"You know the linkage."

"I know the linkage."

"Will you show me?"

That was not what she expected. He had learned that about people who are used to clerks: they expect the first two questions to be about the past and they have answers loaded for both.

She looked at him for a moment, then walked thirty yards along the bank to a low stone structure with an iron frame across it and a wheel standing waist high, and worked the wheel over twice while she talked, and the bar went from his shoulder to the frame, and something in the frame took up with a sound like a man clearing his throat.

"Two fingers out," she said. "Because a man's finger is two fingers, and it was set in the eighth month and never reset, because the reset is the water office's job and the water office came in the ninth month and looked at the flood memorandum and went away again." She put her palm flat on the stone. "Nobody writes down that my father was the one who kept this in true. They write down that there is a gate. Gate is not a person. Gate does not get tired and go to bed and then get out of bed again in the dark."

Marek said nothing, because there was nothing to say to that which would not be worse than silence. Instead he took out his reed-pen case, sat down on the bank, and asked her to say it again while he wrote, in the order she had said it: the date it was set out, the date it was reset, who came, what they looked at, what they wrote.

Tavi Venn watched him do it. That was the moment something in her face changed, and it was not warmth. It was the particular attention of a person who has been shouting into a room and has finally noticed that somebody is taking down the words.

Behind them, up the path, a door had opened.

---

Mara Venn came out with a dish cloth over one shoulder and stopped twenty feet away and did not come closer. She was forty-three and had her husband's hands, which was not a thing you could see directly; it was a thing you saw in how she held her wrists.

"You'd be the clerk," she said.

"I am."

"From the city." She said *city* the way people in the channel country say it: as a fact about the weather. "We had a man from the district office in the third month. He was pleasant."

"I won't take that as a recommendation."

"No." She folded the cloth. "You'll want to come inside. I won't have you doing the rest of this on a bank in front of the whole lane, and I won't have you in my kitchen either. Sit on the step, if you want. You can see the field from the step."

So Marek sat on the step, and Tamsin sat on the step beside him, and Mara stood in the doorway with her arms folded until she decided not to.

"Tell me what Aldren signed," Marek said.

"Once. Then you tell me what you think he signed, and if they differ we will both know where we are."

"Tell me first."

Mara went into the house and came back with a board the size of two hands, waxed green, and set it on her knee, and did not open it.

"He signed once," she said. "In the second month of the year four years back. He went down to the water office with a man from the district and the keeper came up from the gate because the keeper always comes up when there's a sign to make. Seven years, it was. Water and field. The gate and the navigation were hers. The ditching and the field-side were his. That was the whole of it and he was pleased about it, do you understand me, pleased, because a man who is told which work is his will do that work for seven years and not argue. The winter-seed repayment was his. It came out of his harvest and it was paid out of his estate at the end of the term. Not mine. Not hers." She put one finger on the board without opening it. "I was the witness. I stood there and I heard it and I gave my name and I made my mark at the bottom, which is where a witness marks, not at the side."

"And the continuation. The stipulation that came later."

"I never signed a stipulation." Her voice did not rise. It went the other way, quieter, and Marek had already learned that this family did their anger low and precise. "A clerk brought one to me in the sixth month, on the field, when I was turning the second row. He was very young and very polite and he wanted me to put a mark at the bottom of it, same as the year before, he said. I asked what it was for. He said it was so that if anything happened to Aldren the field wouldn't be left without a hand. And I said my husband is dead and I have two hands, and he said it wasn't about me, it was about the field, and I said then it's about the field and it doesn't need me, and he put the paper away and went off up the lane, and I have thought about that every day since because he never came back."

Marek wrote: *no stipulation signed. paper offered, refused, clerk left. sixth month. young clerk.* He wrote it the way he wrote everything, in nouns and dates, because in his experience the feelings in a statement are carried by the nouns and the rest is weather.

"I'm not going to tell you that the court will rule for you," he said.

Mara's eyes came up.

"I'm going to tell you what is in front of me, and what I intend to do with it, and then you will decide how much of the winter you're willing to risk. On what you have told me, the instrument named Aldren as the field-side maintainer and as the person owing the winter-seed repayment. The keeper named herself for the gate and the navigation. The instrument required that anyone taking up a continuing duty sign for it separately. You witnessed the first one. You did not sign the second. There is a correction in the file that says the debt is held against the field and that the present holder of the field answers for it, and that correction has no authority attached to it and a third witness notch on the summary that was never pressed into the copy of the original."

"And if they use it?"

"They'll use it. That's the part I can't fix with paper. It is a clean sentence and it is now in a summary, and summaries are what the next file copies from." He put the pen down. "Which is why I have two days and a boat, and why the thing that matters most is that the court hears you. Not me. You. And the hearing is a decision about the field, and a hearing can take three weeks in this district, and the winter-seed notice goes out on the fourteenth and the gate inspection is on the eleventh, and the notice will be read to your daughter before anybody looks at the water."

Mara was quiet for long enough that Tamsin shifted on the step.

"The fourteenth," Mara said.

"The fourteenth."

"That's the difference, then. Not the law. Whether the field is a hand's work or a hole in the ground with a name on it." She opened the board at last. Inside, in three columns of careful little figures, was four years of the Venn household: seed, feed, wages for the extra pair of hands in cutting weeks, what was owed to whom and what had been paid. "He kept no paper. I kept this. If they say he owed and I say he paid, they will want a number, and I can give them the number, and I can tell them the month."

Marek looked at the figures, and at the wax, and at the way the last column was full and the one before it was crowded with small corrections in a second hand, which would be Mara herself a year later, going back and adding what she had forgotten to enter the first time.

"I would like to copy this," he said. "Not take it. Copy it, here, in your house, in front of you, and give you the copy and keep the original where it is."

"You'll copy a woman's arithmetic for a court that will not read it."

"I'll copy it for the part of the record that is a number."

Mara looked at him for a long moment, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, which from Mara was the same as a handshake.

"Do it on the step," she said. "Not in the kitchen."

He copied the columns for forty minutes. Tamsin made a second fair copy alongside him, in her own hand, and read the figures back to Mara a column at a time, and when Mara corrected one of them Tamsin struck the line through rather than smoothing it, so that the correction stayed visible, which was more honest than any clerk had bothered to be with that household in four years.

Then Tamsin closed her case and stood.

"The court window shuts at three for the flood docket," she said. "I want the Dunn file in my own eyes before tonight, and I want the comparison of your witness copy against the correction entered in their register with today's date on it. If Pell writes to the court tomorrow, the register shows we were here first."

"You're going ahead alone."

"I've got the copy and I've got the name. You go and look at that linkage." She slung the case across her shoulder. "And take the keeper's statement in her own words, Marek, not a version of it you've made tidy."

---

The gate was a quarter mile down the channel, and the crew was at it, and the keeper was standing on the stone platform above the linkage with her wings half-folded and one foreclaw resting on the frame as if she were holding a horse still by the bridle.

She was not large. That was the first thing. People came to the Reed Cut gate expecting something with a neck like a ship and got a dragon the height of a good doorway and twice that in length, sun-black along the back, brass-coloured underneath, with a face that had been worn smooth in two places by weather. She had been here, Marek understood, a very long time. Her nails were scored by a hundred seasons of iron.

"Sivra Oris," she said, before he had got ten feet. "Water keeper, Reed Cut, and this gate is under my seal and my name, and I know the shape of your errand because I have had it before, three times since the third month. You are the city clerk. Are you here to tell me the channel's owner has changed?"

"No, Keeper."

"Then you are here to tell me the work is mine."

"I have come to ask which work is yours."

Sivra Oris looked at him for a moment, and then down at the crew, and then she spoke in the same voice she had used to him, so that everyone on the platform heard her, which he understood later had been done on purpose and would cost her something to have done.

"The gate and the navigation are mine. The ditching and the field-side of the lower field were Aldren Venn's from the day he signed that paper, to the day he died, and they were good work, and I would have been glad to have had them kept by the man who knew how." The iron took up under her claw as she shifted. "I will not be given his work. I have nine gates and two hundred yards of bank and I do not need a dead man's ditching handed to me in a sentence written by somebody in a warm room. And equally I will not have my gate put in his debt, because that is the other half of the trick and it is the half that is actually done."

"So you refuse both readings."

"I refuse both readings, clerk, and I will put that in the record if you will write it down, and I will not need a copy of the sentence because I know what I said." Her eyes were on him, and very still, and not unkind. "You understand that a seal is not a kindness. I have held this gate since before your district had a name in the census. When I put my mark to that instrument, I put it to seven years of a man's work and seven years of my own, and it cost me nothing, because a term that ends is a term I can honour. What I will not do is sign a thing that has no end on it. A person who cannot end an obligation eventually cannot refuse one, and then you have not got a contract, you have got a district."

"May I write that as you said it?"

"Write it exactly."

He wrote it. When he had finished he turned the page round and let Tavi Venn read it, which she did from the top to the bottom without hurrying, and then she said, "You left out that the reset man put his thumb in the frame," and he went back and put it in, and she nodded once and went back to her furrow.

Down on the bank Tavi Venn's face was doing the thing that faces do when they have been given a piece of good news they were not expecting and have not yet decided whether to trust.

At the top of the landing where the path met the bank, Tamsin was waiting with her case open on a low wall, and she had ink on the side of her right hand where she always got it when she was working fast.

"The Dunn file says *the present holder*," she said. "Not a clerk's summary. The clerk's summary of the Dunn file says *the present holder*, and the original instrument in the same packet names Orra Dunn and nobody else, and the summary has not been filed, and the register has it as *received, unfiled*."

"Who's told the child?"

"Nobody on the record. That's the trouble. Nobody needs to be, if the family's been told by somebody's brother-in-law at a gate, and a child hears a thing like that once and then it is simply how the world is." She shut the case. "Her name is Nessa and she is fifteen and she has signed nothing, and she sat on the bench outside the clerk's window and told me so four times without being asked the same question twice."

Marek looked up the levee at the grey water and the black line of the gate.

"Then it isn't a widow's paper," he said. "It's already in two houses, and I have until the courier takes the Venn summary out of my hands for it to be in one."
