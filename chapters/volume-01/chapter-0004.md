# Chapter 4: The Cabinet

The Notaries' Table occupied the north end of the third gallery, behind a rail, and the rail existed for one reason: to keep people at a working distance from the locked cabinet. The cabinet was nine feet of oak with iron furniture and a key that lived on a chain around the neck of whichever supervisor was on duty, and Marek had been in this room for three years without once having a reason to touch it.

On the second morning of the Venn file he had three.

He had been at the end of the Table for an hour before the Table would see him, and the hour had not been wasted, because he had had the packet open on the rail and the one page of it that nobody in the building ever looks at is the back of a correction.

The front of a correction is the part that changes what a person owes. The back is where the office says what the change cost, in the small printed hand that fee entries are kept in.

He turned it over at the rail with the lamp pulled round, and there was a certificate impression pressed into the paper: a loop of scale crossed by a bar, flat and even, with nothing in it that looked as though it had been alive while it was being made. And under the impression, a fee line. A date, an amount, a description, a broker's address in Auremar, Candlewright Lane, and a fee authorised by a district office that had no business authorising a fee on a field-side instrument at all.

He wrote the address out twice on the back of his own docket note, in his own hand, and then looked at what he had written for a while.

He had a name and no use for it. A broker's fee on a field instrument said a person had been paid to do a thing. It did not say which thing, and it did not say who asked, and it could not be put in front of anybody without somebody able to say where a clerk had got it from at ten in the morning with a lamp pulled round.

"Sit down, Marek."

Pell Vey was already at the Table with the Venn packet open in front of him, and he had been open to it for a while, because the packet had arrived by the internal tray at the seventh hour and it was now past the ninth. He had a pen in his hand and he was not writing with it.

"I want the original source leaf," Marek said.

"Which source leaf."

"The Venn source leaf. Seven-year water-and-field instrument, second month of the fourth year, Reed Cut. It's in the cabinet. The witness copy has two witness notches and the summary has three, and the correction was filed in the eighth month under a flood memorandum that arrived the day before it, and I want to lay the original beside the correction and look at the two of them."

"Then you want the comparison form, which is what you have written on this." Pell turned the paper round. It was the standard request: *Requested by clerk M. Kest, Inheritance Desk, for a comparison of source leaf against filed correction in the matter of Venn, lower field, Reed Cut.* It was correct in every part. It was also, in every part, a request. "Signed and dated. Which is more than most of your generation manages."

"I know how to fill in a form, Pell."

"I trained you. I am not worried about the form." Pell put the pen down. "I am worried about what happens after the form. Here is what happens after the form. The leaf comes out of the cabinet, which means it goes into a custody sleeve, which means the Notaries' Table has signed for it, which means that until a hearing either accepts it or returns it, the Table is responsible for a piece of original paper that somebody will eventually be very angry about. And then a clerk compares them, and finds a discrepancy, and the discrepancy has to be entered in a register, and the register goes to the provincial court, and the provincial court has to reopen a matter that was certified in the third month under flood authority during the season when the district cannot afford an open docket."

"That is Sarn's argument, and Sarn gave it to me yesterday with better reasons than you are about to give it."

"Then Sarn and I agree, which is the first time in nine years, and I would like you to consider what it means." Pell sat back. "Marek. I am going to tell you what I think happened, and then you are going to tell me why you think I am wrong, and we will both go to lunch."

Marek sat down. It was the tone. Pell's tone said: *I am your superior and I have been fair to you for three years and I am about to be fair to you again, and I would like you to notice the fairness.*

"I think a clerk in a hurry put a third mark on a summary," Pell said. "I think he was working a flood docket with four hundred households on it. I think he marked the summary because the summary was working from the correction and not from the original, which is exactly what the third notch means and exactly what I told you it meant on the first evening. I think you found the marks, which is the entire point of a witness mark, and found them in a hurry, in the last hour, in poor light, and have spent a night and a boat ride building a case out of a tired man's thumb."

"The notch was copied."

"Natches are copied. That is what a notch is. It is a device for saying *this page came from more than one source*, and it is copied by design, and the fact that the copy is cleaner than the original tells you that a practised hand made it, not a guilty one."

"The third notch is at the wrong height for the witness line."

"The third notch is at the height a third notch goes when the person making the summary is standing at a different desk than the person who took the original marks. I have been copying this docket for nineteen years, Marek. I have made that mark on a thousand pages at a hundred heights."

That was not a lie. Marek could hear that it was not a lie, which was somehow worse than if it had been, because a lie is a wall and this was a man who believed himself, at length, and in writing, and had believed it for nineteen years.

"And the correction itself," Marek said. "The text. *Being a work of the ground and not of a person.* Pell, who wrote that? Not a tired clerk. Not a flood clerk. That's a person who knew the name on the instrument had died and did not want the name to be in the sentence."

"Or a person who wanted the sentence to survive the man, which is the entire purpose of a continuation, and is not a crime, and is written that way in every legitimate instrument in this building."

"It names no duty. It names no person. It says the present holder answers for the same. Same as what? Same as what, Pell?"

"Same as the terms named in the said instrument. It refers back."

"It refers back to a fixed-term repayment and it makes it perpetual by attaching it to a field instead of a man."

"Then the court decides whether it does that. Not a clerk. Not a nervous junior with a boat." Pell picked the pen up and turned it. "Marek, I will tell you the whole of my position, and then I will do you a kindness. I believe the Venn correction is probably bad. I believe that a third notch and a continuation attached to land instead of a person is the shape of a bad instrument, and I have been certifying instruments of that shape for nineteen years and I know it. I also believe that this district is nine weeks from winter, that the Venn field feeds about ninety people, that the woman in it is a widow with a daughter of seventeen, and that if I reopen that file this month, then in the fourth month of next year the district is eating four months late because I reopened a file."

"That's Sarn's eleven hundred."

"That's my eleven hundred. I was the district registrar before I came to this Table, and I know what the number was, and I know whose children it was." He set the pen down. "So here is the kindness. You may have the leaf. I will sign the request myself and I will not make you ask twice. And when you have it, you will lay it beside the correction, and you will find the seam you have already found, and you will come to me, and we will go to the provincial court together, as the Table and a clerk who found something, which is a thing that has happened before and works. And you will not do it this week."

"Why not this week."

"Because on the eleventh the Reed Cut gates are inspected and on the fourteenth the winter-seed notices go out in about four hundred houses, and your file is in the middle of both. If I pull the leaf this week, the Venn notice issues anyway, because the notice does not wait for a comparison, and your widow gets a notice on the fourteenth either way, and she reads it, and she hears that the city has come up to look at her, and then nothing happens until the spring. Whereas if I certify on the corrected basis this week and open the comparison in the spring, she gets no notice, and she has a field, and your discovery happens in a room with a bench in it instead of a lane where a girl of seventeen is standing in front of her neighbours."

Marek said nothing for a moment, because it was a good argument, and because good arguments at the Notaries' Table are always made by men who are not the one who will be standing in the lane.

"And if the correction is bad," he said, "then in the ninth month you will have certified it, and it will be precedent, and in the eleventh month somebody else will copy it, and in the twelfth it will be a district habit, and in the second year of next year nobody will remember it was ever a question."

"Then it will be a habit I can point at, with a date on it, and a clerk's request in the register with a date on it, and a name." Pell's voice was kind. That was the unbearable part. "Marek. I taught you the lesson and you learned it too well. The lesson was that a record is a thing you can lean on. You have learned that a record is a thing you can pick up and move, and I am telling you that moving it is the expensive half. Nothing has been done yet. The notice goes on the fourteenth. If the notice goes on the fourteenth, a girl of seventeen is a debtor in front of her lane, and no amount of good work in the spring takes that back."

From the aisle, quietly, Tamsin Rook put a book face down on the end of the Table.

She had come in behind Marek and had been standing at the rail, which is what copyists do, and now she had come to the end of the Table and put down her second book, opened, at the place where the fourth month fell, where the index was, and where the four lines of the Dunn summary sat in her own hand under a date and a receipt number.

Pell looked at it. Then he looked at Tamsin.

"Copyist Rook," he said. "That is a received tray index and a private copy, and I would like to know by whose authority the second half was made."

"Mine," Tamsin said. "The tray is open until three o'clock. The index is public. The copy is a copyist's working copy, which is what I am, and it has a receipt number on it, so if you want to say it is not a true copy, you can compare it with the leaf, and I will hold still while you do."

Neither of them looked at Marek, and Marek understood that this had been done for him and not about him, and that the understanding was the most useful thing anybody had done for him in three years.

"A Dunn summary," Pell said. "Filed in the fourth month. Unfiled."

"Unfiled."

"Received but not filed, and therefore not operative, and therefore not a matter for this office."

"It names a girl of fifteen as surety for a barn term," Tamsin said. "The original names Mrs Dunn and nobody else. And the third line of it carries the same four words as the Venn summary, in the same order, in the same place in the line, in a document from four months earlier." She put one finger on it. "You can see them together. I have not hidden it in a drawer. I put it on the table because the table is where documents are examined, and because if I put it in a drawer then one day somebody will say that a junior clerk's private book is the whole of the evidence, and it will be true, and that will be the end of it."

Pell read the four lines. He read them a second time. He read them a third time, and Marek watched the back of his neck while he did it, because a man who is looking for a mistake reads three times in a different rhythm, and a man who is looking for confirmation stops at two.

Then Pell Vey, supervisor of the Notaries' Table, said: "Marek. Put your request on the desk and take your hand off it. I will sign it in the first hour tomorrow. Not today. Today I have a seal to fire and a Table to certify and a girl on the fourteenth, and I will not have the cabinet open in flood season with an unsigned sleeve in it, because a sleeve that sits overnight in an open cabinet is how records go missing, and a record that goes missing in this building is a record somebody can say never existed."

"And if the leaf disappears overnight?"

"Then you will have no comparison, and I will have signed nothing, and the notice will still go out on the fourteenth, and you will have spent your week being right in a way that helps nobody." Pell stood up. "Which is the whole of this office, Marek. Not the winning. The not-losing. I have forty clerks and thirty-nine of them can be taught the winning, and only four of them can be taught the not-losing, and I have never once had to fire a clerk for losing."

Marek put the request on the desk. He took his hand off it. Tamsin closed her book and did not put it away, and took her place back at the rail, and stood there with her arms folded in a way that was no longer reserving an opinion.

Marek went back down the gallery. He had got as far as the window when the board clerk's boy came round the desks with the day's notices, calling the desk numbers out loud, which was the practice, and which Marek had never once listened to.

"Venn, lower field, Reed Cut. Subject to administrative hold pending verification of continuity terms. Not to be enforced, removed, or assigned pending further notice. Provincial reference: administrative instruction, sealed. The reference carries no office name."

The boy went on down the gallery.

Nobody at the desks said anything. Notices went up on the board every day; two thirds of them were about drainage.

Marek stood in the cold end of the window with the light coming off the Kiln behind him, and thought about a memorandum signed in a warm room by a man clearing a backlog, and a correction sewn on with correct red thread, and a table at the end of the third gallery with a signature going onto it tomorrow morning and not tonight, and a child on a bench in Mosswake who had already been told what she was going to owe, and the fact that somebody had just taken the field out of everybody's hands with one line, in a sealed envelope, from an office that did not have a name on the envelope.

He had asked for a key. It had taken him two days to be told the cost of the key, and now the cost of the key had turned out to be somebody else's field, and he had a night to decide whether he was going to say so out loud in a room full of people or write it down first.
