# Chapter 29: A Mile Away

The fifth term's works were done, the office's own turf was on the west bank, and the term itself was going to be up in the second month, and after the second month there was nothing.

That was the thing nobody said in the Venn lane in the first month of the new year, and it was not a secret, and it was said constantly in about nine different ways every day, and Nell Kest counted thirty-one separate occasions in a fortnight of the first month while she was collecting the last of the levy money and copying the fourth house's clause into its schedule.

The ruling of the twelfth of the ninth preserved the channel and the lower field to Aldren's household until the second month, when seven years from the signing are up, against any creditor, tenant, surety or assignee. It did not say what happened on the first day of the third month. It could not have; a term is a term, and a court that makes a term longer by saying so is a court that has stopped doing its job.

Tavi Venn was seventeen, she was better at a channel than nine men and a foreman, and after the second month she was a girl with a bar and no field.

---

Mara Venn did the arithmetic on the same board her husband had used, in the same charcoal, and she had done it about nine times in the first three days of the second month and it had not improved any of the times.

*Seed for the lower field: paid. Seed for the flats: paid, and it comes out of the district and not out of us, and the district is nineteen sacks short and that is the district's problem and it is our problem. Winter wages: the canal crew goes in the second month and the canal crew is Mr Vellick's and Mr Vellick has four men and one of them was in my husband's ditch in the fourth month for nothing.*

*What we have after the second month: the house, the four quarters of the upper holding, the two rooms over the shop that we let to a man who works the flats, and a gate.*

"A gate," said Nell Kest.

"A gate is not a field," said Mara Venn. "A gate is a thing a man came out of the ninth year and made an iron keeper for. And I have said to the landlord three times that we will give it up and take the four quarters and a smaller rent, and he says yes and he says nothing, and a man who says yes and says nothing is a man waiting for the water to do his asking, and the water is coming in the second month whether we are on this land or not."

"And after?"

"After, my daughter takes the canal seat if they will look at her, and I am forty-three and I have had this lane's only job for four years and I do not have a trade, and I have never in my life had wages, and the whole of the reason I was able to be brave on a step in the ninth month is that I was not frightened of money, and I am now."

Nell Kest sat on the end of the Venn step in the cold, in a coat that was not hers and a scarf that was not hers, and did not say the nine sentences she had come up with on the road, which were about Odile Fenn's examination in the fourth month and about the fifth term and about how a district works' sheet nailed to a rail is a thing that can be read by anybody, and about how the ruling's fourth term means that nobody can be made to take up a continuing duty without signing for it, and which would all have been true and would all have been the wrong four sentences in the wrong house in the wrong month.

So she said the only one that mattered, which was: "What is the day."

"The nineteenth," said Mara Venn. "Seven years from the second month of the year four years back, and the deed says the nineteenth, and Aldren signed on the nineteenth, and I have never in four years thought about the nineteenth and I have thought about it every day for a month."

"Then on the eighteenth night I will come and sit here, and if you want me to sit here on the eighteenth night I will sit here on the eighteenth night, and I am not going to ask you a single question about the fourth month or the review or the water office, and if anybody in this lane asks me anything at all about any of it I will tell them I do not know, because I do not know, and I am not going to go round a lane in the last week of a term and make a widow's household into a thing people come and look at."

Mara Venn put the charcoal down.

"You were at the table every day for six weeks in the autumn and I never once said thank you," she said.

"No," said Nell.

"You are twenty. That is not a thing I am going to have said out loud twice."

---

The four days she was not in the lane were the four days she spent in Greyfen.

That was not an accident and not an illness. Vare had given her the two afternoons a week and the guild had begun to pay for the copying of the amended clause into the schedules of the four signing houses, and the Beckery's schedule was two hundred and forty lines and had to be gone through word by word against the assessor's form, and it could only be done in working hours, which meant the vats in the morning and a cart down the road after the sixth hour. She went on the eighth of the first month and she came back on the twelfth, and she went without telling the table anything, because there was nothing to tell: a woman who reads papers aloud at a trestle table is not a post, and anybody who has ever held a post knows that.

She came down the lane on the thirteenth at about the fifth hour because she wanted to know whether it had stopped, and it had not stopped.

There were eleven names in chalk and two of them were children, and there was a line under the four questions in a schoolroom hand that said: *I read a seed paper out on the Thursday for a woman from the flats and I did not tell her what to do about it. — the woman from the seed barn.* And somebody had washed the top of the trestle, with a wet cloth, because it was going sticky with the weather, and nobody had been asked to and nobody had told her about it.

That was the detail that got her, standing in the lane mouth in the dark, and she could not have said why: it was the first evidence she had had in three months that the table had become a thing with an opinion about itself.

Nell Kest stood there for about four minutes and let the whole of it arrive, and what arrived was not pride.

It was relief, and it was the specific relief of a person who has been standing at the end of a thing for three months and has been told, in a court, in a workroom, and in her own head, that standing at the end of it is not the same as the thing working — and has been carrying a private conviction that the two are the same, and that if she stopped, it would stop. And this time she had not stopped. This time she had gone to Greyfen on purpose, for money, on a cart, and had left, and it had gone on without her, and that was a different and much better thing than being ill, and she was not going to be able to explain to anybody why it was better and she was going to think about it for a long time.

And the part she missed, and missed more than she had expected to, was not the board and was not the eleven names. It was that on the Thursday a woman had come down the lane at the fifth hour to have a paper read out, and there had been somebody at the table, and that somebody had read it to her, and nobody had written down that she had ever come, and she went away with the four questions on a sheet and no record anywhere in the world that she had existed as a person who came.

Nobody noticed for four days. She had been the person who wrote down who came, and she had not thought about it as a job, because it was not a job, because the rule on the board says *no advice given* and writing a name down is not advice.

---

The Caudle house is the last house on the dyers' lane in Greyfen and it is an hour down the road from the lane mouth, and Nell Kest has known that for three months and had not once gone there with anything useful, because the Caudles have a bench and a levy and four children and a man who keeps the bench, and there is nothing a woman off the vats can bring them except herself.

She went on the second day of the second month, after her shift, with the levy book in her bag because the book was a reason, and the four questions on a sheet, and her own book.

"Mrs Caudle. I have come to ask you four questions about a paper and I am not going to advise you about it and I am not going to tell anybody what you say."

"Ask."

She asked them. She asked them in order, at the kitchen table, with a bar of soap on it and four children coming in and going out, and Nery Caudle answered the first three in about four minutes flat and then stopped on the fourth and sat quite still.

"Who read it to you," Nell said. "And do they get paid if you sign."

"I signed it," said Nery Caudle. "In my own yard, on the twenty-ninth of the tenth, with two pounds and five and a quarter of my wage in it, and you read it to me, and I made you read it back twice, and there is a line on the back of it in my own words that nobody has ever asked to see."

"Who read it to you, Mrs Caudle."

"You did," said Nery Caudle. "And then the man on the rack brought a second one in the second week of the twelfth, on a Tuesday, and he was paid by the assessors, and he did not ask me anything, and he put it on the bench and said it was the same paper, and I said the same paper is not the same paper twice, and he said it is, because the number on it is the same, and he left it."

"And did you look at the number."

"I looked at the number," said Nery Caudle. "I have a good eye for a number and I have been married nineteen years and I can tell you what a man in a good coat is doing with his hands. He was holding it out to be taken and not to be read, and I took it and put it under the cloth and I have not had it out since, and I have not told anybody I have got it, and I have told a woman off the vats in my own kitchen, and I would like that written down somewhere and I do not know where to write it."

Nell Kest wrote it in her own book, at the kitchen table, with a bar of soap on it, and read it back, and Nery Caudle made her read back the figure of the levy as well.

"Twenty-three houses are going to be walked in the fourth month," said Nell, "and I am going to be one of the people who walks them, and I am not going to be able to get to all of them, because I am at a vat from the sixth hour and the four hours afterwards are the only hours I have got. And that is the whole of it. There is no name for what I have just told you I cannot do. There is a board in a lane an hour up the road with four questions on it and no name on it, and everybody can add their own name to it, and every one of those names is a person who can only be in one room at a time."

"Twenty-three houses," said Nery Caudle.

"Twenty-three, and about forty in the two districts that have written, and a hundred more that have not. And that is the arithmetic of the whole thing, and I have got no answer to it and I have got four questions and an hour's walk and a coat."

"My mother used to send things round the lane with the children," said Nery Caudle. "Not papers. Bread. And the eldest carried and the two little ones carried and the baby slept in the pram and went nowhere, and the reason it worked is that the person who carried it was not the person who sent it, so the door got opened."

---

She got to the Sallows two days later, on the road at the top of the upper lane, an hour the other way from Greyfen and two hours from the Caudle house, and she had not gone up to the upper lane for a month and did not mean to stop, and she stopped because Wenna Sallow was standing at her own gate in the dark with a coat on and nothing in her hands.

"Your name is not on the board," said Nell.

"It was on the board every day from the sixth of the twelfth to about the eleventh," said Wenna Sallow, "and I took it off on the twelfth, and I have not put it on since, and you have not noticed, and you were not well, and I am not blaming you for that. I want you to hear me say the thing I took it off for."

"Go on."

"I stopped because of my father," she said. "I have been on that board since the sixth of the twelfth month, and I have read out fourteen papers, and every single person who came was told something true and useful, and not one of them has ever asked me a question about my own house. And I asked at that table in the thirtieth of the tenth, in front of everybody, and a man said *I do not know* and would not tell my mother there was not, and I have gone on putting my name on a board for three months being the person who helps, and it turns out that the reason I have not asked again is that I do not want to be told the same answer in front of people."

"Wenna—"

"And I am not stopping because I have stopped caring," said the girl. "I am stopping because I worked out that a thing which only works when you are not the one in trouble is not a thing for people in trouble, and I have known that since I was thirteen and I did not have anywhere to put it until a woman off the vats wrote four questions on a board."

Nell Kest stood at a gate in the dark a mile up the lane from the trestle and had nothing to say, and said it.

"I do not know either," she said. "I have been round twenty-three houses since Michaelmas and I cannot get to yours without a coach and I have not got a coach, and if you asked me what your father's file says tonight I would tell you I do not know, and I would be saying the same thing he said at the table, and he said it for the same reason I would say it, which is that he has not looked and does not want to be the one who is wrong in a lane."

"Then you will not tell my mother there is not."

"No. I am not going to lie to a woman I have known forty years to keep her out of it for a fortnight. That is the whole of what I have got and it is not very much and you may put it in the book."

"I am going to put it in the book," said Wenna Sallow, "and I am going to put my name back on the board in the morning, and I am going to be angry at you for about a year."

"That is fair," said Nell Kest, "and you should be, and I will be here for it."

---

She did not write to Marek.

She thought about it on the walk back, twice, on the road and at the lane mouth, and both times she got as far as composing the sentence in her head — a sheet, four lines, *a woman on the Caudle lane has a second paper under a cloth and a levy in the fourth month and twenty-three houses to be walked and I cannot get to all of them and I am telling you because I said in the ninth month that I would be told first* — and both times she put it in her coat and went to bed, and the reason she gave herself, which she examined properly and did not believe, was that the work was going on without her and that she did not want to be a person in it.

The true reason was in a workroom in Greyfen on the evening of the thirtieth of the tenth, and it was that a woman of twenty had handed her brother a sentence and had not been asked, and had told him in her own words that the price was that she had not been asked, and that a thing handed to you is not yours to refuse, and that it stays hers for ever.

Marek Kest was forty miles up the road in a building with a stove in a room and a file with a number in ink on it, and he was doing the work, and he was forty miles away, and he did not come, and he was not going to be told.

And the not-coming was the whole of what Nell Kest had wanted in the ninth month of the tenth year, and she got it on an evening in the first month of the new year, standing at a gate in the dark with a book in her hand, and she was not going to say so to him, ever, and she understood while standing there that the reason she was not going to say so was so that he would never know that he had given it to her.

The second month came in on the eighteenth, and the Venn term was up on the nineteenth, and nothing had been decided about any of it, and on the evening of the eighteenth Nell Kest went up the lane and sat on the step of the Venn house from the fifth hour until about the ninth, and did not ask a single question, and was fed, and went home.
