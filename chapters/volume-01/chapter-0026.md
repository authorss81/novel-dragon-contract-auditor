# Chapter 26: The List

The letter from Millbrook Reach came out of a stone on the flat of a dye vat in Greyfen on the fifteenth of the twelfth month, where it had lain since the boy brought it to the table, and it lay under that stone for thirteen more days before it moved to a hand that could do anything about it, and every one of those thirteen days was spent on the question of who was going to pay for the paper.

That was the whole of the beginning. That is what a thing that is going to save somebody looks like on the fifteenth of the twelfth month: a woman at a distance asking for a form, and a woman of twenty-four in a copying alcove in Auremar working out, on the back of a levy sheet, what one of those costs to send.

She worked it out twice and got it wrong twice, and both times the mistake was in the same direction, which was downwards, because Tamsin Rook is a woman who has spent three months in rooms with clerks in them and has learned that the instinct to be impressive about a sum is the one that is dangerous.

A set is three sheets. The five terms of the ruling of the twelfth of the ninth, the reply of the thirteenth of the twelfth, and the four questions off a board in a river lane. The five terms have to go out complete, in the order they were ruled, with the number against each, because a copy of four out of five terms is a rule with a piece taken out of it, and the piece will be the one somebody wants. The reply is four pages and has to go whole, including the paragraph about eleven shillings, because the paragraph about the money is the reason anybody in a district will believe that a thing can be stopped. The four questions fit on one side of a sheet and can be copied in a schoolroom hand or a better one, and have to say on the sheet that they are not a law, because the day somebody prints them is the day they become one.

That is three sheets and a fold, and a set costs, in Tamsin's arithmetic, three and a half hours of somebody's hands.

Not four pence. Three and a half hours.

She wrote that figure at the top of the sheet, and wrote the price of the paper under it, and then wrote the two of them together the way Dren had written hours and shillings in the same column at Reed Cut, so that they could not be taken apart by somebody who only wanted the small one.

The paper came to a shilling and ninepence. The twine, a penny. The courier down the river to Mosswake and back, elevenpence, because the packet boat will not take a parcel of loose sheets and a boy on the coach will not either. Thirty-three pence is two shillings and ninepence, and that is the whole of what it costs to say a thing is true in a place three hundred miles from here, and Tamsin Rook wrote the figure at the head of the sheet with the hours underneath it, and paid it out of her own money, in her own rooms, over eleven nights, and three and a half hours of her hands times the number of sets, which was the number she had not decided yet.

---

The number was forty-one, and getting to forty-one was the actual work of the fortnight, and none of it was difficult in the way the rest of the case had been difficult. It was difficult in the way that asking a person a question is difficult when you have to hear the answer.

The twenty-six were the beginning and they were countable. Nine of them were hers. Six came from the press on the Greyfen road. Four from the woman in the hood at the seed barns. Three from Ivo Harker's tin, which is a bundle of his mother's papers on which he has not spent a penny in four years. Four from two men off the Reed Cut bank who would not give their names in September and made theirs together on the back of a seed tally, arguing about the fourth term the whole way, and one of whom is now on the chalk board under his own name because a rota cannot run on a man who has not got one.

The rest of the forty-one were new, and they came in a way Tamsin had not expected, which is that they came asking.

Two came from the Mosswake court. One was Sarn, who wrote her own set out in her own hand over two evenings at the window after the last person had gone, and would not have it sent; it is in a drawer under the board, and it is the reason the register of hearings is the only register in that district that has never once been lost.

The other was Pella Osk, who asked for one, and got one, and then made four more in an evening and gave them away in the copying room to people who had come up on the packet boat that week, one of whom was a woman from a landing further down the water than Millbrook, who was going home and would not say where from.

The rest came out of the lane, the houses, and the districts, and they came because of the reply of the thirteenth, which Tamsin had not thought of as the cause of anything at all until the twenty-fourth of the month, when a miller she had never met walked into a lane in Mosswake and asked, at the trestle table, whether it was true that an office had offered a thing and a clerk had refused it, because he had heard so from a factor in Glasswater who had heard it from a man on a boat, and what he wanted was the paper, and he wanted to know who to give it to.

Tamsin gave him the paper.

He was the first of them and he set the price, which is the thing she wrote down that night: *People want the reply, not the ruling. The ruling is what a person needs in a lane. The reply is what a person needs in a town, and towns are where the offices are.*

---

It failed on money on the twenty-sixth, exactly where it was always going to fail, and it failed quietly, and it failed to a professional.

The press on the Greyfen road is a one-window shop with *copying* painted on the glass in gold that is going green at the edges, and the woman who runs it had made six of the twenty-six for nothing in the ninth month, out of a workroom bench at the end of four days of other people's trouble, and had told nobody her name and had been let get on with it.

Tamsin went in on the twenty-sixth with a sheet in her hand and an offer in her mouth and was not permitted to make it.

"Six," said the woman, before Tamsin had got to the second sentence. "In the ninth month. That was in the ninth month and you are in the twelfth and I know exactly what day it is in the twelfth because the fog comes back at this time every year." She had a sheet under the glass with a fair copy of a marriage settlement on it, and she was copying it at speed, and she did not stop. "I did those six for nothing because my foreman stood on a bench in my workroom in the second week of the tenth month and read your lane's ruling out loud to eight women and told us that the holder pays, and I have had that shop eleven years and thirty-one years before it, and I have never in my life been so angry in a room."

"I am not asking for six for nothing."

"Four pence a sheet," said the woman, "and that is the price and it is not a new price, it is the price I have charged for nine years, and if you want it cheaper you can take it to the Marrowgate house where the woman does the parish sheet and she will do it for a loaf and she does it badly and I would not give it to a person in a court."

Tamsin Rook put fourpence on the counter for the first sheet and did not argue, and it was the first time in three months that she had paid somebody for something that mattered, and she found that it was a considerable relief.

"You will want to know why I am telling you it was anger," the woman said, "and it is because a person in a lane is going to be asked in about a year why she has got a copy of a court ruling and an office circular and four questions off a board, and the true answer is that a foreman in my workroom was wrong in the tenth month, and that is a good answer, and it is going to wear out, because I will be angry about it until I die and then there will be a girl here who has never heard of the tenth month and the shop will have a window that says *copying* on it and the reason there is a window is that a person can pay for a thing here."

---

The two that could not hold a register were not the same kind of problem, and Tamsin kept them apart in her book.

The woman in the hood has a schoolroom hand that turns every descending stroke into a small loop, and she made four of the twenty-six in the ninth month and a fifth in the twelfth, which is five of the forty-one, and she is entered on the list as a role, the way she was entered on the rota, because a name in a book is the thing she has spent four years not having.

Tamsin asked her, on the twenty-seventh, in the seed barn, whether she would be a keeper. Not a rota, not a duty: a person on a list that says she holds one set, in her own hand, of three documents, on a date, and that if anybody comes to the door with a paper the holder will read it out loud and give them the sheet with the four questions on it.

"No," said the woman.

"That is all right. I only had to ask."

"You did not have to ask. You asked because you wanted me to say it out loud in front of you so that you could write down that you asked." The woman in the hood had a good ear and a good head and had been a schoolmistress's daughter and had not been a schoolmistress. "Here is why I will not be a keeper. A keeper is a person somebody can ask. In four years a person will come to this lane and they will not ask me anything about a duty, because nobody knows my name. They will ask somebody who is written down. And they will be shown the list. And it will have *the seed-barn copyist* on it, and the woman at the table will say: that is the one in the hood, and that is a description of a person, and there are four women in the seed barns and I am the one who lives alone. And in four years there will be a name, and I will have gone where a person goes when they have got a name."

Tamsin Rook wrote it down exactly, in the fair hand, in the book, on the twenty-seventh of the twelfth month, and put the word *refused* against the line, and wrote under it: *and she is right, and the list is weaker for it, and the list is the only thing in this case that is not in a building.*

The foreman was easier and worse. Ostyn Vare had been audited on the seventh of the twelfth and had not answered any of the four letters Tamsin had written him, and she wrote a fifth on the twenty-eighth and did not send it, and wrote in her book instead: *Do not chase. A list of who holds a copy is not a list of people who have reported, and the day it is, it is a different instrument and it will be used as one.*

She had a full morning of not sending it, which is the most expensive kind of morning available to a person with nothing to do but post things.

---

The refusal she did not expect was from her own office, and it was offered kindly, and she turned it down in about nine seconds and then spent three days being uncomfortable about having turned it down in nine seconds.

The young clerk of the intake desk, who is about twenty-five and who has never in his life been asked to do anything wrong and who wrote a file number in ink and initialled it on the eighteenth of the ninth month, offered to get a quire out of the Chancery stores.

"Two hundred and forty sheets of the good stock," he said. "It is in the stores, it is not secret, and I can write it out and it will come out on the district cart with the next load, and you will not have to buy it, and there is a forms clerk in stores who owes me a favour because I did her filing in the eighth."

"Then it will come out on the district cart," said Tamsin Rook.

"Yes."

"From this office."

"Yes."

"And it will be in a list in this office. With this office's name on the cart, and this office's number on the note, and it will be in the stores book, and the stores book is kept for eleven years, and if anybody in this building in three years wants to know which forty-one households in two districts and three provinces were given copies of a court ruling by a roll-keeper of this court, there will be a book, and it will be a legitimate book, and the answer will be in it in a fair hand."

The young clerk looked at her for a second.

"You are saying you do not want them to know you did it."

"I am saying I do not want it to be possible for them to know I did it, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what I have learned in three months," said Tamsin Rook, "and I am not going to explain it to a man in a corridor twice."

He was, she thought afterwards, entirely right to be annoyed with her, and she did not entirely know how to put it right, and she went and asked him three days later whether he had a father, and when he said yes, asked whether the father read the papers at the counter, and he said yes, every day, and there the conversation ended, and she has never found out whether he understood it.

---

She wrote to Millbrook Reach on the twenty-eighth, and it cost two and fourpence, and the letter is the shortest thing she wrote that year: the three documents, the four questions, the name of the woman at the Mosswake court who keeps the hearing slate, and one sentence at the end that she rewrote three times, which was *I am not a court and I cannot do anything about your bond, and I am sending you this because you asked and because a person who asks should be answered even when the answer is that nobody can help.*

It came back on the ninth of the first month, and it came back to the lane, because a woman in a cooper's house three hundred miles down the water did not know what to do with a letter from a court and gave it to the first person who read to her.

Four lines, in the same hand as the first one, and Tamsin Rook read them standing up in a lane in the cold and had to sit down on the end of the trestle table to finish the fourth.

*That is the gauge gone, and a bond on it, and I cannot give you the paper because there is no paper. I asked the office and the man there was kind and he said the filing was in the routine column in the tenth month and it is not itemised and it is not a hearing, and it was not his doing and there is nobody to write to. My son cannot be examined for the trade without the gauge. My daughter is in the surety line of it and she is sixteen and she signed nothing, and I did not know she was in it until the man came about the gauge.*

There is no document. That is the thing Tamsin wrote in the margin of her own book in a lane in the cold, because it is the sentence the whole of the last three months has been walking toward without either of them knowing it: a district that has obeyed a form perfectly has not been made safe, it has been made quiet, and a person with no document has nothing to show anybody, not even the fact of the harm.

---

She wrote the list on the twenty-eighth of the twelfth month in her own book, over four pages, in her own hand, and it has no heading and no seal and no name at the top of it and no promise at the bottom of it, and it is four pages in a copyist's private book and it is the only record in the empire of where the proof of this case physically is.

Each line has six things on it. Who holds a copy. In whose hand. Of what — and there are only three whats, and there will not be a fourth, because the moment there is a fourth thing to hold it becomes a collection and a collection can be asked about as a collection. The date it was made. Whether the holder has said they will read it to anybody who comes to the house with a paper, and that column is the only column with a tick in it on the first page and the only column that matters.

And under the four pages, in the same hand, the rule, which is one sentence long and is not hers and is not Tamsin's and was given to her by a seventy-year-old woman at a seed barn who would not give her name either:

*Ask who read it to you, and do they get paid if you sign.*

The last thing she did before she closed the book was write a line under the list, and the line was not for anybody else, and reads: *I am the person who will be asked when one of these is wrong. That is the price of making it. I had expected to be asked for something else.*

And then she shut the book, and put it in the bottom of a chest under a winter coat, and went down to the lane, where the board was up and there were seven names in chalk and a woman from the seed barns reading somebody's marriage paper out loud in the cold without telling anybody in particular what to do about it, which is the whole of what this table is and is the reason the whole of it works.

On the last day of the twelfth month she wrote to thirty-eight of the forty-one to ask them to confirm nothing, because she had no address for three of them and did not write asking one. None of them had a copy of the list, because the list is not distributed; the list is what a person has in order to know who to write to, and if the list were in the world then the list would be a thing that could be taken.

Thirty-one answered inside a month. Thirty-one answered with a shilling or two, or with a correction, or with nothing at all but a signature.

Ten did not answer, seven of them having been written to and three having never been asked at all.

One of the seven was in Reed Cut, and one was a man who had moved, and one was a woman in Greyfen who had a house full of children and no candle, and four of the seven were in a furnace town four hundred and forty miles away, in a dyers' trade, in a house that had sent a copy of an endorsement to a lane in Mosswake in the tenth month on good paper with nobody's name on it.
