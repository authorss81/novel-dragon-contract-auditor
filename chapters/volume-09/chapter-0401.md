# Chapter 0401: Four Items On A Fee Board, About Four Hundred Blanks Under The Boards, And The Plainest Sentence She Has Said In Eleven Years

The board on the wall behind that counter carries four items and not one of the four is time. The drawer under the board is shut and has been shut for eleven years and there are four things inside it and two of them have never been opened. Under the far end of those boards, out of sight of the door, there is a box of about four hundred blanks that were bought in a quantity and never filled in by anybody, and nothing has ever been left in it, and no form in this empire has ever been drawn to account for it.

**One end of those boards has been hers for eleven years.** Her name is Marn Ottery, she is thirty-four, and she is not a lawyer, and there are three clerks on that floor and she is the oldest of them and the only one who has never left it. The other two are a woman of about twenty-nine at the far end of two joined tables and a man who comes in on the second of the two days and is not a lawyer either. **The box went under those boards eleven years ago and the lid has not been off it in six of those years**, and every week of the eleven the three of them have looked at the same end of the same boards and not one of them has known where a blank sold on that floor comes out of.

It is the second day of the first week of the second month of the year after the year after the year after the year after the year after next, and it is the fifth hour, and the counter shuts at the sixth hour.

---

The man from the yard was on the stairs at half past the fifth with a strap across him and a satchel hanging off it, and he has been going up and down those four flights longer than anybody at that counter has been standing at them.

He is a courier. **He put the satchel on the boards, took a folded thing out of it, set that on the boards as well, and did not take the bag off his shoulder until a woman at the far end of the two joined tables said one word at him.** Marn Ottery wrote the word at the top of the folded thing in the ordinary way and put the word and the number on the top of it and not on anything else.

A courier is a person who carries a thing and is not the person the thing is about and did not make it. **He could have been told what any of it was for and there is nowhere on that floor to tell him.** That is what a courier is for and it is why the trade has lasted as long as it has: he is cheap, he goes, nobody has to be shown anything, and if he were a party then the paper in that bag would have cost somebody a room to be served in. He has not asked her one thing in eleven years. He has set down on that counter more paper than the three of them have written between them, and he has never once asked what a thing was for, and he is not going to start.

Eleven years on a stair and the stair has had one sound in it for the whole of them, and the sound is a man who is not going to stop halfway. Nobody at the top of those four flights has ever heard him count and nobody has ever asked him to and he has never offered, and the not offering is the whole of the trade from where he stands, which is on a stair in a building where he knows one floor and the name of the man who works at the far end of it.

"You have still got that bag on," the clerk of about twenty-nine said, without raising her head.

He took it off. He said nothing, which is what he says.

---

The floor was quiet for the length of a page after that, and Marn Ottery put both hands flat on the boards, and the clerk of about twenty-nine put her pen down before the hands were flat, which she has done three times in eleven years and not once before this week.

"You are going to say it," the clerk said.

"I am not going to say it."

"Your hands are on the boards. I have watched you do that four times in eleven years and three of them were followed by a sentence, and I am not going to stand here and be the fourth thing that happens on that floor this morning."

"Then hear the order of it." Marn Ottery did not take her hands off. "I have said the shape of a thing out loud in this room three times and not one of you has asked me a single thing back, and I know exactly what I am about to do and I am telling you now so that you cannot stand there afterwards and say it took you by surprise."

---

The clerk of about twenty-nine got up off her stool, which she does not do before the sixth hour, and walked four paces along the two joined tables and stopped with her back half turned, which is the position of a woman who is staying and not the position of a woman who is agreeing.

"Do not say it in that voice," she said.

"That is the voice I have."

"I have listened to that voice three times and two of those were followed by eleven months of somebody at the top of four flights saying they did not know where a thing had come from. **You are the only person in this room who can say what one of those pieces of paper is for and that is not a compliment, it is a weight, and a weight does not get lighter by being picked up in front of witnesses.**" She turned round. "And I will tell you the thing I have not told the man who comes in on the second of the two days, and I will tell you now so that you know the cost of the next hour before you spend it."

---

"The fourth of those four is the one none of the three of us can account for," the clerk said. "**I have said the number of it out loud to two people in eleven years.** Once to the man who came in on the second of the two days, and once to a man in a house in this street in a week that is gone, and neither of them asked me one word about what it was for, and I have never since been able to work out whether the second of them heard me at all."

"That was a courtesy," Marn Ottery said.

"It was not." The clerk came back to the tables and did not sit down. "**It was not a courtesy because there is no form anywhere in which a person may be told what a thing on a board is going to be used for.** If there were one, the not asking would be a courtesy, because courtesy is what you do when there is a thing you could do and are choosing not to. There is no such thing, so the not asking is nothing. It is a shape that politeness makes when there is nothing to be polite with, and I have been making that shape in this room for eleven years and I have just now told you that I made it."

"I have wanted somebody to ask me about that fourth for eleven years," Marn Ottery said, "and I have not gone and found anybody, and that is the same arrangement from the other side and you have just said it out loud in a room where I cannot get it back."

"I know which side I am standing on. I have known for eleven years and so have you, and the reason it has gone this long is that neither of us ever said it where the other one could hear it."

The three of the four on the wall behind the counter can be accounted for, and the accounting is not difficult and has not changed in eleven years. Each of them has a name under it and a day of the week against it, and the day of the week has been the same one for as long as anybody working there can remember, and there is a person working there who could put her hand on any one of the three without looking up from the work.

The fourth of the four has no name under it and no day against it and nobody has ever been able to give a reason for either, and every one of the three of them has spent eleven years going at the wall and getting nothing off it.

It has never been claimed by one person and it has never been let go by one either. She said it out loud once to a man who came to a house in this street and never came to the floor, in a week that is gone, and Marn Ottery was in the room and did not hear a word of it, and neither of them has gone near it since, and the man who comes in on the second of the two days, who was in the building for the whole of the week when it was spoken, has the number and nothing else to go on. It belongs to the floor. It has belonged there for eleven years and it is not hers and it is not the clerk's and it is not the man's, and none of the three of them has ever written it down anywhere.

---

So she said it.

She took her hands off the boards and squared the edge of the folded thing the courier had left, which was not necessary and took four seconds, and then she said one sentence out loud in that room and it is the plainest sentence anybody has said on that floor in eleven years and it is not going to be said again.

"**A blank is a piece of paper with nothing printed on it, and it is the only paper in this empire that cannot be laid against anybody, and about four hundred of them a year go out of that counter in the hand of a person who is not doing anything anybody asked for, and not one of them has ever come back into this room with a heading printed over what was written on it.**"

Nobody thanked her.

"I am not going to be thanked for it," Marn Ottery said, before either of them had said anything at all, "and I want that entered in the room while it is in it, and I am not going to be thanked for it later either, and if either of you starts on it I will go back to the other end of these boards and stay there for the rest of the week."

---

The clerk of about twenty-nine sat down. She put her pen in the drawer on her own side, which is the end of the day for her, and it was the fifth hour and about twenty minutes, and she did not take it out again that week.

There was the better part of an hour between the sentence and the sixth hour and it went on as hours do. Not one word was said in any of it about what had just happened at the far end of the boards, and the arrangement that had held for eleven years did not break and did not mend, and by the sixth hour it was a different arrangement and nobody working there had done one thing to make it different.

"The number has been out loud in this room twice in eleven years," she said. "**It is out loud three times this morning, and you have just made the arithmetic of this floor a different number, and I want you to understand that I am the one who has to stand in it afterwards.**" She did not say thank you and she was not going to. "And the man who comes in on the second of the two days is going to look at the board tomorrow and see the same four items he saw yesterday and he is not going to know what the room is, and that is the thing you have spent."

"Tell him."

"I am not going to tell him." The clerk shut the second of the two joined tables down and stood with her hand on the edge of it. "**You do not get to give a thing to a person who has not asked you for it. That is the whole of what I have been saying to you for eleven years and I have only just found the words for it, and I have found them by refusing to let you have mine.**"

---

The counter shut at the sixth hour. Marn Ottery put the courier's folded thing in the ordinary place, and the drawer under the board was shut, and the four things were in it, and two of them have never been opened.

Under the far end of those boards the box stands where it stood at the fifth hour and the dust on the lid is the dust it was at the fifth hour, and nothing has ever been left in it. About four hundred of them a year go out of that counter in the hand of somebody, and not one of the three people on that floor has been told what a person does with one afterwards.
