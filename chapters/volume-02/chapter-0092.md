# Chapter 92: What a Sentence Is Worth at a Counter

The packet came down the morning of the seventeenth of the eighth month and had three things in it, and the third thing was a sheet with a seal on it and nothing at the foot of it, and it was the notice of the sitting of the eighteenth of the third month, and it is in this matter because a clerk of the Docket of Powers entered that the court had not been told a date, and entered it correctly, on the day, which nobody has yet thanked him for.

The first thing was an order of the court sitting in room four, made on the eighteenth of the seventh month, in five entries, with the presiding judge's name at the foot of it and a registrar's certification that said what certifications say, which is what it is and not what it means.

The second thing was a copy of the second finding of the district court of Auremar's lower district of the ninth of the eighth month, in a clerk's hand, with a magistrate's name at the foot of that, and a clerk's own line under it saying that a copy of a district court's finding is not a certified copy of anything and is not evidence in any proceeding and is good for reading.

Tamsin Rook read all three in about eleven minutes at her own bench in the copying alcove, and then she copied the order out in plain words, and read her copy back, and copied it again, and had the two copies and the original in front of her, and then she took the whole of it up the corridor to the room with the barred door, because the certified copy was going out of this matter to an office outside the Roll Hall and the undertaking of the fourth of the first month says that she brings him every sheet that goes out of this matter to a solicitor, a household, or an office outside the Roll Hall, and she has never once in fourteen months not brought it, and he has never once said whether he wanted it brought.

---

He read her transcription standing up, and then he read the certified original, which has a device on it and a seal and an impression and a witness's time, and then he said one thing and it took about nine seconds.

"You have written *cannot be a party, cited, or examined*."

"In the third entry. Yes."

"It says *is not a party, cited, or examinable*."

"That is the same thing."

"It is the same thing and it is a stronger thing," said Marek Kest, "and the difference is about four words and it is the whole of the difference between a rule and a result. *Is not examinable* is what the form says and it means a person does not exist to be examined. *Cannot be examined* is what I would say about a magistrate who has decided not to look at something, and it puts a decision in it, and a decision can be looked into. In nine years somebody will quote your copy, because your copy is the one in a plain hand that a person who cannot read a paragraph can be read, and it will be read out in a room by somebody who has never seen the original, and it will be read out with *cannot* in it, and the room will hear a court that has decided it will not look rather than a court that is saying there is nobody there to look at."

He put it down on the table.

"And that is a fact," he said. "Three times that undertaking has caught a word. This time it caught a fact, and I want that written down, because you have been waiting fourteen months for me to be able to say that to you and I do not think I have ever said it."

"It caught a fact because a fact is worth more," said Tamsin Rook. "A word is what you get when two people mean the same thing. That is a thing about words and it is worth about four pence a sheet to whoever has to read it out in a room in nine years."

She changed the four. She wrote *is not* back in four places and struck *cannot be* out in four places without inking it, so that in nine years a person could see that it had been there and had been taken out, and then she read the sheet back a fifth time.

---

The certified copy went down the road on the eighteenth at about the sixth hour, and a plain copy in a copyist's hand went into the tray on the same bench, and at about half past four on the nineteenth she stood at the end of the intake run in the third gallery and gave four of them away.

The four were different from the four in the fifth month and she wrote that down, because it is the kind of thing a person writes down or does not, and there is a column in a book with a heading on it and the heading is Given and the keeper of it is findable on purpose.

A man of about fifty who had come about eleven sheets and took one, and read the whole of it, standing, in about three minutes, and said: "That is a court telling me I have to describe it to him." "Yes," said Tamsin Rook. "That is what it is." "I have done that." "Then you are one of about four people in this city who knows that they have done it, and there is no register of the four and there is not going to be one."

A woman of about thirty with a folded paper in her sleeve who took one and did not open it and put it in her sleeve, and was not asked, because the rule is that nobody is asked anything.

A young man of about twenty-six who said, out loud, in a queue, to nobody in particular: "There is a name on this." "There is a name on the bottom of it," said Tamsin Rook, "and it is the judge's, and that is the correct order and it is the reason it is worth four pence. If your name is at the top of it then it is a document about you, and then it is worth about as much as a warrant and rather less, and I am not going to pretend to a stranger at the end of a counter that the two are the same paper."

And a man of about thirty-four who had a mark on a card of his own that he had not asked about, and who took a sheet and then asked her, in a low voice, whether anybody had ever traced one.

"Nobody has traced one," said Tamsin Rook. "There is a die in a drawer in the Kiln sheds and a number cut in the edge of it and the number is a number of a book in a press room off the Crown Kiln stair, and about nine hundred plates in a hundred years in this city were cut off that run and nobody ever looked at which. If you want to know whether that mark on your card is yours, the way to find out is to ask the woman who cuts plates, and I am telling you the way and not telling you the lane, and that is deliberate and it is not kindness, because the woman who cuts plates in that lane is the only person alive who can break a number on a plate into hers and not yours, and the day that becomes a fact in a file she is a person in a file, and she has spent eleven years arranging not to be one."

The man of thirty-four looked at her for about a second and then said, "That is the fourth time somebody has told me there is a lane they are not going to tell me about," and went up the run with the sheet in his hand.

---

The clerk of the district court of Auremar's lower district came up the road on the twentieth, at about the eleventh hour, and he had walked four miles and had a sheet in a case and no carriage money, and the question he asked in the corridor outside the alcove, in about four minutes, was the question a person asks when he has been sent for something and is not sure what it is.

"Can I put it in a minute."

"You can put in a minute that this court made an order on the eighteenth of the seventh month and that your court has regard to it," said Tamsin Rook. "That is a fifth of a line and it is the whole of what you are entitled to. You cannot put in that it binds you, because it binds nobody, and you cannot put in that the person before you is not affected by it, because in about four years somebody will say he is and you will have decided it in a minute in a chamber where nobody was."

He wrote it down. He is one of about nine clerks in this city who writes down what a person says to him at about the speed they say it, and the reason he came up the road instead of sending for a certified copy is that a certified copy takes six days and the thing he needed to know was whether he was allowed, and that does not take six days.

"Then what is it for," he said. "I have read it four times. It does nothing."

"It is for a person who has been given a paper and is not in the room where the paper is being used," said Tamsin Rook. "That is the whole of it. It does nothing, and what it does is that it means that if anybody puts a thing in front of you, you can stand up and say *I have not been asked about this, and here is a court that says I have not been asked about it until I am*, and somebody in the room has to write that down. **The sentence is not a thing that wins. The sentence is a thing that gives a person eleven seconds and a sentence to say, and about four hundred people in four offices and one trade have spent the last fourteen months learning that eleven seconds is the entire size of the gap.**"

"Eleven seconds."

"It is not even eleven seconds in practice," said Tamsin Rook. "It is the length of a person standing up. And it is the whole of what this matter has built, and I have spent a year making a sheet with two boxes on it that costs four pence and does not do anything at all, and the sentence on the paper you have in your case is the reason the sheet is worth giving away, and if the sentence goes then the sheet is a piece of paper and so is this."

She said the rest of it standing in the corridor, and it took about nine minutes, and she said it to him because he was there.

"And now the half of it that is going to be the hardest thing anybody in this matter has ever had to say out loud, and I have said it out loud to about two people in fourteen months, and you are the third, and you are a stranger, which is how I know I am saying it properly.

"**A finding that binds nobody who has not been heard is a sentence with no supply of hearings in it.** That court made a rule in a room and there is not one form in this empire for compelling a person to be brought into a room. There is no form for compelling a district court to hold a hearing, and no form for compelling a works to describe a thing, and no form for compelling a person to be told what is in a paper in his own hand. The order creates a duty to describe and a right to be heard and it supplies neither, and the supply has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is a clerk at a counter and a woman with a barrow and about nine hundred rooms that already exist and hold about four hundred people in them on an ordinary afternoon.

"That is what is left. Not a victory. A duty with no one standing at it."

The clerk wrote that down too, and then he did a thing she did not expect, which was that he put the certified copy back in the case, and said: "Then do not send me the certified one. I do not need it for a minute. I need the one in your hand for the counter."

"You cannot put that in a minute either."

"I know," he said. "But a person comes to my window and asks me what a paper means, and if I have nothing at all then I say I have nothing at all, and I have been saying that for four years, and my magistrate thinks I am a clerk who cannot find anything."

---

He had gone down the stairs by then and she was still standing in the corridor with the case in her hand, and the thing she was looking at was the second thing in the packet, and it had been in her own hand for eleven days and she had not looked at it properly since the day it was copied.

It is four lines long and it is the second finding of the district court of Auremar's lower district, made on the ninth of the eighth in a chamber, and it says that the order of the court sitting in room four of the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year does not by itself provide that a person is heard before the instrument takes effect upon them, and that a person who has been warned about a document is afraid of that document, and a person who is afraid of a document cannot get in front of it.

She had been treating that as a fourth sentence, and for eleven days she had not once put the two documents on the same table, and when she did put them on the same table, standing in a corridor, at about the tenth hour of the twentieth of the eighth month, it took her about nine seconds to see what she was looking at.

**They are the same sentence. One of them has got the first half of it and the other one has got the second half, and the two halves were made nine days apart in buildings four miles from each other, and the first half is the one a court of the jurisdiction made, and the second half is the one a magistrate of a district court made, and the one a court of the jurisdiction made is the one that does not work.**

A person may be heard. That is the first half. A person may be heard *before*. That is the second half. The first half has been on the page in four orders and a form and a sentence on a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river for fourteen months and it does not stop anything, because a person who has already been handed a paper has already been warned, and a person who is afraid of a paper cannot get in front of it, and so the first half is a door and the hinges are on the wrong side, and that is exactly what a dragon notary said from two hundred miles away in the sixth month, and he was right, and a court of the jurisdiction gave his House half an answer on the eighteenth of the seventh and gave it the half that is the useless half.

And the second half — the nine days — is in a minute book in a district, with a magistrate's name at the foot of it, and it is four lines long, and it is the half that would have made the first half work, and **nobody in either building has read the other, and the two documents have been eleven days in one corridor of this building on a tray and neither of them has ever been on the same table until this afternoon.**

She stood there for about a minute and a half and then she did the only thing she could think of, which was to write the date of each of them on the back of a sheet of foolscap and put the two dates side by side and write the word *why* against the gap, and the word she wrote was: **because the low half was found by a woman who went and looked.**

And then she went and got Marek Kest out of the room with the barred door, and read him both, out loud, standing, in about four minutes, and he did not interrupt her once, which she has written down as the day the undertaking of the fourth of the first month was worth having and as the day she understood what it was for.

---

She wrote it out at the end of the day in the book with no office on it, and it is the last page of it for the month, and the finding is four lines and it is the reason she did the thing she did and it is worth having in her own hand.

> *The eighteenth of the eighth month, at about the ninth hour, in the alcove. There are four sentences in this book. I have counted them four times since the ninth of the eighth and I am not going to count them again, and the counting is finished, and I want the reason written down in a place that is mine while I still have the energy for a true sentence.*
>
> *One. The third of the fourth month: a question asked and answered, entered and not a finding. Two. The second of the seventh: a die is a mirror. Three. The twenty-eighth of the seventh: a plank and a broken padlock. Four. The ninth of the eighth: a person may be heard before an instrument takes effect upon them.*
>
> *Four in fourteen months and not one of them has paid anybody, and that was true on the ninth of the eighth and it is truer today, because today there is a fifth one and it is the best of the five, and I am not going to write it in this book, and here is the reason, and I have been about two days finding it.*
>
> *The four are mine. I copied them or I was in a room for them or I was at a counter when they were said. The fifth is a copy at a counter like any other, of a document made in another building by people I have never spoken to, and the only thing I did to it was rule four sheets and hand them out at four pence, which is what I do with everything.*
>
> ***And a copy at a counter in a copyist's hand is a document about the copyist.*** That has been the instrument of this whole matter for fourteen months and I have used it on other people about nine times and I have never once turned it on myself, and today there is a sheet going round a district with my handwriting on it and in about four years a man with a tray is going to ask a person in the lower district who put these four sheets on that counter, and the answer is going to be my name, and it will be in a book with my own initial against it in my own book, and the book has no office on it and is not in anybody's custody and I keep it myself.*
>
> *So the fifth sentence is not entered here. It is the best one anybody has written in fourteen months and I am not putting my name next to it, and that is not modesty and it is not a refusal, and I have checked: it is the same reason I took the custody of forty-one pages under a form with **None** in the office box, and it is the same reason the column has a heading and my initial against every line in it, and it is the same reason a column kept by somebody nobody can find is a column that can be denied.*
>
> *I am going to keep it. I am not going to be able to make the next one either, and I said that on the ninth of the eighth and I meant it, and what I actually meant was not that I had run out of sentences. It is that the next one is not going to be mine, and I have found out this month what the difference is, and it is that the next one is going to be written by a person in a district who has never heard of this room, and my whole business for a year has been to make that happen, and it is happening, and I am not in it, and that is the correct outcome and it looks exactly like nothing happening.*
>
> *— T. Rook, the eighteenth of the eighth month, at about the ninth hour. My own book. No office at the top of it and I have not put one there, and today that sentence cost something and I want it entered with the day.*
