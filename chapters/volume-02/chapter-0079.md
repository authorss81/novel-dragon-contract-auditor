# Chapter 79: What a Caught Person Is Worth

The examination of the man convicted on the second of the second month was taken in the second week of the fourth month, in a room with two windows, and it is forty-one pages long, and about four hundred people have read it, and every clerk in this city has read it, and every certifier in every trade in the empire has read it, and not one person in it has asked the man for the name of a client and one district court has entered that it has not asked and will not.

What came out of it is a practice.

It is eleven lines long and it is not remarkable and that is why it is eleven lines. It says that the mark is a way of saying *this is one of ours* to somebody who already knows what ours is; that a mark is not a signature; that a signature says who did a thing and a mark says which of a set of people did it; and that a set of people is a thing that can be counted.

Pyle read it on the second of the fifth month at about the sixth hour and put it face down on his bench and did not talk to anybody for about a minute and a half, and then he went and cut a plate.

He has a press he does not own and a bench he made and about four hundred certificates, sixty-one of which were suspended on the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year, and a room on the first floor over a yard in the Kiln sheds with a window that looks at about nine feet of another building. He is not sorry and has said so in the first ten minutes of every conversation anybody in this book has had with him, and he is not going to be talked out of it, and that has now been tested from both sides in about five weeks.

The plate he cut on the second of the fifth month was nothing to do with any of that. It was a client's, and it was the fourth in a week, and it is the ordinary work of a man with a press he does not own.

The room he is going to is four streets away and he walked it, because it is four streets.

---

The man is a certifier of about sixty. He is not a villain and he does not think he is one and it is of no interest to anybody that he does not. He has held a certificate in his trade's book since a year he will not give, under a client's name, in a town whose name is not on this page, and the client's name is a name and not a mark, and he put it in a form of his own on about the first of the fifth month and sent it to a district court in this city, and it is in a file, and a clerk has read it and written it down.

He is not sorry. He gave that up in about four minutes and gave two things he is sorry about in an order and asked that the order be kept, which is Pyle's own condition and Pyle has been the other side of it.

"He is worth about four pence a sheet," said Garren Pyle, out loud, in a room with two windows, at about the seventh hour of the second of the fifth month, to nobody.

"Four pence and a clerk's half hour," said the clerk of the district court who had brought him, "and it went in at the second hour and I had it read back and initialled at the fourth, and I am telling you the half hour because I have written it in the column and somebody in nine years is going to read the column and want to know what a name cost, and it is half an hour and four pence and it is the only figure in the matter that anybody in it can be honest about."

"He is worth that in the city. He is worth rather more than that outside it and I will tell you why in a minute."

Pyle did not say it in a minute. He sat down on the wrong chair, because there are two chairs in that room and one of them is for the person who has been caught and Pyle has never in forty years sat on it and did not know it was a different chair until the clerk said *you can move it if you like, nobody has ever sat in it*, and he did not move it.

"He has done one thing and it is a small thing and it took a month of somebody else's evidence to get it out of him, and if anybody in this room thinks that he is now the source, he is not. He is the end of it. He is a man of sixty who has been in a trade for thirty-four years and who wrote a name in his own book because a client asked him to, and a client is a person with a business, and there is nothing in the world that a person with a business will not put a name to a certificate about." Pyle put his hands flat on his knees. "About nine of us learned what a mark is for in one week in the second month. That was me and eight others and a woman of sixty in a lane who asked which number was on a plate. And in the last three weeks about forty more have learned what a name is for, and they learned it the same way I did, off a piece of paper, and none of the forty learned it off him and about six of them learned it because of him and they are not going to put that in their books."

Then he said the thing he had come to say, which he had been carrying for four days and had written out once and crossed out.

"A name is worth about a plate. That is the price, and the price is public, and it was set on the ninth of the second month in a registrar's note by a man who meant no harm by it: a matrix is not a ledger, and a tool is a thing anybody can buy. He was talking about steel. He was talking about me, and he was right, and it turns out he was talking about all of us.

"Everything we hold in this trade is a plate. A mark, a matrix, a number cut in an edge, a rule, a form with a box. We have spent thirty-four years learning to say *this is one of ours* without saying who. And a district court has now said out loud, in a room, that it does not know what house keeps the mark, and about four hundred and forty people have read that, and the number of people who can read a mark is the number of people who could tell you, and there is nothing in the empire that stops us reading one at each other, and one person has just handed in the only thing any of us held that was a name, and he got four pence and half an hour, and about forty people now know exactly what the forty of them are worth.

"That is not a leak. A leak is when a thing comes out that was meant to stay in. That is a price list."

---

He went out at about the eighth hour and he walked four streets and then four doors, and the four doors are the four he told in the second month of last year, and three of those four have about a hundred and forty years between them of being asked to produce everything.

He did the same thing at each. He said what the caught man handed in, in the order he handed it in. He said what the examination said. He said the price: four pence and half an hour, and it is in a column with a figure in it and the figure is honest. And then he said the true thing, which is that nobody is coming for them and there is nothing yet, and that a thing nobody is coming for is a thing a person is free to keep.

The first man said thank you and shut the door.

The second man said nothing at all and Pyle stood on the step for about a minute and a half and then wrote *the second* in his own book and went on.

The third said he would think about it, and Pyle wrote that down too, and then the third man said, after he had shut the door and then opened it again about four inches, "Mr Pyle. If I hand it in, does anybody come to my house."

"Nobody comes to anybody's house in this," said Garren Pyle. "That is the price list as well. I will not lie to you about that and there is no reason to."

"Then I'll think about it," said the third man, and shut the door, and it was the same words, and Pyle wrote *the third, and the third asked a question first, and that is worth writing down too*.

The fourth was not at home and Pyle left nothing and did not knock twice.

---

Here is what Pyle did at about the eleventh hour, alone, in a room over a yard in the Kiln sheds, and it is the whole of what this chapter is for and it took him about nine minutes.

He wrote out four lines.

> *Four doors on the second of the fifth month, on foot, at the eighth hour. One said thank you and shut the door. One said nothing. One said he would think about it and then asked a question and then said it again. One was not at home and I left nothing and did not knock twice.*
>
> *I have written which is which. This list is in a book of my own with no office on it. It is four doors in a city of about forty thousand people and in nine years nobody will be able to get at it, and in nine years if somebody does get at it the first thing they will do with it is not find out about a plate.*
>
> *A man who is caught is a quantity. He is worth four pence and half an hour. He is not a source and he is not a witness and he is not a man with a name above him, and nobody in this trade is going to be found because he wrote one down, and anybody who says he was a source has understood the last fifteen months in the wrong order.*
>
> *And there are four or five of us who were not caught, and we know. We have known since the ninth of the second month, when a registrar said out loud in a room that the court does not know what house keeps the mark. We did not have to be told what we are worth. We have been able to work it out since the second month. The only thing that has happened in the last three weeks is that a number of about forty other people have been able to work it out as well, and the only person who has been harmed by that is the man of sixty, who is owed something by all of us and will be given nothing.*

He read it back and did not cross it out, and then he wrote a sixth line, which is the reason he wrote the other five.

> *A name is the only thing a person has that is theirs. Not a plate, which is a tool anybody can buy, and not a mark, which is a direction, and not a rule, which is a bench that answers for you. A name. And nothing in this book or in this empire can make a man put his name down, and the reason is not a legal rule and I have been looking for the legal rule for three weeks and there is not one. The reason is that he has worked out what it is worth. There is no office that can put that back. There is no form. A man who has worked it out will simply not write it, and he will be right not to, and every trade in the empire now knows he is right not to, and the four or five of us who have not handed in are the most protected people in this building and we did it by not writing anything down.*
>
> *Which means the instrument that finds people — a document with a man's name at the top of it — does not work on a man who will not write his own name, and I have been sharpening it for fourteen months and it is a good instrument and it is stopped by one word from a man of sixty at a counter in his own trade, and there is no appeal from a word.*
>
> *G. Pyle, certifier, the second of the fifth month, at about the eleventh hour, at the bench, with the stove out.*

---

The third of the fifth month he went down the Kiln row to a counting house, which has been on that row longer than the Kiln and which takes every packet that comes into the city and which had a copy of a high court's record sitting on its counter from the sixth of the seventh month of last year to a date nobody has written down, unused and unsold and unshown, which was the most frightening answer anybody in this book produced in Volume 01 and which is still the most frightening one.

He did not go in. He stood across the road for about a quarter of an hour.

There is a mark on the sign over the door of a counting house. It is the ordinary mark of a counting house: a filled square with a bar through it, cut in the board and filled with white, and it has a notch out of the top left corner, and it has been on that board for about ninety years, and it means *this is one of ours* to a person who already knows what a counting house is, and it carries a direction, and a direction is the one thing in the world that does not need anybody's permission to be followed.

It is a mark and it is not a name, and it is not a house's name, and it is not anybody's client, and no court in this book has ever known what it is or who keeps it or what it is for, and it is going to be on that board in nine years and in ninety.

On the counter of the counting house there was a girl of seventeen who copies, at a penny a sheet, for a chandler two streets off, and she is called Bryn Mawer and she is not on the bench and was not among the eleven protected people and is a line in a file and has no wages at that counter at all.

She had a bundle of about nine sheets of her own on her own time on the third of the fifth month, and about thirty-one that she knew of, and the house knows she made them, and she has a page of a list of four pages in a hand that is her own, and the four pages do not agree and the disagreement is written on all four, and the sixth column, the one headed *why not*, is empty and has been empty for nine months and is going to be empty for ever because she will not invent a *why not*.

Garren Pyle stood across a road and looked at a mark on a board for about a quarter of an hour and worked out, in about four seconds, standing in the road, that he had told four doors in four streets and had not gone into a building on a street where a mark is on a board and a girl of seventeen is on the counter, and that the reason he had not was that he is a man who is not sorry and who counts plates, and that the reason he had gone to the four doors was that they are four doors and a mark on a board is not a door.

He wrote that down that night, in his own book, with the day on it, and it is the last line in it.

> *The fourth of the fifth month. I stood across a road for a quarter of an hour and did not cross it, and the reason is that the mark on that board is not a door, and a man who counts plates goes to doors, and a girl of seventeen on that counter has a page of a list and knows what a copy is, which is a weapon and a target, and she is seventeen and has no trade and nobody who will stand in a room with her, and if I had gone in there I would have been a man in a good coat in a counting house asking a girl of seventeen about a mark, and about nine people learned what a mark is for in a week in the second month and she was one of the nine and she learned it by asking which number was on a plate, and she does not need me and I am not going to go in there.*
>
> *There are four or five of us not caught and we know what we are worth and we did it by not writing anything down. A mark on a board is not a name and is never going to be one, and the day somebody in this trade decides that a mark can be run back to a person from a mark is the day about forty people who learned something in the last three weeks find out what the price list is for, and it will not be four pence, because a name is four pence and a mark is not a name.*
>
> *G. Pyle, the fourth of the fifth month, at about the ninth hour, at the bench.*
