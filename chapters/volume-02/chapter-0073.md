# Chapter 73: What a Sentence Is Worth at a Window

It came down on the morning packet of the eighteenth of the third month, which is a day earlier than the river usually gives things away, and it was a transcription in a copyist's hand of a high court's answer, and it had five words on the front of it and no office and no name:

*for the wall. read it twice. — T. R.*

Ivet Sarn read it twice on the morning it came, at the window, with the queue behind her, and then she put it under the counter and finished the queue, and then she read it a third time, because the second time is when you find out whether you have understood it or only read it.

It is one side. It says, in effect, that a reading by a person who was not on a register is of no legal effect whatever, and that the record the reading was entered in is a record of a court and is not altered by that, and that every act done in reliance on it stands, and that no person who acted on it in good faith is to be questioned for having done so.

The fourth point is the one that took the rest of the morning, and she wrote it out in her own book in her own hand before she wrote anything else, because she is a clerk and a clerk who has understood a thing writes it down before she tells anybody she has understood it.

> *Eighteenth of the third month. A high court has said that the letters this court sent to about sixty households in the sixth year were proper, and that nobody in this office may be questioned about them, and that the record in which the sentence was applied stands and is not to be disturbed.*
>
> *I have been waiting nine years for a sentence that would put somebody in a room. I have got a sentence that closes a room from the outside and puts a lock on the handle, and it has been done by people who did nothing wrong in a room with forty people in it, and they are not to be questioned, and they should not be, and I would not want them to be.*

Then she went and got the wall.

---

The wall at the Mosswake Roll Court is not a wall. It is the boards either side of the clerk's window, three of them, and they have been painted so many times that the frame is the only original part, and they carry, in the order they were put up:

The head of them, in chalk, in letters about four inches high, which anybody may read from the queue without coming to the sill:

> **THIRTY-ONE OF ABOUT SIXTY. Eighteen returns did not come back. Six copies were never made, by a clerk who was not asked to and did not, and I have her kitchen and the date and her word for it, and she is sixty-one and has a child and is alive. Nobody has been asked to do anything about this and nobody is going to be until a person with a bar comes to this window and asks.**

Under that, the register of pending duties in five columns, going back years, in her own hand, ruled by hand, at a shilling a copy.

And then the paragraphs. There are eleven of them now, and each has a number against it and no name, and she has never once put her own name at the foot of anything on that wall and has been asked about it twice by people who wanted to know whether it was safe to copy.

Paragraph one is what the district has to do about the Cinder Court's record, which is almost nothing, and says so.

Paragraphs two to seven are the rest of the same thing, broken up, because in the fourth week of the sixth month she wrote it as one long paragraph and a man in a good coat read the whole thing standing up in about eleven minutes and she has thought since that a person standing at a sill has about eleven minutes and no more, and so she broke it up.

Paragraph eight is the rule about the hearing slate.

Paragraph nine, in her own hand, dated in the writing *the twenty-eighth of the ninth month*:

> *Nine years and I have never once gone and looked at an outgoing rack. The sixty were in it eight feet from where I work. That is not the district's fault and it is mine.*

Paragraph ten is the transcription of the Cinder Court's first order, in a copyist's hand, with five words on the front of it, which came down the river on the second of the second month.

And paragraph eleven, which she put up on the nineteenth of the third month, between the fourth hour and the eighth, in about forty minutes, and which is the whole of what this chapter is for.

She wrote it in her own hand and she wrote it as a person and not as a court, which is the rule she set herself in the fourth week of the sixth month and has not broken.

> *Eleven. A court has answered a question that was asked in the third week of the eleventh month, on the eighth of this month, and the answer is one side, and it is this. A reading performed by a person who was not entered on a register is of no legal effect whatever. And the record the reading was entered in is a record of a court and is not altered by that, and stands, and every act done in reliance upon it stands, and nobody is to be questioned for having done it in good faith.*
>
> *Here is what that does for the thirty-one on this board, in plain words, because a person standing at this sill has about eleven minutes and it would be a cruelty to make them find it out.*
>
> *It does not put anybody in a field. It does not put one pound back. It does not make one household whole. There is no money in it and there is no land in it and there is no work in it, and the thirty-one households on this board are exactly as they were on the fourth of the first month, and that is the whole of what a court saying a record stands does for them.*
>
> *It does one other thing, and I want it on the wall in a hand anybody can check, and the hand is mine. The record that stands is the only record in this empire in which a girl of fifteen is entered as heard. She signed nothing. She was in a room in a shift with a knife and a loaf and she said no, and her mother asked for the opposite of what she wanted, and the court wrote down that she was heard, and that line is now on a standing record with a judge's name at the foot of it and it cannot be taken out and nobody will ever be able to say again that she was not heard. That is real and I am not going to stand in front of this window and pretend it is nothing.*
>
> *And in the same record, in a different hand, in a column with the wrong heading on it, there is a household of the lower district entered as a person from whom a return has been requested, because she answered a form. Asked. Not liable. And that line stands now too, and nobody can un-ask a person, and the only name of hers in the empire says she was asked a question and does not say who has to pay for the answer.*
>
> *And here is the part I have to say and would rather did not, and I am putting it on a board in a district of four hundred and sixty households where a man in a good coat has to stand and read everything before he reaches my sill. This court wrote those letters. It applied a sentence in letters and without a hearing to about sixty households in the sixth year and could not get their names and did not ask. This morning a court has told this district that the letters were proper and that nobody in this office may be questioned about them. The harm is not finished. The harm has been made permanent, and it has been made permanent by a thing that was said in a room by people who did nothing wrong, which is the only way a harm like this is ever made permanent.*

She stood back from the board and read it, and then she read paragraph two, because she always reads paragraph two after she has written something new, to check that she has not accidentally contradicted a thing she wrote in the fourth week of the sixth month.

She had not.

Then she went to the press in the embankment corridor, which is where the press has been since the fourteenth of the second month, off the third flagstone, and which is not a cold floor any more but is a corridor with a press in it and a window that does not open and a smell of wet cardboard in the third month that she has got used to and her clerk has not.

There are three bundles on the oak shelf in the order of the quarters, and there have been three bundles on that shelf since the second month, and there will be three bundles on that shelf for ever, because the fourth cycle of the sixth year was never copied and there is no fourth clerk and there has not been a fourth clerk in four years and a district that has not had a fourth clerk in four years is not going to be given one by a fire in a ceiling.

She stood in the corridor for about four minutes with her hand flat on the top bundle, and the hand was not doing anything, and then she went back to the window because there were nine people in the queue and two of them had come from the far end of the embankment.

---

She sold a copy at a shilling and she read it at the sill, and this is the part of the job she has been doing for nine years and has never once found anybody else willing to do.

A copy is a shilling. It is not a price that is written anywhere; it is in the fifth column of the register of pending duties, which is the column that says a copy was sold, and it is the reason everything on that board costs a shilling a copy, and the fifth column has been there since before she came and it is the only column in that building that anybody has ever been able to explain to a stranger.

She read the eleven paragraphs to a man of about fifty who had come about a duty of his own and stayed, and then to two women who had come about a duty and one of whom stayed, and then she read the first order again to a woman who had asked for it in the ninth month and had not been charged the first time and paid now without being asked to.

Then the shilling went in the box and the sale went in the fifth column, in her hand, against the date, and she read that back to herself because she reads everything back to herself and the clerk has stopped remarking on it.

The whole of the morning came to about eleven shillings, which is about a fifth of what it cost the district's fund to do nothing this month, and she wrote that down too, because she is that sort of a clerk and because it is a fact.

---

At the third hour of the twenty-first of the third month, when the queue has gone and the light on the embankment has gone flat and the clerk has gone to the back room for a cup of something, a woman of sixty came up to the sill.

She is Iolanthe Rees, of the two strips above the upper lane, and she has paid a hundred and forty pounds of ground rent in that district in twenty years, and she is one of the thirty-one, entered on the fourth of the first month with a date and a door and the word *told* and *next review: none named* in the fourth column.

And she was not alone.

With her was a woman of about the same age who lives on the strip above and whose name Ivet Sarn took down because she is a clerk and she is going to write it in a book, and it is a name and there are two thousand names in this building and it is not on a wall and it is not on a board.

"Will you be seeing me in a room."

The voice came out of the second woman first, and Ivet Sarn has thought about that since, and the reason she has thought about it is that the woman who had come in the first month had asked the question herself, alone, at the third hour, having walked up at a time when nobody could see her, and this time she had brought somebody and the somebody had asked it.

That is the whole of the difference and it took four seconds to see and about nine days to write down.

"Sit down, Iolanthe Rees," said the clerk at the window, which is what she says and which she has said to about four hundred people in nine years and which means nothing at all and is the only courteous thing available.

"I am not here to be spoken to kindly," said Iolanthe Rees. "I am here about the same thing as in the first month and I have brought a witness because in the first month I asked it into a window with nobody standing behind me and I have thought about that for ten weeks and I have decided it was a foolish thing to do and that a person should not ask a question like that alone. So I have brought her."

"Your answer is the same," said the clerk.

"Then say it again, because she has not heard it and she has paid ground rent in this district for nineteen years and she is entitled to hear a thing said."

---

The answer was the same.

Nobody in this office had asked that she be called; no slate carries her name; the court has ordered nothing; and the clerk cannot promise what any other office or any other court will do, and says so when it is asked, and says in the book that she has been wrong four times in twelve months and does not intend to stop saying it.

The reason is the reason that has changed, and the clerk gave the new one first, out loud, at the sill, because the old one had stopped being true and it would have been dishonest to give a reason that was no longer the reason.

"The answer to *will you be seeing me in a room* is no," she said. "And the answer to *why not* used to be: because nobody has asked. In the first month that was true and it was the best answer this window had, because it was an answer that could be changed by one person in one room. I wrote it in your entry and I took a shilling for it and I meant every word."

"That was on the sixth of the first month," said Iolanthe Rees.

"That was on the sixth of the first month."

"And now?"

"Now it is because a court has said, in a room in this city, with a name at the foot of it, that the record this office keeps of that year stands and is not disturbed and that nobody in this office is to be questioned for having acted on it." The clerk put her hand flat on the counter, which she does. "So there is no longer anybody who *can* ask you, and that is not the same as nobody asking, and I am telling you the difference because I have got four months to tell you the difference in and I have decided to start now and not at the counter when it is too late to be useful."

"So it is finished."

"No," said the clerk. "It is not finished and I am not going to let you leave this window believing it is, because if you leave believing it is finished then you will not come again, and you are one of thirty-one and there are about two hundred and seventy other households in this district and one day I am going to need more than one person to have come twice."

She read the answer aloud again to the second woman, at the sill, from the top of the board, and then she read paragraph eleven, and then she read the two numbers, which are the last thing on the wall and which she had put up on the nineteenth and which is paragraph eleven's second half and which she reads differently depending on who is standing there.

"Two numbers, and they are both correct, and they are not the same number and they cannot be made the same number and it is nobody's fault and everybody gets this wrong.

"Three hundred and eighty-eight is a number of times a bar went up and down in four weeks. That is all it is. It is a number of a gate. If you want to know how many people live on the far side of some water it is not that.

"Four hundred and eleven is a number of people. That one came out of a district engineer's own answer, out of the market and the wharf and the mill and the chapel, and a man added them up and the sum is right. It is on nobody's return. If you want to know how many crossings a bar makes, it is not that.

"The day somebody adds one of them to the other one, somebody is going to be hurt, and it will not be an error. It will be a decision, made quickly, by a person with a good head and a bad afternoon. So both of them go on the wall, with the words under each of them saying what it is a number of, and the copy costs a shilling, and the shilling is because the fifth column has to mean something."

The second woman asked whether four hundred and eleven included the mill girls.

"It includes whoever the engineer counted, and he counted what a return told him to count, and I have not seen the return, and it is a number of what somebody counted and not a number of what is there," said the clerk, "and I am not going to tell you it is the same number. I have told a copyist in a corridor in this city in the last three weeks that I do not know what that number is a number of, because I did not, and I have been a clerk for nine years and it is the first time in nine years I have said that about a figure I had been carrying."

She took a shilling from each of them, and she entered both sales in the fifth column, and she read the entries back, and then she closed the shutter half way, which is what she does at the end of a day.

---

At about the eighth hour she wrote in her own book, and this is the part that is on the page because a clerk's book is the only place in that building where what she actually thought is kept.

> *Twenty-first of the third month, the third hour, and the second time.*
>
> *They came together. That is the change and I have written it down and I am not going to dress it up. In the first month she came alone and she asked into a window with nobody behind her because she has paid ground rent in this district for twenty years and she would not be the woman on the board. Today she brought a woman from the strip above and the second woman asked the question first.*
>
> *I have been running a count since the fourth of the first month of the people who come to this window at an hour when nobody can see them, and it is eleven weeks old, and it is on the board, and it is the number I am proudest of, and I have just worked out in about four seconds that the number is going to stop meaning anything, because a person who brings a witness does not have to come at the third hour. She still came at the third hour. She will keep coming at the third hour. But she did not have to, and that is the whole of what a paragraph on a wall has done in nine years, and it took a court to do it and not a clerk.*
>
> *What I have got, and I am writing it plainly because I am not going to have a better week. Paragraph eleven is the best paragraph on that wall. It is true, it is checkable, and it is about four hundred words of plain words, and it has been on that wall two days and four people have read it to the end, and not one household has been put back in a field, and the thirty-one are exactly where they were, and the fourth column still says next review: none named thirty-one times and there is still nothing to review, and the district has still not decided whether to run a process and I have not asked the magistrate and I will not be asked.*
>
> *Restitution is not begun. I cannot order it, this court cannot order it, and I have known that since the fifth month of last year and I have written it on that wall since, and a sentence from a high court in this city has not changed one word of it.*
>
> *And the answer she came for has not changed, and the reason has, and I gave her the new reason at the sill before I gave her the old one, because the old one was no longer true and I would rather be caught out than comfortable.*
>
> *Eleven shillings today. I.S., the twenty-first of the third month, at about the eighth hour, at the back of the window, with the shutter half shut.*
