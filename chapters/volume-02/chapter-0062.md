# Chapter 62: Reason Not Known

The Inheritance Desk of the third gallery has one window, two presses, a rule about a hand's width from the edge, and a habit that has been there since the desk was made, which is that whatever comes in at eleven goes out at four, and whatever comes in at four is left until the next morning, and nobody has ever written that down and everybody works by it.

Halvard Riet had been at that desk for nine years and had asked, in the twelfth month of last year, for the Venn box and not the Kest one, and had been given the Kest one as well, and had taken it on purpose, and has never told anybody he took it on purpose, and the Registrar who gave it to him had not asked.

The Kest box was on the second shelf of the press under the window and it had been on a shelf of some kind since the eighth month of the year the fire happened, and it was in his charge now, and he was not going to open it, and he had not opened it, and he had gone over the label eleven times in nine days.

There is a handover list. There is always a handover list, and it is six items, and it is in Marek Kest's hand, and it has no heading on it, and it came to the desk with the box on the twelfth of the eighth month and has been on the inside cover of the day's book since, and Riet had read it on the first morning and had read it about nine times since and had never once shown it to anybody.

*One. The comparison request of the fourth of the second month, asked for twice, not signed, not answered. It is not mine after the twelfth of the ninth of last year. — M.K.*

*Two. The source leaf is in the cabinet and I am not the key. — M.K.*

*Three. A request of hers, filed over her own signature on the ninth of the fourth month, naming a district and not an office. It is unanswered. Do not answer it for her. — M.K.*

*Four. It is not mine. It is hers. Do not chase it for me and do not tell her it was chased. — M.K.*

*Five. The Venn file cannot be made complete. The second box is blank and is entered as complete so, and the reason is entered, and the reason is that the other party is dead. Do not go looking for a second box. — M.K.*

*Six. Shut. A second decision not to open it and the reason is in the third week of the eleventh month. There is no third. — M.K.*

Item three was answered.

Riet found that out on the nineteenth of the first month, at about the fourth hour, in the middle of a morning when he was trying to get ninety-one leaves of copy certified before the second of the second month, and he found it because the morning post had come into the gallery an hour and a half late and the clerk of the intake desk had brought it up and put it on the counter and said *that one is a roll-keeper's and it has been on this counter since the eleventh of the ninth and it is not addressed to this desk, and I do not know what to do with it, and I have written the time on it.*

It was two thirds of a page and it was signed A. Kesk, clerk, of the district court of Glasswater, and it was dated the third week of the ninth month, and it said that the standing instructions of that district say nothing about itemising, and that the district has held a flood docket since the second year, and that this court has never had a flood-season continuity filing entered in it, not one in six years, and that the itemised filing of the tenth month that is in a provincial record was entered in a river court of the same district, which is a different office.

Riet read it twice, which is not his habit, and then he put it face down on the counter, because it was addressed to a person and not to a desk, and then he turned it back up, because turning it face down was the same as deciding something.

It had been on the counter of the intake desk since the eleventh of the ninth month. Four months. It had come in on the same packet as the custody form that Tamsin Rook signed the same morning, and it had gone into a drawer in the copying alcove at about the same time she had entered two of another person's acts into a register in her own hand.

Item three said: *It is unanswered. Do not answer it for her.*

It had been answered on the eleventh of the ninth month by the person it was addressed to, and nobody in this gallery had ever been told, and the desk had carried a line saying it was unanswered for four months in a book that four other clerks had open and closed every working morning.

---

He did not chase it and he did not tell Marek Kest that he had read it, and he wrote down the reason for that in his own copy of the list, and the reason was not only that he had been told not to.

The reason was that a line on a list that says *unanswered* is a kind of protection. It is a reason for a person in four years to pick it up. Nobody has ever picked up a line that says *answered* and has not been asked why nobody was told. If he had put the Glasswater letter in the file on the nineteenth of the first month, then in four years somebody reading that file would come to the third item and find a note against it in a different hand saying *answered, on the counter, four months*, and the only question anybody would ask that note is why a clerk of this gallery let a letter stand on a counter for four months addressed to a person who works two galleries away.

So he wrote six lines and put them in an envelope and carried it down himself to the copying alcove and gave it to her, and did not leave it on a counter.

> *Miss Rook. — Item three of the list came into this gallery in the post at about the fourth hour of the nineteenth of the first month. It is your answer of the third week of the ninth month, from the district of Glasswater, and it was on the counter at the intake desk from the eleventh of the ninth until this morning, and the intake clerk has written the time on it in ink and initialled it and does not know what he ought to have done with it.*
>
> *I have not answered anything and I have not chased anything. I have read it because it was in my post and I am a clerk and the post is what comes.*
>
> *I am writing to you about it because the list says it is unanswered and it is not, and a list that says a thing is not so is worse than a list that is empty, and I would rather be the man who put the correction in than the man a person found out from somebody else.*
>
> *There is no answer wanted from me and I am not asking you for one.*
>
> *H. Riet, Inheritance Desk, the nineteenth of the first month.*

She read it standing up, which is how she reads everything, and then she said, "You have broken the rule we made," and Halvard Riet said, "I have not read the rule and I do not know that there is one, and if there is one then I have broken it and you may put that in your book, and I would like you to put it in your book, and I would like it in your own hand and not mine."

And Tamsin Rook wrote it down in her own book in her own hand, and he read it back before he went, because she made him.

---

The copy was the reason he had not slept much and it was the whole of the work of that week.

A gallery does not keep a broker's original. It keeps a **copy**, made leaf for leaf by a clerk of the gallery in the sixth year, bound, with the wrapper it was made from kept inside the back board, because the wrapper is where the count is. That is the whole practice. The paper is a copy and it is the copy that gets produced, and what gets certified is not *the ledger* but **this copy is a true copy of the ledger produced to this court on the ninth of the ninth of last year**, and the words in that are the entire legal weight of ninety-one leaves and they are worth nothing at all if the copy is not true.

The book had ninety-one leaves in it and the numbers on them ran one to ninety-one with nothing missing.

The wrapper had ninety-two on it.

Riet found it on the twenty-first of the first month, standing up, with a lamp, because the count on a wrapper is written in a clerk's hand in a corner in the ordinary way and nobody looks at a corner when the book is the right size.

The two numbers were in two different hands. The ninety-two was in the copyist's hand of the sixth year and was the count of what came off the original. The ninety-one was in the same hand and was the count of what went into the book, and under it, in the same hand, was one word.

*Complete.*

And no number against it. And the word was not in the same ink as the ninety-one and had been added after, because the ninety-one had gone grey and the word had not.

Riet took the whole morning over it. He compared the leaves against the book that recorded the seizure, which is a different book and is in a different gallery and which he went and read in the corridor without asking anybody for it because it is on an open shelf and open shelves are not offices. He counted twice. He got ninety-one twice and he got ninety-two twice and the two numbers had been in the same building for six years about forty feet apart.

Then he sat down and worked out what he was allowed to certify, which took him about nine minutes and which is the only piece of professional reasoning in this chapter.

He was not being asked whether the ledger was complete. He was being asked whether **this book** is a true copy of the ledger produced to the court. He could compare the ninety-one leaves he had with the count of what came off the original and he could not account for one leaf, and there was a word in the sixth year's hand saying *complete* with no number against it, and a leaf that is not in the book is a leaf somebody could have taken out in six years and the number on the wrapper is the only thing in this city that says it was ever there.

So he certified the copy, in his own hand, in the form used, and it is a quarter of a page, and it says:

> *This is to certify that the book produced to this court, being a copy of a ledger of the carrying column and four others, bound in ninety-one leaves numbered consecutively from one, is a true copy of the ledger produced to this court on the ninth of the ninth of last year, **save that the wrapper from which it was made is endorsed with the figure ninety-two in the hand of the copyist, that the book contains ninety-one leaves, and that against the figure ninety-one is the word *complete* with no figure against it in the same hand;** and that the certifying clerk has been unable to account for the difference, and that the difference may be a leaf that was never collated into the book and that the certifying clerk is not able to say that it was.*
>
> *The certifying clerk has certified what he has compared and has not certified the completeness of the copy, and no person has asked him to.*
>
> *H. Riet, clerk of the Inheritance Desk, the twenty-ninth of the first month.*

He read it back to himself twice before he sealed it, which is a habit he has never told anybody he has, and then he sent it down to the district court by the midweek packet with the figure ninety-two written on the outside in case a clerk in a hurry put the envelope into a different file, and the outside figure is in his hand and it is the only reason anybody in that building will look at the inside of it.

And then he had to write the covering line, and the covering line is where he lost the afternoon.

He could not write *the copy is short by a leaf*. That was a claim, and a claim is a thing a person is held to at a trial, and Riet had been at a desk for nine years and had watched what happens to a claim. He could not write *please note*. He wrote:

> *This is the certification. The figure on the outside is the figure inside. Nothing in this certification is in anybody's favour and one part of it is against this gallery, and the district may wish to produce the original, and this office will not object if the district does.*

That took him until the fourth hour and he sent it on the thirtieth of the first month, a day and a half before the trial, and then he wrote his own copy of the handover list out again on a fresh sheet because the one in the day's book was in another man's hand and he was not going to write on a dead man's paper.

He wrote out the six items, and then he wrote a seventh, which is this:

*Seven. Certified a copy of ninety-one leaves, the wrapper of which is endorsed ninety-two. I have said in the certificate what I cannot account for and I have sent it down a day and a half before it is wanted and I have not slept properly and I would do it again. If the district produces the original and the leaf is not in the book, this gallery will have to say in a room why a clerk of the sixth year wrote *complete* and no figure. — H.R.*

And underneath item four, which said *It is not mine. It is hers. Do not chase it for me and do not tell her it was chased*, he wrote two lines and nothing else.

*Not chased. Not to be chased by me. — H. Riet, the twenty-first of the first month.*

He did not chase it. He has never chased it and he is not going to, and there is a person in Greyfen who has had a question put to her about her own position in a file and does not know that anybody in Auremar has read anything, and the two lines are on a sheet of paper in a day's book in the third gallery, in a clerk's hand, with a date on them, and there is now a person in the empire who could prove that a preference was a preference and not an oversight.

---

The Kest box he opened, once, on the twenty-fourth of the first month, and he opened it because he had taken it on purpose and a man who has taken a box on purpose has to be able to say what is in it, and a man who cannot say what is in it has taken it for a decoration.

The label is a wrapper. Wrappers are not sealed. There were four openings and closings on it, entered on the face of the wrapper, which is the practice, and the practice is that every opening gets a reason at the foot of it, and the reason names the person who opened it and why, and the four reasons are in four different hands over twelve years.

The first is the fire. The second is the fourth year, a re-entry, a reason, a clerk's initials. The third is the closing of the seventh year, and it is a good entry, and it is dated, and it names a clerk, and **at the foot of the third entry there is no reason at all.** There is a line ruled for it and nothing in it. The fourth is the reopening of the twelfth of the eighth of this year, and its reason is the Registrar's, and the reason is that any person may buy a shilling's copy of the property, the debt and the four openings and closings, and the entry says *a clerk's initials and no witness but that clerk* because the Registrar has said so in a room and the room is the record.

So the box has four openings and closings on the face of it and one of the four has no reason under it, in twelve years, and nobody has ever gone and looked, and the label is a wrapper and wrappers are not sealed and that is why nobody has gone and looked.

Riet took the day's book off the press and turned to the fourth of the current month and wrote the entry the day's book uses, in the day's book, in his own hand.

> *Twenty-fourth of the first month. Kest box. Went through the label. Three of the four entries on the wrapper carry a reason at the foot, in four hands. The third, being the closing of the seventh year, carries none. Not written by me and not to be supplied by me. Entered here against the fourth of the first month as **reason not known**.*
>
> *I do not know why it is not known and I have not asked, because I am a clerk at a desk and not a person this matter is about, and because a clerk who fills in a gap in a reason is a clerk who has decided what the reason was. — H. Riet.*

Nine years at the desk. Nine years of about a hundred and forty entries a quarter going through a rule about a hand's width from the edge, and the whole trade, the whole calling, the entire professional content of Halvard Riet's working life, is knowing what a person in a hurry writes in a box, and the reason nobody has ever caught a clerk of the works doing it with a form is not that the forms are careful.

It is that the box is a box, and a box is a thing a person is handed on a day when they have a hundred and forty of them, and the word in the box is three words long and it was already in the district's mouth, and the totals on the return were right, and a word in a box is not a total.

He wrote *reason not known* in a day's book in a hand a stranger will read in four years, and it is four words, and it is the most he can do, and he knows exactly what it is worth, which is nothing, and which is the whole of what a clerk has.

And then he did the last thing of the week, which is the thing that made it a decision and not a mood.

He copied out the six items again onto the inside cover of the new sheet, in his own hand, all six of them, exactly as the man who wrote them wrote them, including the two about the sealed page and the two about a sister in Greyfen, and he did not add a seventh to that copy. The seventh is on the sheet in the day's book, and the two lines under item four are on the sheet in the day's book, and the six items on the new sheet are in the order they were given to him and are not his, and he will not pretend in nine years that he decided to be the man who is still here because of a piece of paper he improved.

That is a thing a person cannot do. There is no form for it. It is not on the list, it is not in anybody's file, and the only record of it in the whole of the empire is a clerk's handwriting on the cover of a day's book in a gallery in Auremar, and if he dies tomorrow it dies with him, and if he stays in that chair for nine years it is the only thing anybody will have that says he was there on purpose.

He put the sheet in the day's book and shut the book and went out at the fourth hour with the queue of the afternoon still on the counter, and the certification had gone down a day and a half early, and a woman in a copying alcove two galleries away was writing his name in a book of her own with no office on it, and a box on the second shelf of his own press had a line in it with no reason under it, and he had put four words against it, and four words is what a man has.
