# Chapter 80: A Number in the Corner

The clerk of the district court came on the fifth of the fifth month at about the eleventh hour with a form of his own, and he was civil, and he was in a good coat, and he did everything right, and he put the form on the bench and turned it round so that it faced her and then said the sentence he had evidently been told to say by somebody sensible.

"I am not asking you about the plates, ma'am. I am asking for the name to go with the book of numbers, and the court has a clerk's name at the foot of this and mine is at the foot of that, and it is the ordinary way and I would not come to a door for it if it were not required."

"Nobody gets my name."

That is four words and she said them without any particular force, and then she picked the form up, folded it in two, and put it under the weight on the bench with about nine hundred other forms that are waiting for a plate, which is where a thing goes in that lane that is not going to happen.

The clerk looked at the weight for a second.

"Is there a reason I could put in the book."

"There is a reason," she said, "and you could not put it in the book, and I have been asked for a name three times in twenty-two years and I have said no three times and I am not going to explain it to the fourth, and you are a decent-looking man and you have got a form and a pen and you have been told to do it, so go and do it somewhere else and I will not think badly of you for it."

He went. He did not argue. He said that he was sorry, which she did not want, and then he said something at the gate that she thought about for two days afterwards and has not decided about.

He said: "For what it is worth, ma'am, nobody has ever asked me either, and I have had this form since the second of the fourth month and it is the only piece of paper in this court with a line in it that nobody can do anything with, and I have been carrying it about for a month looking for somebody to be angry with."

Then he went up the lane, and she went on cutting.

---

The reason, which she has never said to anybody, and which is this:

She has cut plates for twenty-two years out of a bench under a window in a lane off the Kiln road, three cuts in a triangle is her mark and it is the only way she signs anything and it is the mark of a woman who cuts plates and it does not say who keeps a house, and it is not the mark the court put in a minute book in the second month, and the two have never been in a room together and are never going to be.

Out of the run of the Notaries' Table there come numbers, and a number is not a plate's number, it is a *sending* number: it is the number of the sheet that went out, and every plate that is cut for a thing that is going to be sent carries it in the edge, cut shallow, in a hand of about sixty years, out of one counter, in one hand, in one building, for about sixty years.

And the numbers do not stop. They carry on through two people and then a third, and the numbers that were being cut in that run when she started are still being cut, and there is a point about halfway through the year in which a run changes, because a run changes when the counter changes or the hand changes, and she was in the room when it happened and she was the one doing it, and there is one man alive who was doing it before her and he is dead.

So there is a year, and there is a number on each side of it, and she knows both numbers to the digit, and nobody else does.

In the second month of last year, a district court took seven plates, and a woman of about sixty who had cut plates for twenty-two years in a lane cut and carried in four of them that nobody had thought to ask her for, and nobody identified a house by any of the seven and nobody will, and the court said in a form with a name at the foot of it that a tool is a thing anybody can buy. About nine people learned in one week what a mark is for.

What nobody did was ask her the question, which is not *what is a mark for*. It is: **which numbers are yours.**

Because if she says that, she is not a woman who cuts plates. She is the join. She is the only point in the empire where the run on a plate can be broken into the part that was hers and the part that was not, and a person who can do that can tell a court which certificates in a tray came out of which year, and a person who can do that can tell a court which *client* sent them, because clients have numbers too and the numbers go out on the same run.

She has thought about that for about three weeks, since the examination went out, and about forty people in four trades have learned in that time what a name is for, and she has watched four men go up and down this lane in three weeks, and two of them asked her for the name of the man she cut for in the year before the year.

If she gives her name she is a person in a file. If she gives the year she is a ledger.

So: no.

---

And here is the other thing she has never said to anybody, and it is eleven years old, and she has never said it because there is nobody in this lane to say it to, and because a woman who says it out loud in a lane in this city is a woman with a story.

There is a woman at the far end of this lane who brings papers to be stamped, about three days in ten, and she cannot read. She has never said she cannot read. She has been coming for eleven years and she signs a line with three fingers on it and the stamp goes on the top of it and she takes the paper away and puts it where she puts things, and in eleven years nobody has ever asked her what the paper says, and neither has she ever asked, and the arrangement has worked perfectly for eleven years, and it is an arrangement, and the two of them know it.

Eleven years ago a certifier brought a plate to be cut here — not a certificate, a plate, a matrix, a piece of steel — and gave her a card with him, and the card was made out to a firm, and the firm was a name of about four words, and she cut the number into the edge of the plate because that is the run and the run is the run, and she did not look at what the matrix was for, because nobody has ever asked her what a matrix is for and she is a plate-cutter and a matrix is steel and a plate is steel and they are the same kind of object and the difference between them is a thing that happens in a court.

That plate went out. What it was used for she does not know, in the sense that she could not tell you and did not try. What she knows is the number, because she cut it, and she knows the year, because she knows the years, and she knows her own cut in the corner of the edge of it, which is a triangle of three cuts, about a sixteenth of an inch, and which she put there because a plate-cutter marks her own work and a plate-cutter's mark is not a signature and is not a name and is not a house and is *hers* and there is no office at the top of it.

She thought about it for about a year. Then she stopped, because there was nothing to think about it with, and then in the second month of last year a woman came to her court and said a thing in a room about a tool and a ledger, and she has not been able to put the two things together since, and she has not told anybody, and that is eleven years of her life and about four hundred people have told the truth in a room in this matter and she is one of about four who has not.

---

The question came on the sixth of the fifth month at about the fourth hour, and it came from a girl of about thirteen who had walked two hours to ask it, and she asked it standing at the bench, out of breath, and she asked it badly, and she had walked two hours to ask it.

"Has this one got your name on it."

She put a card on the bench. It is a certificate. It has a playing-card's worth of good paper, and an impression on it in a colour that is not a colour, and a notch out of the top left corner of the impression about a sixteenth of an inch across.

It is not her plate. She can tell that in about four seconds, and she can tell it without touching it, because there is a thing about an impression that she has never been able to explain to anybody and has never tried to.

"Give me your hand," she said.

The girl gave her her hand. She turned the card over with the girl's fingers on it, and got the edge of it between her own thumb and the side of her forefinger, the way you find a cut in a coin, and she moved it about an inch, and stopped.

"That is not mine," she said. "That is a number from about eleven years back and it is not one of mine. I will not tell you whose it is because I do not know whose it is, and I have cut about nine hundred plates off that run and there are about nine hundred and I could not tell you whose any of them are, and that is not because I am careful. It is because nobody's name goes on the edge. There is a number and the number is a number of a thing that went out, and I am a woman who cuts steel, and if I could tell you whose it was I would have been doing something else for twenty-two years."

"Is that bad?"

"It is not bad or good," she said. "It is what it is. What is bad is a person being asked whether a piece of paper is theirs and not being able to find out, and that has been happening in this lane for about four hundred years, and I have been here twenty-two of them, and the only thing I can do about it is tell you the truth about a number."

The girl started to cry, which is what a person of thirteen does at the end of a two-hour walk, and then stopped, which is what a person of thirteen does in a lane where they have come too far to go home crying.

"Can you write the number down," she said. "For my mother. In case anybody asks her."

The plate-cutter picked up a pencil off the bench — a pencil, not a pen, because a pen is a thing that goes in a form and a pencil goes in a corner — and she wrote the number in the top left corner of the card, in about a quarter of an inch of space where nothing is printed, and she wrote it large, and she wrote it the way you write for somebody who is going to look at it and not read it, which is how you write for a person who cannot read a paragraph.

Then she turned the card over and wrote on the back, in a corner, the day, and beside it three short cuts in a triangle, because three short cuts in a triangle is the whole of what she is and there is no office at the top of it and there is no room to put one.

"That is a mark and not a name," she said. "I want you to understand that, because you are thirteen and in about four years somebody is going to come to your house with a tray and ask you what a mark is. It is not my name. It is the only way I sign anything, and it says *this is one of mine* to somebody who already knows what mine looks like, and it does not say who I am, and I want you to be able to say that about it in a room if anybody ever puts it in front of you."

"What is the day for?"

"So that if anybody ever tells you this paper was not in your hand on the sixth of the fifth month, you can say it was, and you will be right, and there is a mark on the other side of it and it is mine, and I have been cutting plates out of a bench under a window in this lane for twenty-two years and anybody in this city can come and look at the bench."

---

The girl took the card and put it in her hand and did not put it in her pocket, and stood at the bench for about a minute looking at the corner of it, and then said:

"Mother's going to be pleased. She'll say you told us."

"I told you."

"She'll say you told us," said the girl, "because that's what she does."

And she went, up the lane, two hours, at about half past four in the fifth month, with a card in her hand with a number in the corner and a day and a mark on the back of it, and no name anywhere on any part of it, and she did not look back, and the plate-cutter watched her to the end of the lane and then went in, because the stove was out and there were about nine hundred plates.

---

The plate-cutter sat down at about the seventh hour on the seventh of the fifth month with a piece of paper and a pencil, because there is a thing in this lane that is not a bench and not a press and is not a form, which is a woman of about sixty and a bad light and a book nobody has ever read.

She wrote the question out in full, because it is the question, and because a question nobody has said out loud is not a question, it is a thing a person has been carrying about.

> *What happens to a rule about records when the person it is about cannot get to the room.*
>
> *I have been asked that by nobody. I have been asking it for about three weeks, since a man at a court said out loud that the court does not know what house keeps a mark, and about forty people in four trades found out inside a week what that does to them, and I found out the same thing and I found it eleven years late by a different road, and that is the whole of what I have learned this year.*
>
> *Here is the answer, and it is the true one, and it is that nothing. Nothing happens. There is no rule about records that reaches the far end of this lane. There never has been. A woman cannot read a paragraph, so a paragraph does not touch her, and if it does touch her then she signs a line with three fingers on it, and that has been going on in this lane since before I was born and it will be going on in nine years, and no court in the empire has a line about it and no office has ever written one.*
>
> *The thing that has changed in three weeks is not the rules. It is that a person can now say out loud what a number on a card is a number of, and before the eighth of the second month of last year there were about four hundred people in this city who could not have told you, and I was one of them, and I was not being careful, I was in a lane.*
>
> *And on the sixth of this month a girl of thirteen stood at my bench and asked me a question and I answered it, and I wrote the number in the corner of her card in pencil and the day on the back and my mark beside it, and I gave it back to her, and I am the receiving person, and I have no office, and there was no box, and I did it anyway.*
>
> *I do not know where I got it from. I have not seen it. There is no form and there is no series and there is nobody at the top of it. I have been putting a number and a mark and a day in a corner of a piece of paper for about eleven years when a person could not find out whether a plate was theirs, and nobody has ever asked me why, and I have never written it down, and a girl of thirteen walked two hours and asked me and I did it without stopping to think, which means it is not a thing anybody invented in a room in this city in the last four months. It is a thing about a bench under a window.*
>
> *It does not help her. She has walked up here with a piece of paper and she wanted one thing off it and I could not give her the one thing, and she went home with a number in a corner that a person cannot read and a mark on the back that means nothing to anybody outside this lane. She is no better off than she was at eleven o'clock this morning and she is better off than she was at eleven o'clock two hours ago, and that is the entire size of the difference and I have been doing this for twenty-two years and I have never had a larger one.*
>
> *And the thing I am frightened of, and I am writing it because a woman in a wall in this city four hundred and thirty miles away writes sentences about herself with the day on them and has told me by post that she is frightened and indispensable at a window with a nail in it and would not rather be otherwise, and so I am going to do the same, in my own hand, in a lane, with no office on it:*
>
> *About forty people have learned in three weeks what a name is for. And I am the only person alive who can break a number on a plate into the part that is mine and the part that is not. And those are two facts and they are about two different people and the only thing standing between them is that I am sixty and I have three cuts in a triangle and no office, and that a man with a tray has to knock on a door to get at me, and he does not knock on doors. That is the whole of my protection. It is not a form. It is not a rule. It is that I am not worth a tray, and I have been not worth a tray for twenty-two years by accident, and I have got about three weeks of that left.*
>
> *And I am not going to hand the year over. Not to a court, not to a trade, not to a girl of thirteen who walks two hours. If I hand the year over then I am a ledger and there is nothing else to be, and if I am a ledger then in nine years there is a file in a building with a line in it saying that a woman in a lane can read a plate, and a man in a good coat writes to the works and the works writes to the trade and the trade writes to me, and I will be a person in a file, and the person in the file will be a woman who can read a plate, and the file will not have the number, because the number is the thing they would want next.*
>
> *So: no. To the clerk with the form. To the four men. To anybody, ever, for as long as I am cutting.*
>
> *And the question stands, and it is not answered, and I am the one who asked it, and I am not a lawyer and I have never been in a room with a seal in it in my life, and I have a bad light and a bench under a window in a lane off the Kiln road in the city of Auremar, and there is a stove in the corner and the stove is out at about the seventh hour in the fifth month, and the plates are in a tray under a cloth, about nine hundred of them, and I have cut every one of them, and there is one year in the middle of that run that only I can find, and that is mine, and there is no office at the top of it, and no form, and no person in the world who can be written to about it.*
>
> *That is the whole of what I have got. It is a number and a mark and a day, in the corner of a piece of paper, given to a child by a woman who cannot sign.*
