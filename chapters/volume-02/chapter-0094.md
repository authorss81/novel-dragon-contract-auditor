# Chapter 94: One Line Long

The Docket of Powers is three rooms off the corridor behind the third gallery, with a card in a window and no sign on the door, and it has been there for about sixty years, and the reason there is no sign on the door is that a person who is looking for it can be found and a person who is not looking for it cannot, and the master of it worked that out about forty years before anybody thought of writing it down.

Marek Kest went in on the twelfth of the ninth month at about the fourth hour with a sheet of foolscap in his coat that he had ruled himself at his own table the night before, and Oswin Marrow read it standing up, and then read it a second time, and then said the three things he always says before he does anything at all.

"I am going to say the three powers now and then I am going to do the thing you have come about, and I say them first so that you can see whether the thing you have come about is one of them, and it is not, and I want that visible before I touch the book.

"**One. I can enter a finding in a public register.** Not a decision, not a judgment, not a sentence. A finding, in my own hand, in a book with a heading on it, and anybody can read it and anybody can take a copy of it at a shilling.

"**Two. I can bar a person from any audit in any jurisdiction until examined.** That is the whole of it. It is not a punishment. It is a line in a book with a disposition against it, and every court that takes a recognition from this office has to answer for that page, and there is nothing else about it, and there is no appeal from it to me.

"**Three. I can refer one question to the court sitting in room four at the Crown Kiln.** One question. Not a matter, not a person, not a complaint. A question, and I cannot refer the same question twice, and I have referred four in twenty-two years, and I have never once been asked to refer a fifth and I would do it if I were asked."

"None of those is what I have come about," said Marek Kest.

"No," said Oswin Marrow. "None of them is. Sit down, because I am going to tell you the fourth thing and I have never told it to a person in twenty-two years and I am telling it to you because you have read four months of a file in which you are the only person named and you are going to have to be named in one more."

---

The register is a bound book about two inches thick and it runs back about sixty years and it has one page to a year and four lines to an entry, and the four lines are a name, a date, an instrument, and an authority.

"About four hundred and ten entries in thirty years," said Oswin Marrow, "which is about fourteen a year, and the smallness is the point and I have said so in open court and I will say it to you in this room. A scale reading needs a permission. A permission needs somebody to have asked for one. And a person agrees to one when there is no other way of getting at the record, and there is almost always another way, and the people who come in here are the people for whom there was not."

He put his hand flat on the book, which he does about twice a day.

"A bar under the second of my three powers is me writing a disposition against a name that is already on a page. That is what a bar is. It is not a line through a name and it is not a black mark and it does not stop you walking about. It is a sentence in a column, and the sentence says the person is not to audit in any jurisdiction in any office until examined, and the examination was on the eleventh of the first month and the bar did not fall away when it was held, and the court above me said in a form on the eighth of the third month that nothing it did discharged it, and I entered that finding myself and I did not enjoy entering it."

"Then what does," said Marek Kest.

"**The line.**"

He turned the book round and put it on the table between them, and turned it back, and did not open it at a page, which took Marek Kest about four seconds to notice and about nine more to understand.

"**The only procedure for being entered in this register is a written application to this office stating the authority under which the person reads. It is one line long. It has existed for about twelve hundred years.** It is on a printed slip and it has been on a printed slip since before the copyists' guild had a rule about anything, and it asks one question, and the question is not *may I audit*. The question is *under whose authority do you read*, and the answer goes in the fourth line of the entry, and the fourth line is the only line anybody ever uses and it is the reason this office is safe."

He looked up.

"You did not write one in the fourth month of last year."

"No."

"Give me the reason."

"I did not want to be in a room," said Marek Kest.

Oswin Marrow waited for about a minute and a half, which is what he does.

"That is the reason you wrote down," he said. "That is a different reason and it is about nine words and I would like the other one before I fill in a book, because I have got to write your name on a page for the rest of my life and I am entitled to know what a man thinks he is buying."

"I think I am buying a room," said Marek Kest, "with a name at the top of it, in which somebody may ask me whether I read a thing fairly. And the whole instrument of this matter for fourteen months has been that a name at the top of a document is the thing that finds a person, and I have spent a year using it on about nine people and I have never once turned it round."

"You have turned it round. It is why you are here. **Go on and say the honest question, because you have had it in your mouth since the third week of the eleventh month and I would rather hear it in a room than read it in a book in nine years.**"

Marek Kest said the honest question out loud, standing, in a room with a stove in it three corridors from his own, and it is on the page because he said it in a room with another person in it.

"**Should a man who has spent fourteen months refusing documents be entered in a register of persons who read?** That is the question and it has been sitting in me since the fourth month of last year and the answer I have been avoiding is that a register of persons is a list of doors, and the only difference between this door and the four in a certifier's book in a room in the Kiln sheds is that this one has a rule at the top of it, and a name at the top of the entry, and a person who keeps the book. And I wrote down, in my own hand, in the fifth month, that a list of people who can be found is a list of doors, and I was talking about a sheet of foolscap that I had just torn up."

"So the question is real," said Oswin Marrow, "and the answer is that I cannot answer it, because it is not a question about this office. It is a question about whether a man with a tray is more dangerous with a register in front of him than without one. And I have been the master of a register of persons for twenty-two years and I can tell you exactly what that office can do with a name, and it is this: it can stop a person auditing. **It cannot ask a person a question. It cannot make a person produce a document. It cannot put anybody in a room. It is a line in a book, and the only thing the book does is make it a fact about you that you may read under an authority, and if you do it badly then the fact is there and the badness is there and they are in the same four lines.**"

"That is not a small thing," said Marek Kest. "About forty people in four trades learned in the last three weeks of the fourth month what a name in a file is worth."

"Yes," said Oswin Marrow. "And about nine certifiers learned in the second month what a mark is worth. **The price went up for both of them in the same fortnight and neither of them knew the other existed, and that is the whole of what this office has to do with it, which is nothing, and I have wanted to say that sentence in a room for about two years.**"

---

He wrote it at the desk, in one line, in a hand of about fifty, and he wrote it slowly, and the second half of the line is the part that matters and he wrote that slower.

> *I, Marek Kest, clerk, of the Received Room, apply to be entered in the register of persons who read, and the authority under which I read is the grant of the limited audit of this court sitting in room four of the eleventh of the third month, which is over, and such authority as this office may from time to time accept from a person so entered.*
>
> *— M. Kest, the twelfth of the ninth month.*

"That fourth line," said Oswin Marrow, reading it over his shoulder, "is not what anybody has ever written in it."

"I am aware of that."

"It is a bad line," said the master of the Docket of Powers. "It is honest, and it is one line, and it answers the question the form asks, and it says out loud on the face of a sixty-year-old register that the authority has ended. **Every other entry in this book names an authority that is running.** Mine says one that stopped, and it says what replaces it, and what replaces it is a thing that has not got a name yet because nobody has thought of one."

"Enter it," said Marek Kest, "and enter the day, and enter the figure, and put the disposition in the column and cross it with a line, and I want the entry to say what discharged it. I want the fourth line to say: *discharged on the twelfth of the ninth month by the applicant's own line, and by nothing else that has occurred in this matter.*"

"That is a strange thing to want written about you."

"It is the only thing in this matter that has ever been done to me by me," said Marek Kest, "and if I do not write it down then in nine years there will be a page in a book with a name on it and a word *examin* against it and no reason, and somebody will fill in the reason, and a clerk who fills in a gap in a reason is a clerk who has decided what the reason was. There is a man of nine years in the third gallery who wrote *reason not known* against a filing in the seventh year and got told it was the only honest thing anybody wrote in that book, and I am not going to be the reason somebody has to write *not known* about."

Oswin Marrow read the entry back and made him read it back too, which he does with everybody, and then the master of the Docket of Powers said the sentence that is the reason the whole of the last three batches happened:

"**I am entering it, and I am going to say the reason out loud, and you are going to have to stand in the room and hear it, because in about four years somebody is going to come to this window and ask what a name in this book is, and the answer has to have been said in a room by the man who is standing in it.**

"**A bar is discharged by a person putting his own name on a page. It is not discharged by an examination, because an examination is a thing done to a man. It is not discharged by a grant, because a grant is a thing done to a man. It is not discharged by an order of a court, because an order is a thing done to a man. It is discharged by a line in the man's own hand, and the line has been there for about twelve hundred years, and you have known about it since the third week of the eleventh month, and you did not write it for about nine months, and today you wrote it in four minutes and it is the only thing in fourteen months that has improved your position by a thing you did to yourself.**"

He wrote the entry and ruled a line through the disposition with a single stroke, and put the book down, and then he said the fourth thing.

"There is one more thing I can do and I am telling you what it is before I do it, because I say the three out loud in advance and the fourth is the same habit and it has never come up before.

"**I can refer one question to the court sitting in room four.** One. I have done it four times in twenty-two years. The question I am thinking of referring is not yours and is not about you, and I would be asking that court to put your name in a document, and I would be doing it because in about four days you are going to be a person who may read and there is nobody in this empire who has asked you whether you should be one, and I am the only person I know who is allowed to ask a court a question in a form."

He put his hand flat on the book again.

"So I am going to write the question down and I am going to read it to you, and you are going to tell me whether to send it, and if you tell me not to send it I will not send it and I will write down that you told me not to, with the day on it, and that will be the fifth time in twenty-two years I have written that a person told me no and it will be the first time it was not a refusal."

---

In the doorway of that room, at about the fifth hour, there was a copyist of the third gallery who had been copying a document in the corridor and had not gone out, which is a thing she does.

She had heard all of it. She came in and read the line of the application over his shoulder, and then she read the entry in the register, and then she said the one thing.

"You have not asked her to take it down."

"There was no copy of it going anywhere," said Marek Kest. "It is a line in a book in this building, forty feet from my own door."

"That is not why you did not ask," said Tamsin Rook, "and we both know what the rule says and what it is for, and you have just entered your own name in a register of persons who read and there is no longer anybody in this matter who can read anything you write in a room where the two of us are the only people in it. **You have just taken yourself out of the only kind of room where you could not be asked what you were doing.** And I am not saying you did it for that reason. I am saying that it is now true, and that I am the one who noticed it, and that in about four years there is going to be a person who reads that register and asks a clerk of this building whether M. Kest keeps records, and the answer will be yes, and there will be no second question, and the whole of what you have just done is that you have made yourself answerable in four lines and unanswerable in a room."

"Yes," said Marek Kest.

"Good," said Tamsin Rook. "I am not going to fight you about it in a corridor. I am going to go and copy out a hundred and forty lines of a return that has nothing to do with any of this and I am going to do it very fast."

She went, and the two of them sat there for about another minute, and then Marek Kest asked a question he had been carrying since the twelfth hour in the fifth month of the fifth, and it came out of him in about four words, and it is the last thing he says in that room.

"What happens on the day it stops being a list of doors and starts being a door."

"Nothing," said Oswin Marrow. "It happens on the day somebody knocks on it, and about nine people in this city have had that happen to them in the last three months, and every one of them is still in business, and one of them wrote a number on the edge of a piece of steel in his own day-book in his own hand, which is more than anybody did to me, and he was forty-one and he had a press he did not own."

---

That night, in a room with a barred door on the inside of the frame and a stove in it, he wrote it out in his own book with the day on it, and this is the whole of it.

> *The twelfth of the ninth month, at about the ninth hour. The bar is off. It is off and I did it with a line and the line was always there and I have known it since the third week of the eleventh month of last year, and I want the whole thing written down in one place in my own hand while I still feel it, because about four days from now it will be an old thing and in nine years I will not be able to remember what a bar felt like, and that will be a mercy, and a mercy is not a reason to write it down, and I am writing it down anyway.*
>
> *I have had a whole day today. From about the fourth hour of the morning, when the entry was made, until now, which is the ninth hour, and there is no bar on me, and there is nothing in this matter I am allowed to read, and there is no instrument in this empire that would let me read one, and I have had five hours of a completely ordinary working day, and I sat in this room for about two of them.*
>
> **I did not read anything. There is nothing to read. And I want it written down that I noticed about the fourth hour that there was nothing to read, and that I was not disappointed, and that the not being disappointed is the thing I have been afraid of for about a year and did not have a word for.**
>
> *The word is that I have been frightened of being the kind of man who cannot stop, and I have found out this month that the bar was the only thing stopping me, and that I have been relying on a line in a book for a thing that ought to be done by a person, and that in about nine days there is going to be a woman in this building who is going to make a desk in front of me and a list of sheets going out of it, and that is going to be the thing that stops me or it is not going to be anything at all.*
>
> *And the fourth question, which I have asked about nine hundred people in fourteen months and which I have never once asked myself: **who read this to me, and do they get paid if I sign.** T. R. read the line of the application over my shoulder in a doorway at the fifth hour and she is not paid for it and she did it anyway, and she is the only person in this matter who has ever got a word wrong on purpose to protect somebody, and she caught four of my words in a year and not one fact, and today she caught nothing at all and told me why, and it took her about eleven seconds and it was the most expensive thing anybody has said to me in fourteen months.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the twelfth of the ninth month, at about the ninth hour.*
