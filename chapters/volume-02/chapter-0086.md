# Chapter 86: A Bench Under a Window

The card came into the chamber of the district court of the lower district on the twentieth of the sixth month with four words on the front of the envelope and no office on it, and the four lines that were in it with the card were read into the file and then the file was shut for four days, because a magistrate cannot direct an examination of a thing in her own court on the strength of four lines written by a clerk with a barred door, and she was not going to try, and she had a rule of nine years' standing about that.

On the twenty-fourth of the sixth month she sent for two people. One of them came and was no use in about nine minutes. The other did not come, and the officer who went to the lane came back with four words and a sentence, and she was asked twice and answered twice, and that is how this week starts.

---

The first is Emrys Rudd, of the lean-to at the head of the crossing, sixty-four, and the court sent him a card and he came, and the question was about the cost of the crossing, and the answer is in the minute of the twenty-first of the fifth month and is a penny a day, and it did not help with a card with a mark on it.

The second is a woman of about sixty who has cut plates for twenty-two years out of a bench under a window in a lane off the Kiln road, and she is not named in this and she is not going to be named in it, and the court sent for her because it is the only trade in this city that can look at a piece of steel and say what was done to it, and the officer who went to the lane came back with four words and a sentence, and the four words are the same four words she has said twice since the fifth of the fifth month and the sentence is the reason.

"Nobody gets my name," she said. "And I am not coming to look at a piece of steel, and I will tell you why and then you can go, because I would rather say it out loud than have it said to me twice in a month by two different people standing in my doorway."

"Ma'am—"

"**A die is not a plate.** A plate is a thing that is pressed and thrown away. A die is a tool, and a tool is a thing anybody can buy, and there are about four hundred dies in this city and a third of them were cut out of an impression and nobody has ever asked any of us, because nobody has ever needed to know and because it is a trade matter and trades do not go to courts. If I come and look at a die and I say a thing about it in a room, then in four years somebody is going to come to this lane with a tray and ask me how a die is cut and which ones were and what year, and I have got one year in the middle of that run that only I can find, and the year is worth more than the die, and I am sixty and I have three cuts in a triangle and I am not going to hand the year over to a court to be polite."

The officer asked her one question, which he was entitled to ask and which she answered, and it is the whole of what the court got out of that lane.

"Then will you say whether a die cut from an impression can be told from one that was cut by hand."

"No," she said. "And I will tell you why not, and then you are going to go, because I have said two things now and I have not had my dinner. If I say yes, you will send me a die and I will say yes, and then in nine years there will be a file in this city with a woman in a lane in it who can tell a cut die from a hand-cut one. **And a person who can do that is not a person anybody sends a tray to once. A person nobody can find is the only kind of person in this trade that is safe, and being safe is the whole of what I have got, and I am not giving it up to be useful to somebody I have never met in a matter I am not in.** You want a person who can be found. Go and find one. There are about nine of us in this city and there are more in the Kiln sheds and I do not know any of their names and I have never wanted to."

"That is twice," said the officer. "I have been to this lane before."

"Yes," she said. "That is twice, and nobody has stood at this door twice in four years except the two of you, and I would like it noticed that I have said the same four words twice and got the same answer twice, and the difference between you and the last one is not the coat. I am not saying that unkindly, I am saying it because in about four years somebody is going to describe this lane, and there is going to be a number of visits in the description, and the number is two now and it is not going to be three while I am cutting."

---

Joane Tranter read the officer's four lines and the sentence twice, and then she did the ordinary thing, which is to go and look for a person she can find.

She found one in about four days and it cost one pound fourpence and a day's wage, and her name is not in this and was not asked for twice, and the woman is a plate-cutter of about thirty-four who has a bench of her own in a different lane and who has a trade name over the door and a customer list and a book of day-work, and every one of those four things is a thing about a person, and she has four of them, and the woman of sixty has none of them and that is the whole of the difference between the two.

She came to the bench in the lane on the first of the seventh month, at about the tenth hour, and Joane Tranter came with her, and there was a clerk with a book.

What Tranter did first is the thing that is in the file, and it is four lines and it has her name at the foot of it, and it is the first thing in nine years she has entered about a person she was about to put in a room.

> *Warned, the first of the seventh month, before anything was put in front of her, and this warning is entered before the examination and not after it.*
>
> *You are about to describe a mark. The description will be in a minute of this court and a copy of that minute may be had by anybody at a shilling. I am telling you in advance, in a room, with a clerk in it, that from today you are a person in a file in this city who can answer this question, and that I cannot prevent it and would not prevent it, and that the only protection anybody in your trade has is that a person is not findable and that you are findable, and have been for as long as anybody in this city can remember, and that this afternoon is not what makes you findable and I want that said because I have read four hundred minutes this month and about forty of them are about a person learning what their name is worth.*
>
> *If you would rather not do it, say so now and it will be entered that you were asked and declined and I will write the reason for the direction myself and it will be a bad reason and it will be my name at the foot of it. — J. Tranter, the first of the seventh month, at about the tenth hour.*

The plate-cutter read it twice and asked one question, which was whether anybody in this court thought a day of her time was worth more than a shilling, and Tranter said the figure out loud — **one pound and fourpence and the carriage** — and the woman said that was four days of her bench and that she would do it for the four days and not for the kindness, and Tranter said that was the correct reason and entered it.

The fee is entered in the fund of this court under the heading it is entered under, and the heading is the first thing anybody will read in nine years and the heading is not her name, because the fund does not pay persons, it pays services, and the service is *an examination of a mark*, and the fund has had that heading in it before and will have it again.

---

Then she looked at the die, and it took her about four seconds, and twenty-two years is what it took her to be able to do it in four seconds.

"It is a mirror," she said.

"Say more than that."

"It is a mirror," said the plate-cutter, "and everything in it is the wrong way round, and you cannot see it and I do not think anybody without steel under their thumb can see it. Hold the card to it."

Tranter held the card to it.

"Look at the weight of it. On the card, the mark is heavy on the left. Look at the shoulder of the steel: it is heavy on the right. **A hand pressed the original and the hand was left-handed or the hand was tired on the right and it does not matter, the hand pressed the left.** A die is the thing that makes the mirror, and the mirror is the seal, and so everything in the die is reversed, and the pressure that was on the left is on the right, and it is not that the steel is worn unevenly, it is that the steel is showing you the hand from behind."

"That is a fault in the steel."

"That is not a fault in the steel, ma'am, that is the definition of a die, and every die in this city has it and about nine hundred plates off a hundred years of them have it and nobody has ever looked. **A hand cannot press with its own pressure backwards.** That is the whole of it. You could make this card in four seconds in a room with a press and it would pass in every court in this city except one that has a plate-cutter in it, and the reason it would pass is that it *looks* laid, and the only thing wrong with it is that it has been through a mirror, and a mirror is not a thing anybody thinks about, and I have been thinking about it every working day for twenty-two years because it is my whole job."

"And the second thing."

"There is no second thing. I have looked for a second thing for about a minute and a half and there is not one, and I am going to tell you what that means, and you are going to want me to stop, and I am not going to stop because I have had this bench eleven years and nobody has ever asked me a question in a room before.

"It means the original is real. Somebody laid that seal with their own hand, on a real card, and the break is in the right place on the right side where the hand came off it, and it is a good seal and it belongs to a person. This thing is not a false seal. **This thing is a true one, photographed, by a person who has never once in their life seen a hand come off a card.** And that is worse, and it is a different thing, and I want that in the minute in those words, because a false seal is a crime and this is not a crime yet. This is a man who has got hold of a good card and wants a second one and does not know that the second one is a photograph."

---

She wrote four lines and read them back, and they went in on the second of the seventh month and there is a name at the foot of the minute that is the magistrate's and not hers.

> *The die produced before this court on the first of the seventh month is a cutting made from a previously made impression. In it the variation of pressure runs in the reverse of the variation of pressure in the impression on the card produced on the nineteenth of the sixth month, the card being heavier at the left and the die being heavier at the right. A die is the instrument by which a mirror is made, and a mirror is a scale impression, and the whole of the variation of pressure in a scale impression is the record of a hand. The die therefore carries the record of a hand reversed, and no hand can produce its own pressure reversed.*
>
> *The court enters that the impression on the card is laid and spoken and was made by cutting a die from a true impression; that a die so cut will carry a true impression to a person who will accept it, and that about nine hundred plates have been cut in this city in a hundred years from impressions and that the court does not know and cannot find how many were cut from a true impression and from a false one; and that the court makes no finding against any person, names no person, and identifies no house and no client.*
>
> *The examiner was warned before she began, in a form, with the magistrate's name at the foot of the warning, and the fee of one pound fourpence and the carriage is entered under the head of this fund for an examination of a mark, and the examiner's name is not in this minute and was not asked for.*

And then the fourth line, which is the reason the other three exist, and it is in the magistrate's own account at about the ninth hour of the second of the seventh month and it is on the page.

> *I sent a card to a lane in this city in the last week of the sixth month and the woman who cuts plates there declined to come and gave me her reason, which was that a person who can tell a cut die from a hand-cut one is a person anybody can find, and that being unfindable is the whole of the protection her trade allows her. I did not put a second card and I did not send anybody else, and the reason I did not is that she is right. I went and bought a person I could find, out of a fund, under a heading, at one pound fourpence and a day, and I have entered the warning before the work and not after it, and I have not entered her name, and I want all three of those things read in nine years as what they are, which is a court that took a woman's protection and paid for what it took and wrote down that it had taken it.*
>
> *It has still cost her. She was findable at ten o'clock on the first of the seventh and she is findable at nine o'clock on the second of the seventh and the only difference is that on the second somebody has a minute with a question in it that she can answer. I have made her a person who knows something. That is the price and it is in the fund under a heading and the heading does not have her name on it, and that is the only mercy in this and I do not think it is much of one.*
>
> *I am the magistrate who did not ask for four months, and then did not ask for a month, and then did not ask a second time, and I have entered all three of those in the same book, and there is a heading in that fund for examinations of marks and I have now put money under it twice and I would like somebody in nine years to tell me whether the second time was worth the first pound fourpence, and I do not know, and the woman of sixty in the lane is the only person alive who can answer that and she has told me not to come to that lane a third time.*
>
> *— J. Tranter, the second of the seventh month, at about the ninth hour, in the chamber, with a clerk in the room.*

They walked back up the lane together, because the lane is four streets long and the court does not send a person back down it alone with a bag of steel in her coat, and about a third of the way up the plate-cutter stopped and said the thing she had been not saying since the bench.

"Ma'am. There is one more question and I am not going to be able to help with it, and I would rather say so now while I am standing here than have somebody in your chamber find out in four years that I could have told them and did not."

"Say the question."

"The die has a number in the edge of it. Forty-four thousand eight hundred and nineteen." She said it the way she said everything, flat, at about the speed of a person reciting a thing she has recited to herself. "That is a sending number out of the Table's run and the run does not stop, and there is one year in the middle of it where the run changes, because a run changes when the counter changes or the hand changes. I was in the room when it happened and I did it. I know both numbers to the digit. The certifier who had that die has written it in his own day-book in his own hand, which is more than anybody else has done, and I am not going to say that he is wrong, and he is not wrong, it is a number of a book.

"And I am not going to be asked what the year is, and if I am asked I am going to say no, and I would like it entered somewhere that I said it in advance so that in four years when somebody wants it they find out that a woman knew and declined and was not hiding."

"Entered," said Joane Tranter, and wrote five words on the back of the officer's paper while standing in a lane, and dated them, and did not put the lane's name in the file.

"Thank you," said the plate-cutter. "You will not get it out of me by asking me twice, so do not send a second card, and if you send a second card I will say the same four words and I will say them to the person at the door, and then this lane is going to be a place where a court has been twice, and I have been a plate-cutter in it for twenty-two years and I would like it to stay a lane where people bring steel."

Joane Tranter put the paper in her coat and said: "There will not be a second card. And I am going to say why, in a room, on the record, because it is the fourth time in two years I have had to promise a person something I cannot promise, and the last three promises I made are all in files in this city, and one of them was made to a man of sixty who is owed something by about forty people and will be given nothing, and I am not going to make a fourth and then fail at it in front of you in about four years."

---

The die went back into the drawer in the Kiln sheds and the card went into the file of the district court of the lower district, and neither of them identified anybody, and a woman of thirty-four went back to a bench in a lane four streets off and has been findable ever since, and nobody thanked her and she did not expect it and said in the lane, on the way out, that she was going home.

The card is in a file. The card is genuine-looking and impossible. The card has a mark at the top of it and the mark is not anybody's name, and it was made by a person who had a good card in his hand and wanted a second one, and there is no instrument in this empire for asking a person what is in his hand. And it was used before it was offered, which is the part of it that nobody in this building thought to ask about, and a thing that is used leaves a mark on somebody's paper, and the only record in this city of who brought a paper to a counter is a book at the end of a corridor with a time in it and an initial and no heading at the top of it.
