# Chapter 66: A Matrix Is Not a Ledger

Garren Pyle had a room over a yard in the Kiln sheds with a press in it that he did not own, a bench he had made himself, and about four hundred certificates in a rack against the wall of which sixty-one had been suspended on the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year by a registrar in a room at the Crown Kiln who had not heard an argument, because there is no argument.

He had stood in a river courtroom with a certificate in a leather case that had a cold press mark on it and a copied third notch, and he had withdrawn the record rather than read it, and he had been asked whose hand pressed the matrix and had not said, and he had given his name and the name of the office that instructed him, in that order, and both of them are in the file and he has never had anybody tell him that the order was the right way round.

He was not sorry.

He said that in the first ten minutes, to a copyist who had come about something else entirely and who had said his name, and he said it plainly, in the voice of a man who has had since the sixth month to decide how he is going to be about this and has chosen the flat one.

"You are not sorry about the certificates."

"I am not sorry about the certificates," said Garren Pyle. "I am not sorry about the certificate in the leather case. I am not sorry about having stood in a room and let a court make a finding about my trade. I am sorry about two things and I will tell you both of them because you did not ask and because you are going to write something down and I would rather you wrote the true version."

"Go on."

"One. I did not ask what the plate was for. There was a plate in a drawer in this room and it was in the drawer when I took this room and it will be in the drawer when somebody takes it after me, and I never once in eleven years asked a living person what a plate is for. I asked the man who brought it, and he said *for the certificate*, and that is not an answer, because the certificate is on the card and the plate is the steel. Two. I would rather be the man who handed it in than the man who had it." He put his hand flat on the bench. "Those are the only two and I have thought about it since the sixth month and I would like you to write them in that order and not in the other one."

The copyist wrote them in that order and read them back, which she does, and did not put a heading on them, and did not ask him a third question, which he noticed and did not remark on.

---

The plate is about the size of a hand.

It is steel, a quarter of an inch thick at the shoulder, ground at the edge, and there is a mark cut into the face of it about two inches long, and the mark is a filled square with a bar through it and a notch cut out of the top left corner, and it is a mark and not a name, and there is no name on the plate anywhere, and there never has been.

There is a number cut into the edge of the plate, shallow, in the way a man cuts a number into a thing he is not going to see the face of again. It is in the hand of whoever cut it, which is not Pyle's hand, and Pyle cannot say whose hand it is, and has said so twice, and will say so a third time.

It came back to this room eleven years ago with a letter. The letter said, in four lines, that the plate was not wanted back and was to be held, and that the holder was not to sell it and was not to use it, and that the holder would be told if it was wanted. The letter is not in the room. The letter is in a file in a building in another part of the city and the plate is in a drawer in this one, and the two of them have been in different buildings for eleven years, and the plate is the only object in the empire that Pyle is the last owner of.

He is fifty-one and has no premises and no apprentice and no trade name of his own, because a certifier is a person other people own, and the whole of what he is is a room, a press he does not own, and a plate in a drawer.

And a number cut into the edge of it.

"You are going to tell me that number is about a book," said the copyist.

"I am going to tell you that I asked a man about it in the eleventh year and he said it was the number the thing was entered under, and I did not ask what the thing was, because the thing was a certificate and a certificate is a card." He took the plate out of the drawer and set it on the bench and did not let go of it for a second. "It is not my number. I have never issued that number. Mine are a small series with a letter in front of them and I know every one of them because I cut them. That number is out of the series the Table uses, and the Table numbers everything it sends and everything it makes for something that is going to be sent, out of one run, on one counter, in one hand for about sixty years, and the number on a thing like this is a fact about the book the thing was entered in. It is not a fact about the plate and it is not a fact about the mark. I have known that since the eleventh year and I have never once done anything with it."

"And you are handing it in."

"I am handing it in at the fourth hour this morning," said Garren Pyle, "and I am going to write four lines and go down to the district court with it myself, and I am going to say in the four lines that I kept it and that nobody came for it and that I have never known whose mark it is, and I am not going to say I am sorry about anything, because I am not, and a piece of paper in a file in nine years is not a place to be sorry and it is a place to be accurate."

---

The registrar's office of the district court took it at about the fifth hour of the ninth of the second month, and the clerk who took it asked him three questions and wrote down his answers, and then the registrar came out and looked at the plate on the bench in front of him for about a minute and a half without touching it.

"This is the mark," said the registrar.

"It is the mark."

"Then this is the best thing anybody has handed this court in five months and I am going to tell you in a room, in a form with a name at the foot of it, exactly what it is worth, because a person who is owed a thing is entitled to know its size before he goes and tells eight other people." He squared it with a ruler. "It is worth nothing to this court. That is not a figure of speech and I am not being unkind to you. A matrix is not a ledger. This proves that an impression was made and cut from steel. It does not say who asked for the impression. The asking is in a book, and not on this, and the book is not in this building, and if you had brought me the book I would have had a person and you would have had a house. You have brought me the tool. A tool is a thing anybody can buy."

Garren Pyle stood in a registrar's office in a district court and looked at a piece of steel on a bench.

"Then give it back."

"Certainly not." The registrar did not look up. "It goes in as what it is, which is a plate bearing a mark, and it goes in with your four lines on it, and it goes into the file of this court and it is to be produced at the examination that is directed, and when that examination is held I am going to read your four lines out first and then I am going to hold up a piece of steel in front of nine people and say *this is the mark and this does not tell us whose it is*, and about nine people in this city are going to understand at that moment what the last five months were for, and not one of them is going to be able to do one single thing about it after it."

He wrote it down in a form with his own name at the foot and read it back and offered it to Pyle to sign, which is a thing a registrar does and which Pyle had never had done to him.

> *Received from Garren Pyle, certifier, on the ninth of the second month: one press plate, steel, quarter inch at the shoulder, bearing an impression cut in the face, being a filled square with a bar through it and a notch cut out of the top left corner; and a number cut into the edge in an unknown hand. The holder states that he has held the plate eleven years, that he was not asked what it was for, that he does not know whose mark it is cut in the form of, that he is not sorry about the certificates suspended on the seventeenth of the sixth month, and that he would rather have handed it in than kept it.*
>
> *The plate is entered as a plate and is not entered as a name. This court has no power to identify the house of any mark and has been told in open court by a higher court that it does not know it, and the number cut into the edge of the plate is a fact about a book and is not a fact about the mark.*
>
> *The mark is admitted to the file as a mark. — Registrar, the district court of Auremar, the ninth of the second month.*

Garren Pyle signed it. He has signed about nine thousand cards in his life with a name, and this is the first thing he has signed with a name that anybody in a court has read.

---

The eight others are the real week.

There are about nine certifiers in this province whose certificates were suspended on the seventeenth of the sixth. Two of them are four hundred and forty miles away in a furnace town and cannot be walked to, and one of the other seven is seventy-one and sold his press in the spring and had kept the plate, which means seven of the nine are holding a plate and those seven are the seven plates, and one of the seven is him. Pyle had three of the other six addresses in his own trade's book, because a trade of eleven people knows where the other ten are, and one of the three is a house in a lane he has not been down since he was thirty, and the last three came to him from a woman of about sixty who has cut plates for twenty-two years in a lane and who asked him which number was on one before she gave him her own.

He went on foot. He did it in two days, on the tenth and the eleventh, and he did not take a case and did not take money, and of the seven doors four gave him the answer he expected and three did not, and the difference is worth about nine hundred words to nobody except the seven of them and it is the reason the week is worth having.

The answer he expected was: *it is a plate, it is in a drawer, what would you like me to do with it.* He got that four times.

The answer he did not expect was: *which number is on it.* He got that three times, and it was the seventh answer, in a lane, from a woman of about sixty who had cut plates for twenty-two years and who said, before he had got to the second sentence:

"What number."

He told her the number. He said it from the top of his head, which is a thing a man can do about a number he has looked at every day for eleven years without ever once having had a reason to.

The woman went and got a piece of paper and a pencil and wrote it down, and then she said: "That is a sending number. That is out of the Table's run. I have cut about nine hundred plates off numbers out of that run and I have never once been told what any of them was a plate *for*," and she looked at him, "and you have not either, and here is the thing I have been angry about since the sixth month and could not put anywhere. It is not that they have taken my certificates off me. It is that nobody has ever once asked me what the number on a plate is a number *of*."

The rest of that week is nine people finding out what a mark is for.

It is not for signing. That is the first thing every one of them got wrong and it is wrong the way a first thing is wrong, and it took Pyle four days to get it said properly in a room with two of them in it, on the twelfth, at about the eighth hour, over a pot of something, and what he said was this and it is the sentence the volume is standing on:

"A mark is not a signature. A signature says who did it. A mark says *this is one of ours*, to somebody who already knows what ours is. It is a way of saying a thing to a person who cannot read, and it is a way of saying it to the one person in the room who is looking for it. And in eleven years nobody has ever been hurt by a mark, because a mark does not carry a promise. A mark carries a **direction**." He put his hand flat on the table. "That is what a court has just told us. It has told us that it does not know what house keeps this one. And it has told us in open court, in a minute book, with a name at the foot, that it does not know. And the plate is in a file. And a direction is the one thing in the world that does not need anybody's permission to be followed."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Take it in," said the woman of sixty. "I will cut the other four if you carry it to them. There are four I have not asked and I have not asked because I did not know what to ask."

Seven plates went in by the fourteenth of the second month: three carried in by the people who had been holding them, and four cut and carried in by a woman of about sixty in a lane who had not been asked and had not thought to ask anybody.

That is the whole of the escalation and it is smaller than a sentence in a registrar's note. Seven plates. Two of them are the same mark cut twice, which means there were two presses in this city at some point in the last decade and nobody ever established which was which. One of them is in a hand that Pyle recognised and could not place and did not name, because naming a man because you recognise his cutting is exactly the thing this book has spent nine months warning everybody about.

The rest went into a district court in a form with a name at the foot of it, and a registrar read them in, and the registrar's note on the sixth of them is in the file, and it is a paragraph long:

> *Seven plates, produced voluntarily between the ninth and the fourteenth of the second month, in answer to nothing and in answer to a plate. Two carry the mark of the plate first received. The others carry marks this court has not seen. This court can identify no house by any of them. It records that nine persons in this province have stated in writing that they have produced every physical object in their possession bearing a mark, and that two of the nine held nothing that was a plate and said so, and that they were not asked, and that nobody had asked them in five months, and that the reason nobody asked is on the face of the order of the seventeenth of the ninth month, which protects the persons and not the objects.*

Seven plates and a paragraph. About nine people, in one week, learned what a mark is for, and three of them had to be found on foot over two days by a man with a number in his head, and four of them had to be found by a woman with a pencil who had never been asked and had thought to ask nobody, and the two officers of the district court who wrote it all down were not the people who did any of it, and the finding of the whole week is one sentence in a registrar's note about the difference between a person and an object.

Garren Pyle did not keep the plate and did not keep the letter and did not keep a copy of the minute, and he was not thanked by anybody in a court, and he went back to the room over the yard and cut four certificates that week, and the two of them that mattered were for a woman of about sixty who had taken her press back out of a cupboard after twenty-two years and had a number in her own hand on a piece of paper on the bench beside her, and they were laid and took eleven seconds each, and they had a rim on them, and there was a second impression, and the pressure varied with the hand.
