# Chapter 71: One Side of a Sheet

It came down on the morning packet of the eighth of the third month, and it was one side of a sheet, and it came down the same way the last one had, which is that it was addressed to the Docket of Powers and copied to the person who had been in the room when the question was framed, and the Docket of Powers has three rooms and a card in a window and no way whatever of finding a person, and so it sent the thing to the gallery where the person worked, which is the third gallery, and the answer arrived in an copying alcove with two people in it at about the seventh hour of the fourth of the third month being five days early for a man who reads everything twice.

Tamsin Rook read it standing up. That is how she reads everything, and she read it twice, and then she sat down, which she almost never does at the seventh hour, and read it a third time, and then she went and stood in the passage behind the gallery for about a minute and a half and did nothing at all, which is also a thing she does.

It said this.

> *In the court sitting in room four, upon the question as first referred by the Docket of Powers, being whether a reading so performed was of any legal effect whatever. Received the eighth of the third month.*
>
> *First. The question is answered. A scale reading performed by a person not entered in the register of the Docket of Powers at the time of the reading is of no legal effect whatever. That extends to the two readings found by that Docket to be unauthorised, being those of the twelfth of the ninth month of the ninth year and the sixth of the fourth month of this year, and to any other reading so performed by a person so not entered.*
>
> *Second. The record in which such a reading was entered is the record of a court and is not altered by this answer and is not disturbed by it. A finding of the Docket of Powers is a finding about a person. A record of a court is not altered by a finding about a person. The entry stands. Every act done in reliance upon it stands. No person who acted upon it in good faith is to be questioned for having done so.*
>
> *Third. The bar of the applicant upon any audit in any jurisdiction in any office is not discharged by this answer and is not varied by it.*
>
> *Fourth. This court names no person. Being asked, it states again, in a form, that it does not know and cannot compel the authority under which the instruction sealed with the device was given, and that it knows of no person at the head of any office whose standing authority sets in type the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month.*
>
> *Fifth. Nothing in this answer is a finding that any person has done wrong, and nothing in it is a finding that any record has been altered. It is an answer to the question as referred and to no other question.*
>
> *— S. Vint, presiding, room four.*

There was a second sheet behind it. The second sheet was a printed notice of the eleventh of the third month, which is three days away, saying that the court would be at a sitting in room four on that day at the fourth hour on an application of the applicant, and there was a device at the head of it and a name at the foot of it, and the words *and to grant such further order as it may think fit* in the middle, which is a form of words that is printed on about nine notices in the empire in a year and means nothing to anybody, and which sits in a sheet that has got a name under it, which is the only place it is ever printed from.

Tamsin Rook put the two sheets down on the counter, squared them, and put the kettle on, which she does at about the seventh hour, and which had nothing to do with anything.

Then she sat down and wrote in her own book, in her own hand, because she has been getting better at that for twelve months and the thing she has got better at is putting what a document says before what she wants it to say.

> *The eighth of the third month. One side. Four of the five lines are the answer and the fifth one is the reason the answer is safe.*
>
> *It is the worst of the two and I want that on the page before anything else, and it is worse than the last one, because the last one was a door and this one is a shut door with the key described. The readings were of no legal effect whatever. The record they were entered in stands. Those are the two answers that were available and the court has given both of them, on the same sheet, in the same handwriting, three paragraphs apart, and neither one contradicts the other, and a man who wanted to be pleased could read either half of it and be right about that half.*
>
> *I have been waiting since the third week of the eleventh month for a sentence that would put a person in a room. There is no person in it. It is the first answer I have had from that court that names nobody at all, and it is the first answer that is final.*

She stopped there for a bit and drank the tea standing at the window, which looks at a court yard and about nine feet of another building.

Then she went on writing.

> *Now the part I am actually frightened of, and I am going to write it in the plainest words I can find and not in the words I would use in a room.*
>
> *The second paragraph is the one that does the work. Every act done in reliance upon it stands, and no person who acted upon it in good faith is to be questioned for having done so. That is the sentence. I have read it four times and it is eleven words long and it has given permission to about sixty letters.*
>
> *This court has now said, with a name at the foot of it, that a record made at a hearing where an unauthorised person stood and read is a good record, and that everything anybody did on the strength of it is safe for them, and that they are not to be questioned. And the district that wrote those letters in the sixth year, without a hearing, to about sixty households in a district of four hundred and sixty, and could not get the names, and never asked — that district is now answerable to nothing at all. Not to me. Not to the person whose name is in the file. Not to the girl. There is nothing in that sheet anybody can use against a district that has just been told its letters were proper.*
>
> *And the other side of it, which nobody will write down because nobody can see it, is that the same sentence has made the best thing in this book permanent.*
>
> *There is exactly one record in the whole empire in which a girl of fifteen is entered as heard. She did not sign anything. She had a nightdress on over a shift and a knife and a sliced loaf and she sat in a river court and said no, and her mother asked for the opposite of what she wanted, and the court entered her as heard. That entry is a line in the register of the hearing at the district of Mosswake, and the reading that was performed that morning was the reading the Docket found unauthorised eight months later, and this morning a high court has said the record stands.*
>
> *So that line stands, with a judge's name under it, and it cannot be taken out, and nobody can ever again say she was not heard, and that is a real thing and I am not going to be the person who pretends it is nothing.*
>
> *And in the same record, in a different hand, in a column headed for the wrong thing, there is a household of the lower district entered as a person from whom a return has been requested, because she answered a form, and a person who has been asked a question is filed under asked. That line stands too. She is sixty-one and she is a bridgewarden and she signed one instrument in sixty-one years and she has refused a second, and the only line in the empire with her name in it says asked, and it is now unanswerable, because you cannot unask somebody.*
>
> *So: the reading is nothing and the record is everything and I do not know what to do on any ordinary working day after that, and I have not got used to having an ordinary working day after that.*

She read that back and did not soften it, and then she wrote one more line, which is the one that is going to matter.

> *And the number I have been carrying since the twelfth of the twelfth is a number of a bar.*

That is the whole of what happened in the alcove between the seventh hour and about the ninth, and the forty-one pages of the Cinder Court's record that sat in a folder on the end of her bench under a four-line form with **None** in the second box did not get a look in until the ninth hour, which is the honest way round it: the folder is the only thing in this matter that is entirely hers and the day a court finally answered the question was the day she did not touch it until nine.

---

She copied the answer out three times.

Not summarised, not extracted, not in a paragraph — the whole of the five points as five points, in a copyist's hand, in plain words, because a paragraph is how an answer gets turned into something else in nine years. She did it standing up, and the first one took about forty minutes and the other two took about thirty each, and she checked all three against each other at the end word by word, which she has not done since the ninth of the ninth month and does now.

Then she wrote the order down before she acted, on a separate sheet, with the day on it, because she made that mistake once in the twelfth month and has not made it since.

> *Copies of the answer of the eighth of the third month, three, made this morning. Order settled before I move:*
>
> *One, into the Docket of Powers, because the Docket referred the question and the answer to a question that office asked belongs in the file of the office that asked it and not in an alcove.*
>
> *Two, to Silas Sowle, who is the household's solicitor at the crossing at Lowcross and nobody else's, with four words on the front and no office on it and no name on it that the court had not already put there.*
>
> *Three, down the river to Ivet Sarn at the Mosswake Roll Court, for a wall, in a copyist's hand, with five words on the front.*
>
> *The Docket first this time and not the household first, and I have written down why, because last time I sent the household first and told Marek Kest four days afterwards at about the eighth hour of the evening and broke the first half of a rule twice in three months doing it. The reason is not that the household matters more. The reason is that the Docket is a room three corridors away with a man in it who is sixty years old and has a register, and if the answer is in his file before nine o'clock he will read it this morning, and if it is in his file tomorrow he will read it tomorrow, and there is no third thing. The household gets it before the fourth month either way. I am not going to pretend that a rule I made about myself is a rule about who is owed.*

She read that back too, and then she wrote one line under it, which was not part of the order.

> *And the second and the third go to the Received Room first, because the undertaking of the fourth of the first month is live and says they go to him, and I am not going to start the eleventh month by pretending a thing in my own book does not apply to two sheets in my own hand.*

---

Marek Kest was in the Received Room and had been in it since the seventh hour, and the stove was in and the window was doing what it does, which is showing him about nine feet of the wall of the light well at a height where a person cannot see anything, which is the reason the room exists.

She came in at about the ninth hour with two sheets in her hand and put them on the table and did not put them in his hand.

"Read these before they go," she said. "Read them in front of me. And if you have a reason, say the reason in a room and out loud and not on a page."

"I know what I am doing," said Marek Kest. "You said it four months ago and you said it twice before that."

"I have never once been able to tell the difference between you saying you know what you are doing and you saying it because I am standing here, and I have decided to stop trying to tell and to just make you do it."

He read both sheets. It took him about a minute and a half and he read them standing up, which she had stopped remarking on in the ninth month of last year on the grounds that it was his own business and always would be.

Then he read the second one a second time, and then he said:

"I have a reason and it is a real one, and I am going to give it to you in words, and you are going to tell me I am right, and then it is going anyway."

"Give the reason."

"The second paragraph." He put his thumb on it and did not move it. *Every act done in reliance upon it stands, and no person who acted upon it in good faith is to be questioned for having done so.* "That is eleven words and it is the most dangerous sentence anybody has written in this matter, including your four sentences about the hole in the instrument, and it is dangerous because it is the only sentence in this book that anybody might actually be glad of. Everything else we have produced has a cost on it that somebody has to pay by hand. This one has no cost on it at all. There is no district that is worse off for that sentence. There are about sixty letters that are now safe, and no household back in a field, and nobody worse off, and the reason that is, is that being worse off was never the thing that happened to them."

"Yes," said Tamsin Rook.

"I want to put the household's copy in a different order to the Docket's and I want you to say no."

"Read the two sheets. Ask me for the order."

He read them again.

"The order is right," he said. "The Docket is three corridors away and the man is sixty and if it is in his file this morning he reads it this morning. You sent the household first in the twelfth month and I have not forgotten it and I have not forgiven it, and it was right then, and it is wrong now, and the reason it is wrong now is that the thing has changed shape. In the twelfth month you were handing a household a warning. In the third month you are handing a district a court saying its letters were proper, and a warning and a permission are not the same object and they do not go in the same bag."

"Agreed," said Tamsin Rook. "And the one to the wall?"

"The wall is a wall," he said. "That is a woman four hundred and thirty miles away with a hole in a wall and eleven shillings in a cloth and about sixty households she cannot get names for, and you are putting a judge's name under a sentence that says her court did nothing wrong, and she is going to put it up because it is the only thing anybody has ever sent her from anybody who is allowed to say it, and it is going to cost her nothing to sell and it is going to be worth a shilling to about forty people, and she will have to be the one who decides whether to say what it is worth. That is not your decision to make and it is not mine."

"Agreed," said Tamsin Rook, "and I am going to write the word on it, and I am going to read it back to you first, because that is what we agreed and because I have now sent three things in this matter without asking anybody and I am counting them."

She wrote *agreed* on the sheet and the day on it, and turned the book round, and he read it, and then she made the three copies and sent the first one down the corridor to three rooms off the same passage at about the ninth hour and a half, and the second one went out with a man who was going to the lower district on his own account and did not know he was carrying anything, and the third one went down the river on the morning packet behind about forty pieces of other people's correspondence, with five words on the front:

*for the wall. read it twice. — T. R.*

---

At about the eleventh hour she went back to the arithmetic, because it was the only thing left in the morning that she could do something with.

The bill at Lowcross is nineteen pounds three and fourpence. The fund holds six pounds and eightpence for the quarter. The real cost of the crossing is about a penny a day. Four hundred and eleven people live on the far side of that water and the district's own return for the last four weeks says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings, and that number is on a card in a room over a cobbled yard and it is not wrong and it will not be disputed by anybody in this book.

She wrote it out in her own book, the way she writes everything down, in a column, with the day on it.

> *Two numbers, both correct, and they are not the same number and cannot be made the same number and it is not anybody's fault and I have known that since the second of the twelfth and I have not written it anywhere until now.*
>
> *Three hundred and eighty-eight is a number of times the bar went up and down in four weeks. It is a number of a bar. It is not a number of people, and it is not a number of households, and it is not a number of anyone who is on the far side of that water and cannot get over it in a flood, which is the only number anybody in this matter wants.*
>
> *Four hundred and eleven is a number of people. It is on nobody's return. It is in a file in a solicitor's coat in the lower district and in a paragraph a copyist wrote in an alcove in the twelfth month, and it came out of a passage in a district works' own answer where an engineer said the market and the wharf and the mill and the chapel and gave a number of people for each, and a man in a good coat added them up, and the sum is right.*
>
> *So on the third of the fourth month a solicitor is going to stand up in a room in the lower district and he is going to have to say what the crossing costs and who should pay for it, and he is going to have two figures, and one of them is a shilling a copy's worth of arithmetic about a bar, and the other one is a number of people, and he is going to have to choose which one to put in a sentence, and both of them are true, and only one of them is a number of the thing the application is about.*
>
> *I have been carrying three hundred and eighty-eight for three months as though it were an argument. It is not an argument. It is a count of a gate and I put it in a page in the twelfth month because I found it on a card and it was the only figure anybody had ever given me about that place, and I did not know what it was a number of until a man of twenty-five told me in a corridor that there was no heading at the top of his sheet and I did not ask him what the figures under the heading were a number of.*
>
> *I am not going to send him a letter about it. He is twenty-five and he would do it, and if he did it for me it would be me using a person I did not ask as a way of not going to a hut myself, and I have done that twice in a year with better people than him.*

She squared the three copies that had gone, and the original that had stayed, and then she went and looked at the folder at the end of the bench, which was the forty-one pages of the Cinder Court's record, and copied two lines out of the first order into her own book at the back where nobody looks, which is a thing she has done eleven times in twelve months and has never once told anybody about, including him.

At about the seventh hour in the evening she wrote the last of it, standing up, in the alcove, in the copyist's hand.

> *Two entries on the list and the third column is not going to get one, and I said in the last week of the first month that I was not going to fill it and I meant it and I am not filling it, and the reason I am writing the day down anyway is that this is the first morning since the fourteenth of the ninth that I have not wanted to be wrong about anything in this matter, and I would like that to be a thing somebody could find in about four years and be annoyed at.*
>
> *I wanted a document that made a person answerable. I got a document that makes a record answerable. The difference between those two things is the whole of the last four months and I did not know it was the whole of anything until this morning, and I found out at about seven o'clock in the evening with a kettle cold on a counter and forty-one pages on a bench I did not touch until nine o'clock.*
>
> *The only person in the empire that this helps is a clerk four hundred and thirty miles down a river, and what it helps her do is sell a sentence at a shilling, and I am the person who made it sellable, and I have no idea yet whether that is a good thing and I have four weeks to find out, because the application is on the third of the fourth month and she is going to be asked what it is worth by about forty people who have never met me.*
>
> *T. Rook, the eighth of the third month, at about the seventh hour, at the counter, with the lamp lit.*
