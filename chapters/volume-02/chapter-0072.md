# Chapter 72: A Different Thing Wearing the Same Word

The sitting in room four on the eleventh of the third month lasted eleven minutes and Marek Kest was in it for nine of them, and the reason he has written the whole of it out in his own book that evening is that he was handed a thing and he wants to be able to say in four years exactly what the room looked like.

It smelled of heated seal-wax, which he can find in a building by from the bottom of the stair, and of the cold off the great stair of the Crown Kiln in the third month, which is not really cold but is a temperature that makes people hurry. The registrar read the terms the way a man reads a thing he has read once and does not intend to read again, and at the end put his hand on the paper.

"Mr Kest. I am going to read this again in about nine months to somebody who has never met you, and I would like it to be right."

"Read it as many times as you like," said Marek Kest.

"It is one page and it is a grant of a limited audit, and I say at the head of it that this court has granted four things in about nine years and three of them were asked for by the applicant."

---

The grant, in full, because the whole of this chapter turns on the difference between two words in the fifth line of it.

> *In the court sitting in room four, upon the application of Marek Kest, clerk, made in consequence of the answer of this court of the eighth of the third month. The eleventh of the third month.*
>
> *A limited audit is granted. It is confined as follows.*
>
> *One. The subject of the audit is the two entries in the register of the Docket of Powers for the twelfth of the ninth month of the ninth year and the sixth of the fourth month of this year, and nothing else. It is not an audit of any record of any court. It is not an audit of the Venn file. It is not an audit of the instrument of nineteen years back at the crossing at Lowcross, of the returns of the district works, of any numbered series, or of any standing instruction.*
>
> *Two. It is to be conducted in one room in the city of Auremar and not in any other place, and **it may not be carried out in any district.***
>
> *Three. It is to be conducted within fourteen days of this grant, by a person named at the foot of it, and that person is the applicant, and it is to be conducted in his own time and at his own wage.*
>
> *Four. It may take a document which a person produces of its own motion. It may compel no document, no office, no witness and no return. No finding made under it may be used in any proceeding in any court other than this court, and no finding made under it may be produced in any proceeding without leave of this court.*
>
> *The bar of the said person upon any audit in any jurisdiction in any office is not discharged by this grant and is not varied by it, and no part of the answer of the eighth of the third month varies it.*
>
> *— S. Vint, presiding, room four. Audit granted to M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the eleventh of the third month.*

Marek Kest read it standing in the room, which he did for about four minutes and which the registrar afterwards entered in the file as *the applicant read the grant standing up and did not sit down and did not ask for time*, which is the kind of thing registrars enter.

Then he asked one question, and it is the only question he has asked in that room in thirteen months and it was not about the case.

"Registrar. The second line says the subject of the audit is two entries in a register. Are the two entries a record?"

"They are entries in a register of persons," said the registrar, "which is a book, and a book is a record, and this court is not going to be drawn into the difference in a corridor."

"Then that is a no with a reason in it and I will take it," said Marek Kest, and signed the foot of it, which is where his name goes because a grant that does not say who is conducting the thing is a grant of nothing, and he asked for that on the eleventh of the ninth month and got it in a different sentence in a different building.

---

He was back in the Received Room before the fifth hour and put the grant on the table and stood over it, and Tamsin Rook read it twice in about four minutes and then read the second line nine times.

"It may not be carried out in any district," she said.

"No."

"That is not a limitation on the audit. That is the audit. A district is the only place in the empire where the harm is and where a person can be found at a counter with a paper in her hand." She put her thumb on it. "Auremar's lower district is a district. Ivet Sarn's court is a district court. The Reed Cut water office is a district office. The place the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth went to forty-six times is a district."

"Yes."

"And you signed it about an hour ago."

"He read the whole of it out in a room at the fourth hour at the top of his voice and offered me a second reading, and I said read it as many times as you like, and I would have said no to a grant of that size in about nine seconds if I had not been reading it while he was offering."

"You would not," said Tamsin Rook. "You would have said yes in nine seconds and taken it away and read it in here, and then you would have been a man who took a grant and found out afterwards what it was for, and that is the thing you did in the ninth month of last year and it is the reason there are two readings in a register with your name against them."

"Write that down and put the day on it," said Marek Kest, "and then read it back to me, because I want it in the book in a hand that is not mine."

She wrote it down and read it back, and then copied the grant out in plain words and made the copy for Silas Sowle and put it in an envelope with the copy of the answer of the eighth of the third month that had gone to the lower district three days before, and she did not seal it, and she put it on the corner of his table.

"That is going out of this matter to a solicitor," she said. "You read it in front of me before it goes and you give me a reason in a room and out loud."

"I am going to do that and I am not going to pretend it is the same thing," said Marek Kest. "The undertaking is about paper going out. This is about me telling a man something to his face, and I am telling you that because I have been in this building thirteen months doing the second one and calling it the first one about nine times, and the difference is the only thing I have got."

"Write that down as well," said Tamsin Rook, "because it is the first sensible thing you have said in about six weeks and I would like it on the page in case you stop being sensible."

---

Silas Sowle came up at about the tenth hour of the thirteenth of the third month and had been in the building since half past eight, and he had not come to see Marek Kest, and he had told nobody he had come.

He is fifty-five or thereabouts and has done this district's poor business for nineteen years and says on the record that he is not good at it, and he said it again on the stair, unasked, in the way a man says a thing he has got used to saying.

"I have had your copyist's four words since the evening of the tenth," he said. "Read all of it. And I have been sitting in a room downstairs with a copy of a court's answer and I cannot make one single thing of it, and I have been in this city since half past eight and I have not been to a counter and I have not been to a court and I have not sent anybody anywhere, because I am the household's solicitor and nobody else's, and I have wanted an hour to work out what to do before I did anything at all, which I am told is a habit of a person in my position and I have never before had a habit worth the name."

"You have three weeks," said Marek Kest.

"I have nineteen days," said Silas Sowle. "The application is on the third of the fourth and I have to file on the second, and the court sits at the second hour, and I know both of those because they are in the refusal of the second of the twelfth month and the refusal gave me a date, which is the only reason I have any hope at all."

He put a hand on the table, not on the envelope.

"Then you have read the grant."

"I have read the grant. It is one page and it is the plainest paper that has come into my hands in nineteen years and I understood it on the second reading." He looked at it. "May it not be carried out in any district. Auremar's lower district is a district. Is it not."

"It is."

"Then the grant is not for me," said Silas Sowle, "and the answer of the eighth of the third month is not for me either, and I would be obliged if somebody would tell me what any of it is for, because I have a woman of sixty-one who is going to be asked a question on the third of the fourth and I am the person who asks it."

Marek Kest did not sit down. He had not sat down since he came in, and he stood on the other side of a table that is not big enough for two people to work at, and he told Silas Sowle in a room, out loud, standing, in about eleven minutes.

He told him what a grant is and what it does and what it is not, and that the reason it cannot be carried out in a district is not that a court thinks a district unworthy of an audit — this court said nothing of the kind — but that the reason is in the fourth line, which is that a finding under it cannot be used in any proceeding in any court other than the one that granted it, and a district court is a different court. He believed the drafters meant that. He had not tried very hard to establish it, because the second line says *it is not an audit of the instrument of nineteen years back at the crossing at Lowcross* in as many words, and there is no way to read that sentence with a district in it.

Then he told him what he had, and it was this: a record of a court in another province that is the only record in the empire in which a girl of fifteen is entered as heard and a household is entered as asked; and a high court that has now said, with a name at the foot of it, that the reading performed in that room was of no legal effect whatever, that the record stands, that every act done in reliance upon it stands, and that nobody is to be questioned for having done it — which means the letter this court sent to about sixty households in the sixth year is now proper and unchallengeable. He had hoped for eleven months for the opposite of that. What has arrived is the other thing, and he would not have believed that a court could hand a person a defeat and a victory in three paragraphs on one side of a sheet and be correct in both.

Silas Sowle listened to all of that without moving, and at the end of it he said:

"Then what is it for."

"It is for the person who did the reading," said Marek Kest. "It is not for anybody who was read about."

"Was he not."

"He was barred from any audit in any jurisdiction in any office eleven weeks ago and he is barred now, and the answer says so in the third line and it was written by the same hand as the second line and the second line is the one that helps your client and the third line is the one that does not, and I have not decided yet whether that is a court that is being fair or a court that is being tidy, and I have been chewing on it for three days."

---

And then Silas Sowle did the thing that Marek Kest has thought about every day since, and which is the reason the whole of the twelfth month of last year is a live instrument in this book and not a memory.

He took out a sheet, and he wrote on it, and he turned it round.

> *Mr Kest. — I am going to use the rule on the front of the copy your copyist sent me, and I am telling you I am using it because you will want to know that I read it.*
>
> *The question: does the grant of the eleventh of the third month reach the crossing at Lowcross or the household's position in it?*
>
> *The date: the fifteenth of the third month, at about the eleventh hour, in a room, in words, and not on a page.*
>
> *I am a solicitor of fifty-five who has said on the record that he is not good at this, and I have never once in nineteen years written a line like that to a clerk. I got it off a sheet of paper with four words on the front and no office on it. You will notice that it costs the writer nothing, and that it is the only instrument in this building that nobody in it can answer by writing something.*
>
> *S. Sowle. The thirteenth of the third month.*

Marek Kest read it twice.

"You have written to me," he said, "using a rule two people in this building made with each other in the twelfth month of last year. I am one of the two. The other one is standing in the room. Three things about that, and the first is that it works."

"Yes."

"The second is that you have no standing to make it and I am going to answer it anyway, because the rule is not about standing. It is about a person putting a choice in writing with a date on it and another person being made to answer out loud, and the whole of what is wrong with this matter is that nobody in it will say a thing out loud in a room with a witness in it, and a stranger with nineteen years of poor business behind him has just done the single most useful thing anybody has done in this building in four months."

"The third," said Silas Sowle.

"The third is that I am not going to enjoy it, and I am going to do it on the date you have written and not before, and if I answer you early it will be because you asked me and not because I could not wait."

"I did not ask you to."

"No. I am telling you that I will not enjoy it, and the reason is that the rule is mine and it is being used on me by a man who read it off a sheet, which is exactly what I asked for in the fourth month of the first month. I have got it. It is worse than I said it would be, because I did not know when I asked for it that the point of it was that I would also be on this side of it one day. The only thing I have to give a man who came two hundred yards to be told no is a list of foolscap with my own bar on the top of it."

---

The answer was given at about the eleventh hour of the fifteenth of the third month, in the Received Room, in words, with Silas Sowle standing on one side of a table and Tamsin Rook standing at the door because she was copying and would not go out, which is a thing she does.

The answer was: no.

The grant does not reach the crossing and does not reach the household and does not reach the instrument of nineteen years back, and the reason is in the second line, and Marek Kest read the second line out loud in a room to a man of fifty-five, and then he read the fourth line out loud as well, because the fourth line is the one that explains the second, and a man who is not good at it needs both.

And then, because a rule that produces only a no is a rule that anybody could have written, he gave him the other thing.

He gave him a list of paper, and it is the only thing he had, and it took about nine minutes to dictate and Tamsin Rook wrote it standing up in the copying alcove doorway on the back of a sheet of foolscap, and it is this:

> *One. The eleven-line schedule and the number on the face of the wrapper. You have it. Take it out of the coat on the third and put it on the table in front of the magistrate, because a file that is in a coat is a file that can be lost and a file that is on a table cannot.*
>
> *Two. The form the works returned on the reverse, and the sentence on it: the form is returned because the form cannot be entered and not because the work is refused. That is nineteen words and it is the most useful thing the district works has ever said about itself and nobody has ever read it out in a room. Read it out.*
>
> *Three. The line the magistrate entered on her own motion, that no officer of the district works has at any time asked this district to stand the cost of any crossing. You cannot have that; it is hers. Ask her, in a room, in words, on a date, whether she will let you read it into your paper. She may say no. If she says no, ask her why, in the room, and write the reason down, because the reason will be better for you than the line.*
>
> *Four. The works' return of four weeks, which is three hundred and eighty-eight crossings, and the schedule of the passage about the market and the wharf and the mill and the chapel, which is four hundred and eleven people. Put both in, as two numbers, and say what each of them is a number of. The day somebody adds one of them to the other is the day a magistrate stops being able to be a court and starts being an office. That is not law. That is arithmetic, and I am giving it to you because I have been carrying the wrong one of the two for three months and found out on the eighth of the third month that I was wrong, and nobody made me.*
>
> *Five. Nothing else. Do not put the answer of the eighth of the third month in, and do not put the grant in, and do not put anything I have said to you in this room in. The answer is about a record in another province and the grant is about two lines in a register three corridors from here, and if either is in a file in that court in nine years a person will read both and conclude that a clerk of the Roll Hall had an opinion about a bank.*
>
> *That is the whole of what I have. None of it is law, I am not a lawyer, and I have been barred from any audit in any jurisdiction in any office since the third week of the eleventh month and I am barred now. Know that before you use it. A thing said in a room by a barred man is a thing anybody may put in front of a jury with his name at the top of it.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the fifteenth of the third month, at about the eleventh hour. Read back and confirmed in the room.*

Silas Sowle folded it in three and put it in an inside pocket and said, "That will cost me a shilling a sheet to copy and I have never had a client who could afford that," and Marek Kest said, "It is foolscap and it is one side and you will copy it at a chandler's counter for a penny a sheet if you go to a girl of seventeen in Candlewright Lane and she will not charge you more, and I am not going to tell her to do it, and if you go and she is there, she is there."

Then he held out his hand, which nobody in that room expected, and Silas Sowle took it, and said:

"I have wanted to win this since the second of the twelfth and I have not thought about a bank once."

"Then it is not about a bank," said Marek Kest. "It is about a number that somebody has to be answerable for, and there is nobody in that file to be answerable, and that is the whole difficulty and it is not going to be solved by the third of the fourth."

---

At about the seventh hour that evening Marek Kest wrote in his own book, with the stove in and the bar on the inside of the frame, and he wrote four lines and then stopped for a while and then wrote four more, which are on the page and have never been read by anybody, because he did not bring them out.

> *The fifteenth of the third month. I answered a question in a room in words with a date on it and the question was put to me in writing with a date on it by a man who got the rule off a sheet of paper, and I want it written down that the rule worked, and that the thing it did was make me say no to a person who wanted the only thing I had, in a room, standing, out loud, in about eleven minutes, with a woman copying at the door who was not going to close it.*
>
> *I asked for that in the fourth month of the first month and told her it was worse than the rule we made. I was right, and it is worse than I said, because I did not know when I asked for it that the point of it was that I would also be on this side of it one day, and the only thing I had to give a man who came two hundred yards to be told no was foolscap with my own bar on the top of it.*
>
> *The grant is accepted, it is one page, it is the correct size, and it does not reach the only place in the empire where anybody is presently owed anything that can be put in a sentence. Granting it is not a small version of what I asked for. I was told that about a request before, in the ninth month of last year, when nine paragraphs became one district. It is the same sentence in different clothes, and the difference is that last time the thing was still going to be useful somewhere.*
>
> *The bar is not discharged. I asked for no relief and meant it in the eleventh month of the first month and I mean it now, and the reason is that if a bar is lifted by being answered, then this morning it was answered, and it is still on.*
