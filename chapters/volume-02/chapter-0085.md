# Chapter 85: The Third Time

He came on the nineteenth of the sixth month at about the fourth hour in the afternoon, and he was let in without a question, because the clerk of the intake desk writes the time a thing arrives in a book with no heading at the top of it and has never once been asked to write a heading on it, and the time on that afternoon is in the book and nobody will read it in four years.

He is a certifier of about forty. He has a press he does not own and a day-book he keeps in his own hand and about four hundred certificates, thirty-one of which were called in on the fourth of the second month of this year by an officer who read the list off the counter and did not ask him a single question, because there is no question to ask a man about a number on a counter. He has been doing it since he was twenty-two. He is not sorry about the thirty-one. He has not been asked about them and he has not thought about them and he is not going to think about them this afternoon, because he is here about something else and he is about four minutes late getting to it.

He put it on the table.

---

It is a card about the size of a playing card of good commercial paper, and in the middle of it there is an impression.

It is not three words. It is not a filled square with a bar through it and a notch out of the top left corner, which is what nine hundred people in four trades have spent the last fourteen months looking at. It is a **scale impression**, and a scale impression is a different animal entirely: it is pressed into damp card, it stands about a sixteenth of an inch proud at its edge, it has a rim, and inside the rim there is a second impression, smaller, where the seal settled the second time because the hand went down twice.

And it has pressure. The pressure varies across its width. It is heavier at the left of the device than at the right, and the variation goes the way a hand goes.

It is, in other words, exactly what a true seal looks like.

"Read that before I say anything," said the certifier. "I know what it is. I want you to look at it properly first, because everybody who has ever looked at one has looked at it wrong."

Marek Kest looked at it for about a minute and a half. He did not touch it, because the notary of this city had taught him that in a corridor in the eleventh month of last year, and because a thing you can only see once should be looked at once.

"Go on," he said.

"It is a seal," said the certifier. "It is not my mark and I cannot tell you whose mark it is, and I have had the die in my drawer for two months and sixteen days and I have not used it and I have not sold it and I have not copied it, and I have not written down the number on the edge, which I am going to do now because you will ask and I would rather have it in front of you than in a drawer."

He took a small book out of his coat. It is a day-book, about four inches by two and a half, ruled in five columns, kept in his own hand for eighteen years, and he turned it to the last written page and put it on the table next to the card.

It has one line in it that is not his work.

> *N. 44,819. Die. House mark. Kept. Two months back.*

"Forty-four thousand eight hundred and nineteen," said Marek Kest.

"That is a number," said the certifier, "and I have known since I was twenty-three that a number in the edge of a die is not a number of the die. It is a number of a book. The Table numbers everything it sends and everything it cuts for a thing that is going to be sent, out of one run, on one counter, in one hand, and the number goes in the edge of the die because the die is for a thing that is going out of the building. **I have never known what forty-four thousand eight hundred and nineteen is a number of, and I have written it in my own book, in my own hand, twice, and that is a thing I can do, because it is my book and my hand and nobody has ever asked me for it.**"

"And you were told it was a House mark."

"I was told it was a House mark. A man brought it in a leather case in the third of the fourth month and said keep it, do not use it, do not sell it, and you will be told if it is wanted, and it has not been wanted, and he did not give a name and I did not ask, because I am a certifier and about four hundred people in this city have brought things to me and I do not ask, and that is not a rule anybody wrote for me, that is just what the trade is."

---

He came to it then, and he came to it in about a minute, and he had clearly been carrying it for some days.

"The application in the fourth month," he said. "I read the return of it. I read it because the paper came back through the trade and everybody in the trade read it, and it says the works cannot certify whether the crossing at Lowcross is a work of the district or of a holder, and it says the works will not certify, and it says the reason is that certifying is a promise to inspect. And I want to say to you that the works man is right about that and I would not certify it either if I were him, and that is not the point.

"The point is that a certificate ends a dispute. That is what a certificate is. I have been doing this eighteen years and I have certified about four hundred things, and a certificate is a thing a court acts on the same day, and I have watched a district court in this city act on a certificate in about four minutes, and it was the right thing to do and the person who gave it to them was owed a great deal of money and got it inside a year.

"So here is what I have come to say. I will certify that the crossing at Lowcross is a work of the public of that district and is not the work of a holder. Not as a guess. I have been up that yard twice this year, on my own time, and stood at the end of the bar, and I have watched that crossing used every week of my life by more people than I could count standing at a gate. I do not know how many there are and I have not counted and I am not going to. What I know is what I saw. A woman with a sack goes over three times a morning. Four of them go over on a cart with a load. A mill sent two carts down the river in the twelfth month and paid a toll for them. And there are people who cross and the bar does not go up at all, and I asked a woman about that on the second of the fifth and she told me about a gate and a plank, and I have not been able to think of anything else since. There is no line in that fund for a bridge and there has not been one since I was a boy, and every pound of that crossing in nineteen years has come out of a box in somebody's kitchen.

"If a certificate says it is a work of the district, then it is a work of the district, and the district has to stand it, and the woman does not, and the box in the kitchen does not, and it is a thing that is finished in a week. And I have brought the die because I have wanted to do this since the fourth month and I could not find a way to do it that was not stealing, and this is the way that is not stealing."

He put his hands flat on the table.

"I am not being paid for this and nobody has asked me to and there is no house behind it. I am forty-one and I have a press I do not own. If you want to call it a forged seal I will not argue with you about the word, because I do not know the word, and if you want me to go to a court on my own account and say where a die came from, I will do that at my own cost and I will do it in a day, and I have thought about that and I have decided I will, and that is the whole of my offer and there is nothing behind it."

---

Marek Kest did not answer for about two minutes.

He was aware, and he wrote it down afterward, that he was doing the thing he does, which is to look at the object for longer than the object needs, because in fourteen months the two times he has been beaten he was beaten by a document that answered before he had finished arranging himself.

"Two things," he said at last. "I am going to say them in the order I found them and I am going to be fair to you, because you have come here on your own time and offered something and I do not think there is anybody in this city more entitled to be told the truth than you.

"**One. You are right.** About nine cases out of ten a certificate ends a dispute, and in the ordinary case the person who gives it is owed money and gets it, and the works man is right that certifying is a promise to inspect and I would not certify it either. And what you saw at the end of that bar is true, and there are four hundred and eleven people on the far side of that water according to a schedule I have read and cannot vouch for, and a headcount is not a crossing and I know that now and did not know it in the fifth month, and a mill that went two carts down the river in the twelfth month is a real fact with a real toll on it, and I have been in this matter for fourteen months and nobody has ever stood at that bar and said any of that out loud before, and you did it on your own time and you are right.

"**Two. I am not going to use it.** And I am not going to use it for a reason that has nothing to do with you, and you are going to be angry, and I am going to tell you the reason anyway."

"Tell me."

"This seal is not a seal." Marek Kest did not touch the card. "It has a rim. It has a second impression. It has pressure that varies across the width and goes the way a hand goes. It has every single thing a real one has, and it was made in about eleven seconds, or somebody wanted you to think it was. And there is a number in the edge of the die, and the number is a number of a book, and the book is in a press room off the Crown Kiln stair about a hundred yards from where I am sitting, and I have stood in that room and read that book, and the book has four lines on a card for every thing that has left the Notaries' Table in sixty years, and the fourth line is *by whose authority* is sent.

"**I cannot show you that line, and I cannot tell you what is in it, and that is the whole of my reason, and it is a reason about a book and not about you.** A seal like that is made by cutting a die out of an impression that somebody else laid. And when you cut a die out of a laid impression, you copy the copy — and every fault in the original is in the die, and when the die is laid, the fault is in the card twice, and the two faults are the same fault, in the same place, at the same depth, in the same order. **A hand cannot do that. A hand cannot repeat a pressure.** And that is the only test there is and I have got it from a woman of about sixty in a lane who cuts plates and who was not in this room and is not going to be.

"So I have in front of me a thing that will end a bridge dispute in a week, and behind it a book in a press room, and I cannot get the book, and I will not put a thing in front of a district court that I cannot account for. Not this week, and not with you offering it free, and not knowing your name is not the point."

---

The certifier took about nine seconds and said one thing.

"You have refused it," he said. "You have looked at it and it is the best-looking thing I have ever taken out of a drawer and you have refused it, and you have given me the reason twice and the reason is a book. I believe the reason. I do not think it is a good enough reason. And I am not going to argue with you about it in this room, because I can see that you would let me, and I would rather not have won that."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair at all," said the certifier. "It is four pence and half an hour. That is what my trade is worth to a court when it gives something up. I am telling you that as a fact and not as a complaint, because I have read the paper about the man of sixty and I know what it says and I know what a name costs now. You have refused the same thing three times, have you not."

Marek Kest put the pen down.

"Yes," he said. "I have, and I am going to say it out loud because you have earned it and because I would rather be judged on a habit than have somebody discover it. **The ninth of the second month: a plate came into a registrar's office in a district court and a registrar said out loud that a tool is a thing anybody can buy, and I read the account of it in a copying alcove at about the sixth hour and I did not go and look for the book. That was not a decision. That was an absence, and I have counted it anyway, because an absence and a decision have the same result and the result is what people keep asking me about.**

"**The eighteenth of the second month: four lines of a delivery stub with a supervisor's hand in the fourth column, and I had a man, and I did not build towards him, and I wrote in my own book that building a case towards a person is the same move as building a chain out of four true facts and one invented join.**

"**And this.** Three, and the same reason all three times, in the same words, and I have said it to you and it is going into a record: a document that cannot be produced cannot be protected, and a mark that cannot be read can only be described, and a description does not name anybody.

"And I want the other half said out loud as well, and you will not like it. **A man who refuses the same thing three times has a habit and not a principle.** And a habit is cheaper to refuse than a principle is to hold, because a principle is a thing you have to hold in a room where the room is against you, and I have been in three rooms in fourteen months where the room was against me and I have got this far, and I would rather have the habit. I have decided I would rather have the habit. I would rather be a man with a habit that has refused three things than a man with a principle that has never been tested, because a principle that has never been tested is a thing I would be *telling* you about and not showing you."

"You have shown me," said the certifier.

"I have shown you that I have refused a thing. I have not shown you that I was right. Nobody in this matter can show anybody that."

---

Tamsin Rook came in at about the fifth hour and the certifier did not know who she was and she did not tell him, and she copied out four lines in a copyist's hand and read them back to the room, and it is four lines because a mark cannot be read into a record and can only be described into one, and the four lines are the whole of what any room is going to get out of that card until somebody who cuts plates for a living is asked to look at it.

> *A card of good commercial paper. In the centre, a scale impression standing proud of the surface at its edge, with a rim, and within the rim a second and smaller impression. The pressure varies across the width of the device, being heavier at the left than at the right. The device is a scale device and is not identified by anybody and the person producing the card does not know whose it is. The card is produced with a die and not with a matrix and the die is produced with no ledger and no day-book of any trade and no person named.*
>
> *A seal laid and spoken takes about eleven seconds and leaves a rim and a second impression and a pressure that varies with the hand. This card has all three. Whether the hand was there, or a copy of the hand was there, cannot be determined by looking at the card, and is not determined here by anybody.*

The card went to the district court of the lower district on the twentieth of the sixth month, in an envelope with four words on the front and no office on it, and Marek Kest read it in front of her before it went, and gave no reason, and she wrote *agreed* and the day on it.

The certifier's die went back into his drawer and his day-book went into his coat and his name went into a line of a minute in a district court four miles away, and he is a person in a file now, which he was not on the nineteenth of the sixth month, and he said in the yard on the way out, to nobody, that it was the first time anybody had written his name down in this city in eighteen years and that he had not expected to be thanked and had not been going to ask.

Nobody thanked him. There was nothing in the room to thank him for, and the real cost of that crossing is about a penny a day, and a woman of sixty-one in a yard four hundred yards from that bar has been putting the penny a day into a box in her kitchen since before the parties to this case were born, and a plate with somebody's device on it would have put the bill of nineteen pounds three and fourpence on somebody else's desk by Friday and would not have moved a penny of it.

The thing that would have ended it in a week is in a drawer in the Kiln sheds, and there is one trade in this city that can look at a piece of steel and say what was done to it, and the court is going to have to go out of its own building to find that trade, and a trade is made of people.
