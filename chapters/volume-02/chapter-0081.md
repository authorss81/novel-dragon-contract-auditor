# Chapter 81: Four Pence and a Column

There were three ways to move a sheet of foolscap with two boxes ruled on it, and she went through all three of them at her own bench in the copying alcove on the tenth of the fifth month, in about forty minutes, and ruled one underneath the other two, and wrote down the three of them in her own book with the day on it, and they are the first thing in that book in five months that anybody could pick up and understand in nine seconds.

The first was to an office. That makes it a document with an office at the top of it. There are five hundred of those in this building, every one of them can be asked what it is for, and the whole of what the two boxes are for is that nobody asks.

The second was by post to a person. That makes it a document about her, in a copyist's hand, dated in the fifth month, in a matter the recipient cannot read, and she used exactly that reasoning in the second month of last year to talk a woman four hundred and thirty miles down a river out of writing eighteen letters, and the woman was right and she has never got over it.

The third was a list.

---

She got to the list at about twenty past four in the afternoon and sat with it for a while.

The list is the only one of the three that is not stupid. The other two are refusals dressed as methods. A list of people who could hand a sheet to a person who cannot read is the correct shape, and it is four names long inside four minutes, and she wrote all four of them down, and then she looked at what she had written, and then she took a fresh sheet and wrote something underneath it, and the something underneath it is this and it is in her own hand.

> *A list of people who can be found is a list of doors. Garren Pyle has four doors in a book with no office on it about four streets from where I am sitting and he wrote down, in his own hand, on the second of the fifth month, that the first thing anybody would do with a list like his in nine years is not to find out about a plate.*
>
> *I have been holding four pages of a list of the same kind since the seventh month of last year and I have said out loud in four rooms that a copy is a weapon and a target. I am not writing four more names down in a book. I am going to tear this sheet up and I am going to write at the top of it why, and the why is the whole of it.*
>
> *— T. Rook, the tenth of the fifth month, at about the half past four in the afternoon, at my own bench.*

She tore it up and put the pieces in the waste box under the counter, which is a thing you do with a document in a building of four hundred people and it is not the same as burning anything and it is not going in a press.

---

Then she went up the corridor to the room with the barred door and put the two ruled sheets on the table and asked him the question she had come to ask, which is the plainest one anybody has asked in five months and the only one in this matter that nobody has answered.

"What is a thing for," said Tamsin Rook, "before anybody has used it twice."

Marek Kest did not answer for about a minute and a half, and she let him, because she has learned what a minute and a half in that room means.

"You are asking it the wrong way round," he said at last. "You are asking what it is for, as though it is a rule. It is not a rule. Granting it is not a small version of the thing asked for; it is a different thing wearing the same word, and I said that out loud standing up to a man in the twelfth month of last year and it is the best sentence I have."

"Then I will ask it properly. What is it for before anybody has used it twice."

"It is for somebody," said Marek Kest. "That is the whole answer and it took you four months and a book to get to and it took me fourteen months to arrive at. Every instrument anybody has made in this matter has been made so that a **person** may do a thing. The hearing is so that a person may be heard. The witness order is so that a person may be named without being bought. The column on a counter is so that a person may be counted. None of them is for anything. All of them are for somebody, and the somebody is almost always the one who is least able to come."

"Then what do we do with it."

"We work out who is going to hand it over," he said, "and we work out who is going to keep the column, and we work out where the four pence comes from. Those are three questions and they are not law and they are not clever, and if we get one of them wrong then the two boxes are a piece of paper."

"And if we get all three right?"

"Then in about two years there is a person in a lane who has one and there is no name on it and a day on the back of it, and nobody in this building will ever hear about her, and that is the correct outcome and it will look exactly like nothing happening."

---

Then she did the money, standing up, because you cannot do this sitting down.

The price is four pence. She set that on the twenty-sixth of the fourth month and it has been a sentence about a person ever since: a woman with four children and no candle can walk sixty miles and pay four pence and owe nobody anything, and a penny is a trade price, and a trade price is a price a person who is not in a trade cannot use because they do not know the counter exists.

That has all been true for a fortnight. On the tenth of the fifth month she found out that four pence is also **a number**, and that the number has to come out of an account.

A sheet of foolscap is about a halfpence. Ruling two boxes by hand takes about a minute and a half, because a ruled box cannot be bought in this city in any shop she has been to in a year. Writing the day and an initial on the back takes about ten seconds. So a sheet is about a minute and forty seconds of a copyist's working time and a halfpence of paper, which is a cost of about three-quarters of a penny, and the price is four pence, and the difference between the two is three and a quarter pence a sheet and it is not a margin and it is not a wage and it is not going to anybody.

It is the price. That is the whole of it. She wrote the sentence out twice because she wanted to be sure she had not written a nastier one by accident, and the second version is on the page in her book, and it is this.

> *Four pence is not the price of paper and not the price of the ruling and not the price of my hour. It is the smallest sum in this empire that a person who has been told about a thing once, by another person, can hand over without owing anybody anything afterwards. I have priced a rule. I did not know that is what I was doing on the twenty-sixth of the fourth month and I would have told anybody who asked me that I had set a price on a sheet.*
>
> *And it is going to have to come out of my own account, because there is one account in this matter that can pay for it and it is not the Roll Hall's and it is not the Docket's and it is not a fund, and I am the only person in this matter who is paid for anything I do with a pen.*
>
> *— T. Rook, the tenth of the fifth month.*

---

She ruled eleven sheets on the tenth, in a dead hour, between the fourth and the sixth, and it took her about twenty-eight minutes all told, which is about two and a half minutes a sheet and not the minute and forty she had costed, and she wrote the corrected figure underneath the wrong one and did not cross the wrong one out, because a clerk who fills in a gap in a reason is a clerk who has decided what the reason was, and that sentence is not hers, it is a clerk of nine years at the Inheritance Desk's, and it is true.

On the eleventh she gave four of them away.

Not by post. At a counter, to people who were already standing there, in about nine minutes, and the counter was the end of the intake run in the third gallery, and she stood at the end of it the way she had stood at the end of it on the twenty-second of the fourth month looking at a clerk's book upside down from four feet away, and she did not say anything to anybody for the first four minutes.

The four were: a woman of about fifty with a bundle of papers under her arm who wanted to know whether a sum on a paper was what it looked like, and who did not want to be told, and who was told anyway in one sentence and did not go; a young man of about twenty-three who had signed something on the sixth of the third month and did not know what he had signed and did not want to write his name anywhere and was given a piece of paper with a day on it and an initial and no name on it, and read the back of it and put it in his coat; a woman of about thirty-five who took one and did not look at it and put it straight in her sleeve, and who was not asked, because the rule is that nobody is asked anything; and a man of about sixty who read the front, which is the two boxes and three lines, and could not read a paragraph, and had his wife read it to him, and then said, out loud, in a queue, to nobody, *that is the only thing in this building that does not want anything off me*, and gave it back, and did not take one.

So it is three.

The man of sixty went about four steps and then came back, which she had not expected, and stood at the end of the counter and asked her, in a voice that four people could hear, whether a person was allowed to take two.

"You are allowed to take two," said Tamsin Rook.

"I am not after two," said the man. "I am after whether I am allowed to, because the last person that asked me for something in this building asked me twice."

"You are allowed to take two and you are not asked anything and nobody is going to know you had one, and the reason I am telling you that is that a person who has been asked twice does not need a rule about being asked and a person who has not has no idea that the rule exists. It is not fair. It is the only shape I have got."

He took one and put it in the same pocket as his wife's and went up the run, and she entered four lines against the day and one of them says *returned* and it is the only one of the four that is not a giving and she left it in.

So it is three given and one returned, and the column says both, and that is the correct shape of the column and it is the first thing in five months she has written that is not a sentence.

---

The column is in her own book and she ruled a fresh page for it on the eleventh, and the page has a heading and the heading is the one she argued about for two days in the fourth month and has not changed.

> *Given.*

Three lines against it. A date, a hand's worth of description in six or seven words, and her own initial, which is not her name and is not a mark and is a thing she has done for two years on the corner of a sheet she has copied for somebody else.

The finding of the morning, which she wrote out at about the eleventh hour and read back twice, is four lines and it is the whole of what she has.

> *A column has to be kept by a person.*
>
> *That is all. It is not a rule and it is not a finding and I am not going to dress it up. A column headed Given is a book with a person's hand in it, and a book with a person's hand in it can be found, and the only person in this matter who can be found is me, and I have spent five months building a form whose whole purpose is that the person holding it cannot be instructed, and I have just written a column that a person can be instructed about, on purpose, and I want the reason written down before somebody finds it in nine years and thinks I did not know.*
>
> *The reason is that a column kept by somebody nobody can find is a column that can be denied. None protects a holding and it does not protect a loss. The first half of that has been in my book since the fourteenth of the ninth month and I signed it, and the second half is the thing I did not have until this morning, and it took one sheet of foolscap and a dead hour to get it. If the person who keeps the column cannot be found, then in four years anybody who wants this matter to have never happened writes one line and the line is believed.*
>
> *So: a column, kept by a person who can be found, on a page with a heading, and the heading is what the person does and not what the clerk does. Received is what the clerk does. Given is what the person does. A third thing I have ruled out in the two days since the fourth month and I am not going to rule out in here because a copyist who starts putting alternatives on a page ends up with a page of alternatives and no rule on it.*
>
> *And the last of it, and it is the only one of these four lines that is about a person rather than about a book. I have written my own wage into a book with no office on it. There is no figure in that book that has ever been about me. I have kept it eleven months and it has got four other people's money in it and a shilling and a price for four sheets of somebody else's sentence, and as of the tenth of the fifth month it has a line in it that says what I get paid to rule a box, and I read that line back twice and I did not cross it out, and I am frightened of it in a way I have not been frightened of anything in five months, and the reason is that in nine years somebody will be able to work out what this instrument cost, and the answer is going to be a number and the number is going to be about me.*
>
> *— T. Rook, the eleventh of the fifth month, at about the eleventh hour, at my own bench, the alcove, the third gallery.*

She squared the book and put it under the tray with the forty-one pages in it, which is where it has been since the ninth month and where nobody has ever asked for it.

Then she went and found Marek Kest, because there is a rule and it is his, and she had four sheets going out of this matter to a person and an office outside the Roll Hall is not involved but a person is, and the undertaking of the fourth of the first month says she brings him every sheet that goes out of this matter to a solicitor, a household, or an office outside the Roll Hall, and three of four went to neither, and she brought him the four anyway.

He read them in about two minutes, standing, and gave one reason, and the reason was that the fifth thing on the sheet was the man of sixty who gave it back and said it was the only thing in the building that did not want anything off him, and that if that ever goes in a file it is a description of a man and it is the only one of the four that could be used against him.

"Yes," said Tamsin Rook, "and I wrote it down and I am not going to change it, and I am bringing it to you because I said I would, and because I have been wrong in this matter five times and I would rather find out about the sixth at a bench than in nine years."

"Agreed," said Marek Kest. "And write the day on your one and not only the day of the giving, and if he comes back, which he will not, that is the end of it."

"It is the day of the giving," she said. "That is the whole of the instrument. It is not a register of the giving. It is a register of a day."

"Then I want one thing said out loud and not on a page," said Marek Kest, "because we have both been wrong in this matter in front of other people and I have never once done it in front of each other. In about three weeks somebody with a tray is going to come down that run, because there is a person at the end of it who has a column with a heading on it, and the person at the end of it is you. And when they come, the thing that keeps you is not the four pence and it is not the boxes and it is not *None*. It is that you will be able to say out loud, in a room, to a stranger, exactly what this is and exactly what it is not, and I have watched you do that in a corridor about nine times this year and you have never once got it wrong."

"I have got it wrong four times this year," said Tamsin Rook, "and you were in the room for three of them."

"Yes," he said. "And I have never once been able to say afterwards which of the four I would have got right if I had not been in the room. That is the finding and you may put it in your book with the day on it and I will not read it."
