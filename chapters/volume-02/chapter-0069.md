# Chapter 69: A Document With a Man's Name at the Top of It

The press room off the Crown Kiln stair has a table in it, four chairs, a press, a shelf of cordage, and a window that looks at the wall of the light well in another building, and on the second of the third week of the second month there were two men in it and a sheet of paper on the table, and one of the men had asked for the room and one of them had not been told the room existed.

Marek Kest had asked for it in a note of about forty words that he wrote standing up in the Received Room on the fifteenth of the second month, and the note said that he was a clerk of the Roll Hall with no standing in anything, that he had been examined on the eleventh of the first month and the examination did not discharge his bar, that he had a finding in his hand that concerned a delivery stub in that room, and that he would like forty minutes and would bring the sheet himself and would not bring anybody.

Pell Vey read the note and came, and came at about the fourth hour, and did not bring a clerk.

"Well," he said.

"I am going to put a document on this table and I am going to ask you one question, and I am going to tell you the question before I put it down, because I have learned in eleven months that a person who is asked a thing they did not know they were being asked answers the wrong thing."

"Go on."

"I am going to ask you whether the Notaries' Table issued a direction to the clerk's window of the Mosswake Roll Court on the ninth month of last year to reissue a witness order in that matter with a certificate of the district entered first." Marek Kest put the sheet down. "Those are the four lines. They are a copy, in a copyist's hand, of a stub out of the delivery-stub book in this room, made standing up, with the book in place, and the leaf is on the back of my own hand and not on anything of yours. The fourth line is a column headed *by whose authority* and the answer in it is your name."

Pell Vey did not look at the sheet for about nine seconds. He looked at it, and then he read it, and then he read the fourth line a second time, and then he did the thing that beat every other argument in this case and he did it without raising his voice at all.

"A document with a man's name at the top of it is a document about the man."

"I know," said Marek Kest. "It is your sentence. You said it about me in a referral in the eighth month of last year and you put my name in the second line instead of the first, and the difference between those two things was the difference between a matter and a campaign, and I have been thinking about that sentence every working day since, and about four people in this building have heard me say so, and one of them is a man of twenty-five who counts paper at a door and who is not on your side or mine."

"Then you will understand that I am not going to be able to help you with it."

"I want to try anyway. I have brought it to you and not to a registrar because I have four documents with your name on them and I have never once asked you a question, and I have wanted to for about eight months, and I did not do it because you are the only man in this building who has never lied to me and I could not think what I would do about it if you lied."

Pell Vey sat down. He was a man of about fifty and had been head of the Notaries' Table for nineteen years and he put both hands flat on the table, which Marek Kest noticed and which cost him something to notice, because it is a thing Tamsin Rook does.

"Then ask it."

"The question is the one on the paper. Did the Table issue a direction to that window on that day."

"No," said Pell Vey.

Marek Kest waited for the rest of it, and the rest of it took about eleven seconds, and it was this:

"I have not answered your question. I have told you that I do not answer it in this room, and you will want me to explain the difference, and the difference is that your question has two answers and one of them is a man's life, and the other one is a Table's, and I am not going to give you either in a room with a window that looks at a wall."

"That is not an answer."

"It is not an answer, and it is the first refusal that has been put in a form in this matter with a name at the foot of it, and I have been waiting four months for one and you have just given it to me for free, and I would like to say that I think you did it on purpose." He turned the sheet round and pushed it back. "I am not going to be able to use it."

"No," said Pell Vey. "You are not, and I will tell you why, and it is the whole of what I have to say and I have been a supervisor for nineteen years and I have never had a chance to say it to anybody who wrote it down.

A person who signs a column headed *by whose authority* is not admitting authorship of a thing. He is discharging an office. Every sheet that has left this building in sixty years has a fourth line, and a great many of them have my name in it, and a great many of them are the wrong thing done by nobody's hand at all, because what a Table does is not decide and execute. A Table is the instrument by which an office acts, and an office that issues a direction is not the man who wrote the direction and is not the man who thought about it and is not the man who wanted it. If you take line four of a stub into a room and say *look, the man's name is here*, then in four years there will be a file in that building with my name at the top of it and a finding in it, and the finding will be that I arranged a thing, and there will be about four hundred and forty people in four offices and one trade who will read that finding and conclude that the danger is a man, and it is not a man, and in nine years there will be nobody left who remembers that the sentence of the twelfth of the ninth was printed by a standing office with no person at the top of it, because I will be the man they remember, and the standing office will be a drawer."

"And if somebody had a hearing about it," said Marek Kest, "they would be hearing about a man, and there would be a name in the room, and the four hundred and forty people would find out in a week what the name was. And that is a real thing. It is a real and terrible thing to do to a man."

"Yes," said Pell Vey. "It is. And you want me to prefer the alternative, and the alternative is that nothing is in a room, and you have spent eleven months finding out what nothing in a room looks like, and you have a board at a window four hundred and thirty miles down a river that says *thirty-one of about sixty* in letters four inches high, and you know what it looks like, and I have read about it and I would not put it in front of anybody."

---

"There is a second thing," said Marek Kest, "and it is worse than the first, and I am going to say it in this room because you are the only person I can say it to and because you are going to find out about it in about eleven days anyway."

"Go on."

"The stub says one sheet, sealed, with a direction. The sheet is gone. It went to Mosswake in the midweek packet on the ninth month of last year and it is four hundred and thirty miles away in a district court in another province, and this court cannot get it, and I cannot get it, and you cannot get it, because a district court in another province does not answer a letter about a sheet."

"Correct."

"So the finding that the Table caused a third issue of a witness order rests on four lines of a stub and a sheet nobody has, and if I take those four lines anywhere, what I am taking is a **method**. Not a wording. Nobody in this matter wrote *the present holder*. Nobody wrote the sentence of the twelfth of the ninth. A man in a good coat used three words that were already in the district's mouth in nine minutes with a hundred and forty returns to do. The Table sent a direction to a window. The window did what a window does with a direction. And in between the two of them there is a person who was in a copying room in a river court at half past eleven on the ninth of the ninth month of last year and put a certificate into the first line of an order with a press in it, and that person is not named anywhere in anything I have, and I have looked, and I have had four months."

"You have found a hand," said Pell Vey.

"I have found four lines of cardboard with your name in a column that means the opposite of what I need it to mean. I have not found a hand." He put his own hand flat on the table. "And a supervisor who arranged a thing is not a person who wrote a clause. I have been using those as the same thing since the seventh month of last year and they are not the same thing, and it has taken me a week with a plate and a number and a room with a press in it to work out that the whole case I have been building is a case about a **method of arranging**, and a method of arranging is not a clause, and there is no clause in this matter that anybody has written, and the Cinder Court's sitting in the fourth month is about a document with no person on the face of it, and that document is not a person and my four lines do not make it one."

Pell Vey sat back.

"You have had this for a week."

"Nine days."

"And you came to me first."

"You are the only person in this building I can say it to, and I have said it to you, and you have not asked me a single question about it, and I have noticed that, and I have noticed it before, and I wrote it down in the eleventh month that a man had not asked me about a yard and I wondered why."

"I have not asked you about the yard," said Pell Vey, "because you would tell me, and because I have not earned it, and because the day I ask you about the yard is the day this conversation becomes a negotiation, and I have been in this building for nineteen years and I have watched about nine negotiations and every one of them ended with a document with a man's name at the top of it."

---

The refusal was written out at the table, in about nine minutes, in Pell Vey's own hand, and it is one page, and the form is the form he has used twice before in this matter and a clerk taught him in the eleventh month of last year and it is now a habit of a man in a building where the person who taught it has left for a room with a barred door in it.

> *Refused. The applicant put to the head of the Notaries' Table, in a room, in words, the question whether the Table issued a direction to the clerk's window of the Mosswake Roll Court in the ninth month of last year to reissue a witness order in the matter of the Venn rehearing with a certificate of the district entered first. The head of the Notaries' Table declines to answer that question, in this room and in any other, and states that he has answered no part of it and denies no part of it, and states the reason, which is that a person signing the fourth line of a delivery stub is discharging an office and is not thereby admitting authorship of a thing.*
>
> *The applicant is told, and it is said to him in a room and not on a page, that this refusal is worth more to him than an answer would have been, and for the following reason. A refusal in this form, with a name at the foot of it, is a thing that can be read back in nine years by a person who has never met either of us; an answer given in a room in the second month would be a thing two people remember differently.*
>
> *I have been asked by a man of twenty-nine why I do this. I am going to answer him once, and I am not going to answer it twice.*
>
> *— P. Vey, head of the Notaries' Table, the last week of the second month, in the press room.*

Marek Kest read it twice.

"I am going to read this back to you," he said, "and then I am going to ask you to sign the record of it, which is on the back, and which is the part that is not the form."

"That is the part that matters and it is the part nobody wants."

"Sign it."

Pell Vey read the back of it — four lines, a date, and a line that said *the applicant read this refusal back to me in this room in the words on the face of it* — and then he signed it, and then he turned it round and gave it back, and Marek Kest put it in his coat.

And then Pell Vey said the thing that the chapter is for, and he said it standing up, with his hand on the back of a chair, and it was not a speech and it was not in front of a witness and it was said to a man of twenty-nine in a room with a press in it.

"Now you listen to me, because you have come here nine times in eleven months and got something wrong every time and I have watched it, and I am not going to be the man in the next nine years who was wrong about you and also right.

You are not going to get me, and I am not going to be a villain, and both of those are the same piece of news and neither of them is what you came for. If I were a villain I would have destroyed the stub. The stub is a book that has been kept for sixty years because a building that cannot prove what it sent is a building that gets told about things it does not remember doing, and I have kept a record of every sheet this office has sent in nineteen years because I read once, in a smaller building, what happens to a man who cannot say where a thing went. **I did not arrange a thing. I issued a form to a window.** A window is not a room. And the difference between those two sentences is the whole of what you have found and it is worth nothing to you and it is the truest thing I have said in nineteen years.

And you will be asked, in about four years, to name the man who put a certificate into the first line of a witness order, and you will not be able to, and I want you to hear me say now, in advance, while there is no witness, that **you will not be able to, and that this is not a failure of yours.** There is a person. He was in a copying room in a river court on the ninth of the ninth month of last year at about half past eleven and he is not in my book and he is not in yours and he may be in a book in a building in a district you have never written to. And if you spend the next four years looking for a name to put in a room, you will find one, because a man who looks for a name for four years finds a name, and then somebody in a building that is not mine will have the thing I have just refused to be, and it will be a man who has been made into the answer because you needed one."

Marek Kest stood in a press room off the Crown Kiln stair and did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"How do I write that down."

"You don't," said Pell Vey. "You write *the applicant asked a question and the head of the Table declined and signed the record of the refusal, and the reason given was that a person signing the fourth line of a stub is discharging an office*, and you put that in the file and you send it to the person it is going to and you do not put my speech in it, because my speech is worth exactly nothing and I would be embarrassed to have it in a file in nine years." He sat down again. "It is a morning's worth of nothing. I have been telling a clerk things for nineteen years and none of it has ever been worth anything, and I have made my peace with it, and you are the first one who has been kind enough to notice, and I would be obliged if you would not make a thing of it."

"You have five documents in this matter now."

"I have five," said Pell Vey. "I have had four for eight months and I have thought about the number more than I have thought about any of them. A document with a man's name at the top of it is a document about the man. I said that to you eleven minutes before I signed a fifth one, and I did it anyway, and I would like it noticed that I noticed."

---

Tamsin Rook had the copy by the seventh hour. She was not in the room and had not asked to be and was told nothing about the speech, which is the correct amount of notice.

The second of the last week of the second month is the sitting date, and the sitting has no date, and that is the next six weeks of Marek Kest's life and it is the end of what he has been able to do with a name.

He wrote it out that night in the Received Room, in a book of his own, and it is the last thing he wrote in that book for six weeks:

> *Second month, last week. Asked a man a question in a room and he refused it in a form with his name at the foot of it and I read it back and he signed the record of the refusal on the back, unasked, and did not take nine minutes about it, and it took four.*
>
> *I have five documents. I have had a name since the seventh of the ninth month of last year and I have had a hand since the eighteenth of the second month, and neither of them is a person, and a person who is going to be in a room in the fourth month of the next year to be asked about a clause does not exist, and no court is going to make one, and the Cinder Court has already said in its own hand that it cannot answer a question about a record without a person to examine.*
>
> *So: nothing. I have the fact that an office issued a form to a window in the ninth month of last year, and the fact is in a form with a name on it, and it is a form I cannot use, and the man who wrote the sentence of the twelfth of the ninth is still nobody, and the standing office that printed it is still an office with no person in it, and the registrar who wrote the note about five offices is right and has been right since the seventeenth of the sixth.*
>
> *And I want it recorded, in my own hand, with the day on it, because I am going to be asked in four years why I stopped, and the answer is not that I was frightened and it is not that I gave away. The answer is that the person I have been building a case towards is not the person who wrote the thing, and building a case towards a person is the same move as building a case out of four true facts and one invented join, and I have watched somebody else do that in a lane and I have watched myself do it in a book for nine days.*
>
> *Tamsin Rook has the copy. It is in a book of her own with no office on it and the Docket has one and Silas Sowle has one for a household and there is no fifth copy, because I did not ask for one and she did not offer, and I want that written down, because in four years somebody will ask why there are only three.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the last week of the second month, at about the ninth hour.*
