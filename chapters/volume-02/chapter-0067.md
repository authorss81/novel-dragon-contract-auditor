# Chapter 67: What It Costs, in Pounds, to Leave

Four of the four trades had answered by the eighth of the second month, and the answers were on the morning post in a bundle that came to the vat flat in a woman's apron and was put on the flat of the trestle, and Nell Kest read all four out in the lane on the ninth at about the fifth hour, in the open, with about sixty people there, because the number is a wage and a number nobody has heard of is a number that gets made true by whoever is standing in the room when it is read out.

The first three were ordinary and one of them was not.

The ordinary ones said the same thing in four different hands, which is that a house whose assessor has said no does not stay in the lane, and the price of going is a coach fare and a removal and a bench bond, and the numbers are the same four numbers in all four letters because there are only about four numbers that anybody in this trade has: **four pounds ten each way, or eleven if you go on the day they go, two days lost at four shillings a day, and a bond of eleven pounds, and four years before you can come back without a second one.**

The fourth letter was from a trade she has never been in and could not name to anybody, and it is the one she read twice, and it began like this:

> *We will answer anybody who writes to us, and we will answer in writing, and we will put our answer on our own paper with our trade at the top of it, and we will charge two shillings for an answer to a stranger and nothing at all for an answer to a house we have dealt with in the last four years. The two shillings is not for the ink. It is so that nobody owes us anything.*

Nell Kest read that last line out three times and then put the sheet down on the table and asked the lane a question.

"Who here has ever been given an answer for nothing that they were not already owed."

Nobody put a hand up, and about nine people said *yes* at once and could not agree on what they meant, and she let it go, because she has learned that a question at a trestle in a lane costs a face and she put that on a board herself in the ninth month in chalk with her own name under it.

---

Here is the rule she has worked out in eleven months, and she has never written it down anywhere but in a book of her own, and it is on the back of a copy of a levy finding:

> *Ask a person with power a question and get a clear answer, and do not build a case out of it. The answer is a thing about that person, on that day, and it is worth exactly what it costs them to say it, and if it costs them nothing then it is worth nothing, and if it is expensive then it is worth a great deal and it will be true. Do not put a man's answer in a book and hold it up in a room as though a man had said it to the room.*

And on the ninth of the second month, at the same trestle, about a quarter of an hour after she had read four letters out in a lane, a letter came that put the rule to the test, and it did it by being too good.

It was from a man. He was the standing assessor of a third trade and she had never met him and had written to him once, in the fourth month of last year, from a coach fare she paid herself. He was fifty-one and he had been an assessor for nineteen years and he had four houses in his association that owed money and a bench he could not fill.

He had not answered her fifth question. He had answered a question she had not asked, and he had answered it before the post went.

> *Miss Kest. I am told you are the one who puts the four questions on a stone in a lane and I am told it is at no charge, which I think is a mistake and I will tell you why, and then I will tell you what I have come to say, and I will say it now so that you do not have to write to me for it.*
>
> *First, the reason I say four questions at no charge is a mistake. A question asked for nothing is answered for nothing by the person with nothing to lose, and the person with nothing to lose is a person who has not thought about it. A person who pays two shillings has thought about it and has decided it is worth two shillings, and the answer that comes back off that is worth the two shillings, and I would rather you were expensive.*
>
> *Second, and I am not asking you for anything and I am not asking you to write to me again: if any house in your lane is pressed out of a trade, our association will take that house. Bench, standing and wage, at our own cost, without a bond, and we will find the money for the coach. Four houses in the last two years and we have taken three and we have not had trouble with any of them.*
>
> *Third, that offer is not for the person who asks me. It is for whoever writes to me about a house of theirs. A person who is out does not write to an assessor. A person who is in a lane and is frightened writes to an assessor, and the assessor is the only door there is, and if the door opens to the person who is frightened then it is a door and not a trade.*
>
> *I have not asked you for anything and I do not expect to be thanked, and I will not be writing again, and if your assessor says no to a house you are welcome to pass that on. — M.T., standing assessor.*

She read it at the trestle twice, and then she took it off the flat of the table and did not read it to the lane, and that was a decision and it took her about a second and a half and she wrote it down that night as a decision and not as an instinct.

Because here is what it is, and she is the only person alive who has worked this out, and she worked it out standing at a vat with a letter in her hand in about four seconds:

He said yes before she asked. She got a clear answer from a person with power, and the answer is generous, and it is in writing, and it is on his own paper with his trade's name at the top, and it is about a man who wants four houses filled and a bench filled, and **a concession made for a reason is not a fact. It is a door.** And the door has him behind it.

The last trade's letter said the price of a stranger's answer is two shillings and that the two shillings is *so that nobody owes us anything*. This man has offered to pay for four coach fares he does not have to pay, and in exchange, in about two years, four households in a lane will owe a man in another town for a bench.

And a bench is a thing a person can stand in front of and say the holder of the bench is the bench. B. Breg wrote that in the sixth month about a fourth trade, and she sent it to Emberwatch, and it came back to her from a furnace town as a thing that was true about somebody else's trade. It is true about this one too, and it is true about a free offer as well as about a paid one, and the price is not the thing. **The price is a thing that lets a person answer without owing anybody, and a door with no charge on it is still a door.**

So she did not read it out in the lane, and she wrote on the back of it, in her own hand, with the day on it, four lines that went to the copyist on the Greyfen road at four pence a sheet, because she pays for every copy she sends and always has:

> *I have been given a clear answer by a man with power and I am not going to build a case out of it, and I want it written down that I wanted to, at about the sixth hour, in a lane, in front of sixty people, because it is the best thing anybody has offered me in a year and it would have gone into the fifth question's answers like a lamp into a bucket.*
>
> *The rule is not *do not trust a man who says yes*. The rule is that an answer I did not ask for is not an answer to me. It is an answer to whoever writes next, and there is a bench behind it.*
>
> *A price is what lets a person answer without owing anybody. A man with no charge on his answer is the same door with a nicer step.*

---

The number is the other half of the morning and it is the reason she put the four houses' children in the room in the eleventh month, and it is on the flat of the trestle in chalk, in her own hand, with no name against it, and there are about a hundred and forty people in that lane and the chalk was there for about six weeks.

> *The Caudles' share of the levy is two pounds five and a quarter a quarter. Roke read out in the sixth month: eight pounds and a penny a quarter on four houses, four times a year, for a year, is thirty-two pounds and fourpence out of a lane. The next four houses are the ones who will be out, and there are four of them, and I am not going to write their names on a stone, and nobody has to stand here and be one of four.*
>
> *The woman at my vat gave me the fifth answer and gave no name: four pounds ten each way, or eleven if you go on the day they go. Two days lost at four shillings. A bond of eleven pounds, and a house has to find eleven pounds or get a man to sign. Four years before you can come back without a second bond. One house went to Thornfen about eleven months ago and she is on a bench and she is not sorry and she does not know anybody here, and everybody knew all of that on the day she went and she went anyway.*
>
> *So here is the two numbers and I want them both on the same stone.*
>
> *To ask four people with power one question and be told the truth by one of them: **two shillings.** That is the price in a letter from a trade I have never been in, and it is not for the ink, it is so that nobody owes anybody anything.*
>
> *To be in a lane with a clause in your own trade's book about a girl who leaves: **two pounds five and a quarter a quarter, four times a year, and thirty-two pounds and fourpence out of the lane in a year**, and the next four are the next four, and there is no clause in my lane's book that would do that to anybody if anybody had been asked.*
>
> ***Twenty times.** Being told the truth costs a twentieth of what not being told costs, and a twentieth of two shillings is two pence, and two pence is what a woman in a lane can find. That is the whole of the fifth question and I have been four hundred and forty miles and nine days and six months finding it out and it is two numbers on a stone.*
>
> *The four of us signed a clause about a girl leaving and we are paying two pounds five and a quarter a quarter for the privilege of having signed it, and the four of us are not the four who will be out, and the reason the four of us are not is that we signed.*

She read that out twice at the trestle, with the four houses' children at the back again, because the number is a wage, and there are nine of them between nine and nineteen and one of them is seventeen and works the same bench as her mother and said in the eleventh month that it was going to be her in a year and she has not changed her mind and she has not been asked again since.

And then the last thing happened, and it was not a thing she had put in the book, and it is the only part of the middle of the second month that she will still be telling anybody about in four years.

---

He came on the twelfth of the second month, off the midweek packet, on two days' leave he took out of a room with a barred door, and he did not say why he had come and she did not ask him, and he stood in a vat flat in a dyers' lane four hundred and forty miles from anything he had to be in, with his coat still on for about ten minutes, and looked at the chalk on the flat of the trestle, and read all of it standing up, which is a thing he does with paper.

Then he put his hands in his pockets and said, "Say it."

"You know what it is."

"I know what you wrote in a letter on the twenty-eighth of the ninth month. You said you had not forgiven the ninth month and you were not going to and you would say it to my face and it would take about four hours." He did not move. "I have come four hundred and thirty miles for it and I would like you to know that I have had a list of things I was going to say on the way and I have left all of them on the road, and if you ask me one question about any of them I am going to answer it badly, so do not ask me anything."

So she said it.

It took about four minutes. It was not the four hours and it was not a speech and it was not about the ninth month of last year in the way anybody who has not been in the room would expect. It was about the four hours she had spent in a copying room in Auremar, four hundred and thirty miles down a river, on the tenth of the ninth month, and about a sentence she had read on a wall, and about the fact that she had worked out in about four seconds that the record of that afternoon spoke first and answered for a person, and that she had said it out loud in a room with two other people in it, and that he had not been one of the two other people, and that he had been sent for.

That is what it was about, and he did not know that until she told him, and when she told him he sat down on the flat of a vat bench for about four seconds and then got up again.

They were both wrong about the four hours. Neither of them can remember what the other one said. That is the true shape of it and Nell Kest wrote it down that night in nine words in her own book with the day on it, and the nine words are: *it took four minutes and I cannot remember one sentence of it and I am not going to pretend to him that I can.*

What she has said out loud, in words, to her brother, on the twelfth of the second month, is this, and it is the whole of what eleven months of a cloth in a drawer was:

"You were right not to tell me. That is the part I have not forgiven and it is the part I have had to say, and I am not taking it back now that I have said it, and I am not going to be one of the people who forgives a thing because it has been said out loud. I am not going to be a person who forgives a thing because it has been said out loud. What I want is that you had to make a decision about me in a room, on your own, with nobody to stop you, and that you made one, and that it was not a small one."

And Marek Kest said, "That is the worst thing anybody has said to me."

"No it isn't."

"It is the second worst," he said, "and the first one is that you are right about it and I have no answer."

Nell Kest went back to the vats at about the fourth hour. There was a pot on the third flat and there were two women from the far end of the vats who wanted to hear the whole of it and were told no, and one of them said *you have got a brother at the Roll Hall* and she said *I have got a brother who works in a room nobody can get at, and he came for four hours, and it took four minutes, and that is the whole of what I have got and I have got it in nine words in a book.*

The chalk stayed on the flat of the trestle for about six weeks and nobody took it down, and it was washed off in a month of rain at the end of the third, and by then the fourth month had come and gone and a public timber bridge in another district had been the subject of an application and there was a plate in a file in a district court and a number cut into the edge of a plate was about to turn into a supervisor's hand in a column that was never designed to hold one.
