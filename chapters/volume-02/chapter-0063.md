# Chapter 63: Two Thirds of a Page

It came down on the morning packet of the twenty-ninth of the first month, and it was two thirds of a page, and it was addressed to the Docket of Powers and copied to the person who had been in the room when the question was framed, and it arrived at the copying alcove of the third gallery because the Docket of Powers had no way of finding the person it was copied to and did the only sensible thing, which was to send it to the gallery she worked in.

Tamsin Rook read it standing up, which is how she reads everything, and then she read it again, and then she went and stood in the passage behind the gallery for a minute and a half without doing anything, which is also a thing she does.

> *In the court sitting in room four, on the question referred by the Docket of Powers with a condition upon its shape. Received the twenty-ninth of the first month.*
>
> *First. This court will not say that the record is altered. It will not say that it is not altered.*
>
> *It has before it a finding of the Docket of Powers, made in the third week of the eleventh month, that two readings performed by the applicant were unauthorised scale readings within the practice of that office. It has before it a question as to the effect of that finding upon a record of a court in another province. It does not have the record. It cannot get the record, because the record is the record of a provincial court and this court may direct its production and may not take it, and the provision by which a person may be examined is the provision by which a person is named, and in the matter before this court no person is named. It will not answer a question about the effect of a finding upon a document it has not seen, and it will not answer one in the alternative, and it does not say which answer it would give if it could.*
>
> *Second. It records, with a name at the foot, that a Docket of Powers keeps a register of persons and not of records; that the finding before it is a finding about a person; and that this court cannot answer a question about a record without a person to examine, and that there is no person before it whom it may examine.*
>
> *Third. The question as first referred, which was whether a reading so performed was of any legal effect whatever, is not answered by this answer, is not withdrawn, and stands referred.*
>
> *Fourth. As to the sitting on the instruction sealed with the device, escalated by this court on the seventeenth of the sixth month on its own motion over the applicant's objection: this court will not give a date. It states the reason in a form rather than in words, and the form is this, that a date is not to be fixed for a matter in which no party can be named, and that when a date is fixed for that matter it will be fixed with a name at the foot of it.*
>
> *— S. Vint, presiding, room four.*

She read it four times and then she wrote down what it was, in her own book, in her own hand, because she has been getting better at that for eleven months and the thing she has got better at is putting what a document says before what she wants it to say.

> *It is the worst of the two answers that were available and it is not a refusal and it is not nothing and I want that on the page before anything else.*
>
> *The two available answers were yes and no. Yes: the record is altered, because the reading that went into it was unauthorised, and everything anybody has done since the twelfth of the ninth month of last year is standing on a record made by a man with no right to make it. That is what I have wanted for eleven months. No: the record is not altered, because the finding is about the reader and not about the record, and a record of a court is not altered by the fact that a person who wrote a minute in it was not entered in a book three rooms away.*
>
> *Either of those two would have been an answer. Either of those two would have been a sentence a person could carry into a room in the fourth month. What I have been given is that the court will not say either, and a sentence about a register of persons, and a statement that it cannot answer a question about a record without a person to examine.*
>
> *Which is a door. That is the thing I have to write down and it is the reason I cannot use it. A door says: name a person and this court will answer. I cannot name a person. I have no authority over anything, I cannot compel a soul, and the person who would be examined is a person I have never met and am not permitted to ask anything.*

Then she wrote the other three lines of it, and the fourth one is the one that mattered most, and she wrote it in a hand that got smaller.

> *And it does not help Lowcross.*
>
> *Lowcross is about a person. It is about a woman of sixty-one in a yard in the lower district who is in a file in the wrong column and who signed a box she read at arm's length. The application in the fourth month is going to be made by a solicitor of about fifty-five who says on the record that he is not good at it, and it is going to be about whether a parish that was dissolved in the sixth year can leave a duty on a household. A ruling that a high court will not say whether a record is altered is about a record, and there is nobody in the crossing at Lowcross who is a record. If I take this down to that household as the good news, I have handed a woman a sentence with nothing in it, and she will be asked to sign something in the fourth month on the strength of it.*
>
> *A ruling that is morally satisfying and materially useless is a failed ruling. I have written that sentence about a bridge in a district court and I am now writing it about my own work and I am not going to be able to take it back off the wall at Mosswake and neither is anybody else.*

---

She copies it out twice. In a copyist's hand, in plain words, with the four points as four points and not as a paragraph, because a paragraph is how an answer gets turned into something else in nine years.

The first copy went to Silas Sowle, who is the household's solicitor at the crossing at Lowcross and nobody else's, at his address in the lower district, with four words on the front and no office on it and no name on it that the court had not already put there.

*Read all of it. — T. Rook, no office.*

The second went into a file in the Docket of Powers, because the Docket referred the question and the Docket has a file with a man's name at the top of it, and the answer to a question that office asked belongs in the file of the office that asked it and not in an alcove. The Docket is three rooms off the corridor behind the third gallery and the same post goes to it, and it went there, and Marek Kest read it standing up on the thirtieth and gave her one reason against it and she agreed with the reason and it went anyway.

He said: "It is two thirds of a page and it is going to be the first document in this matter that anybody in nine years will say was the turning point, and there is not one line in it that is about a person anybody can help."

And Tamsin Rook said, "I know, and I sent it, and I wrote the reason down before I did it, which is that a solicitor who is told nothing will be told something by a person with a tray in about a year, and a solicitor who is handed two thirds of a page on the evening of the twenty-ninth has to work out for himself what to do with it, and there are three weeks to the fourth month."

A third thing went down the river in the same packet, behind the other two, because a packet out of a copying alcove in Auremar goes to a river court four hundred and thirty miles away and it costs four pence a sheet less from this desk than from any shop window in Greyfen, and she is the only person in this building for whom that is true.

It was not a copy of anything. It was a transcription of the Cinder Court's first order of the seventeenth of the sixth month in a copyist's hand, and it went to a wall at a clerk's window, and on the front of it were five words and no office and no name:

*for the wall. it is the first. — T. R.*


Then the packet had one more thing on it, and it was not a copy of anything, and it was a printed notice from a district works, and it was about a floor.

> *Notice. Old records room, Mosswake Roll Court. A heating flue is to be cut on the fourteenth of the second month, in the corner, and the third flagstone is to be taken up in the corner, and the press standing upon it is to be cleared. The clerk of the court is to remove any papers required to be kept from the press and the corner before the fourteenth.*

Tamsin Rook read it twice and then went and got out the sheet she keeps for things she is not going to be able to do, which is a sheet of foolscap in a book, and she wrote on it:

> *The fourteenth of the second month. That is the press. The press is on the third flagstone and the fourth one is the drain and the floor is cold and the window does not open, and there are three bundles on the oak shelf in the order of the quarters, and the fourth cycle of the sixth year was never copied by a clerk of sixty-one who had a child and eleven weeks of docket and no replacement, and there is still no fourth clerk, and there has not been a fourth clerk in four years, and a district that has not had a fourth clerk in four years is not going to be given one by a notice.*

And then, underneath, in the same hand, on the same sheet, she did the thing that turned the whole month over.

She wrote to Ivet Sarn, and the letter went down the river on the second of the second month, and it was short, and the covering line said five things and each of them was a condition.

> *Clerk Sarn. — The notice of the fourteenth came on the twenty-ninth. I know what is on the shelf and I know which flagstone it is on and I know there is still no fourth clerk.*
>
> *I am offering to write the fourth cycle. Eighteen letters, the same form I have used before, and I can do it in about four days in your records room, which is a cold floor and a window that does not open, and I will pay my own carriage and I will not be paid, and I will not put my name on them in any office, and they will be the district's and not mine.*
>
> *That is the whole of it and I want you to know that I have had to write down that the word is **write** and not *copy*, and that I did not notice that until I had written this letter, and that if it is the wrong word then the offer is the wrong offer and you may put the end of it there.*
>
> *I am not an officer of your court and nothing in this offer makes me one. You may refuse it and I will not write again. I am asking because the flue is on the fourteenth and because the fourth cycle is the only one of the four that has no returns and no press-marks and no names in it at all, and when the press comes off that floor the eighteen households in it stop existing for ever, and they will not stop existing in a bundle, they will stop existing the way the fourth cycle was never made.*
>
> *T. Rook, the second of the second month, no office.*

And then she sat down in the alcove with the four-line form in front of her and the pen in her hand and did the thing she had been putting off since the eleventh of the ninth, which was to open the book of her own and turn to a blank page and write a heading at the top of it, in the copyist's hand, with a date.

> *Things I have been wrong about, and the day I found out.*
>
> *One. The third of the fourth month was about a province. I wanted three provinces and a pattern and I got a district that has never had one, and the reason I asked the question was that I wanted the province to be a province. I found out on the eleventh of the ninth, four months after I filed it, and I did not send it to Marek, on purpose, and I wrote down that I had done it on purpose, and the reason I wrote it down was not the reason I did it. — The eleventh of the ninth month. — Added the nineteenth of the first month, and I am leaving the date of the finding alone: a clerk of the Inheritance Desk has made me account for the second half of this in a room, and the only reason he was able to is that he had a book with a time in it and my initials against it, and I had a drawer, and a drawer is not a record of anything except that a person put something in it and shut it.*
>
> *Two. I believed that a condition on the shape of a question would make a court say what the question is about. Oswin Marrow put that condition on his own finding and countersigned it and I read it as a promise that the answer would come back with a word in it that I could carry into a room. It came back with a statement that the court cannot answer a question about a record without a person to examine, and there is no person. — The twenty-ninth of the first month.*
>
> *Both of them are the same mistake and it is not a mistake about documents. It is that I wanted a thing I could use, and both times I had been told a thing I could not use, and both times the thing I could not use was the true one. I have been wrong four times in twelve months by the book clerk's own count and I have been wrong about a *thing to carry* twice, and the second one was four months after the first, and that is not a coincidence either, and I am putting it in the book so that it is a thing I have written down.*

She looked at the heading for a while.

It was not enjoyable. She had a whole afternoon of copying in front of her and forty-one pages of a high court's record that she held under a four-line form with **None** in the second box, and she had two hands, and one of them had been steady for eleven months and the other one had started, somewhere between the eleventh of the ninth and the twenty-ninth of the first, to want things.

She did not put the list away. That was the whole of the discipline and it took about four seconds, and she wrote one line under the two entries, and the line is the reason she is a person anybody can be frightened for in four years.

> *Three. I am not going to fill this column. I am not going to write a third thing down today to make it look like a habit, and I am not going to take the two out. A list with two entries in it and a heading is worth more in a year than a list with nine entries in it and no heading, and I have watched a woman four hundred and thirty miles down a river put a sentence about herself on a wall with the day on it and it is the only inheritance she has ever been left, and I am not going to be the person who teaches her a different habit.*

And then she put the pen in the stand, and squared the two copies and the original, and went back to the forty-one pages at about the fourth hour, because the forty-one pages do not care what a high court has said and they are the only thing in this matter that is entirely hers.

The intake clerk of the Chancery came up the stair at about the fifth hour with a notice in his hand and the look of a man who has been handed a sentence and does not yet know whether it is one.

"Is this matter going to the district court on the second of the second month."

"It is not this matter. It is a man called Dray and the local forgery counts, and this matter is in the same building."

"I am the officer at the door." He put the notice down on the counter and squared it, which nobody had taught him and which she noticed. "The order says every sheet accounted for at the door by an officer, and I asked who the officer is, and the registrar said the officer is whichever officer of this court is on the roster, and I am on the roster, and I have never been on a roster in my life and I did not know that a clerk of the intake desk was on one."

"Do you want to be on it."

"I do not know what being on it is." He looked at the notice. "It says accounted for. It does not say what accounted for is. Does it mean that I stand there and count, or does it mean that I stand there and know where they went, or does it mean that if a sheet goes missing somebody comes and asks me first."

"It means all three and it means the third one most, and the third one is the reason it is written that way rather than *the papers are to be produced*."

"Write that down for me in something I can take into that room with me," said the intake clerk of the Chancery, "because I have got four words on a piece of foolscap and I have never been allowed to ask anybody anything and I have been in this building two years."

Tamsin Rook wrote it out in a copyist's hand and he read it back twice before he took it, and then he put it inside his coat, and at the door he said:

"The woman in the gallery said *you have been standing at that door for two hours*. How does she know that."

"Because somebody has been in that room with a tally of the sheets since the first hour and there is one column in it and the column is his," said Tamsin Rook, "and a person who is keeping a column has a face, and faces are what people read in a room, and it is going to be his own fault and he is twenty-five and he has not worked that out yet."

"Then I will work it out for him," said the intake clerk, "and I will tell him at the door, in a room, where there are people."

At about the fifth hour she opened the drawer, because she had been copying for two days and her hand wanted the other thing to do, and there were four envelopes in it and three of them were empty and one of them was not.

The fourth one was addressed to a standing assessor of a furnace town, and it was written in the fourth week of the first month of last year, and it was about Marek Kest, and it was the one still going to matter in nine years, and she put her hand flat on it for about a second and a half and then she shut the drawer, and the drawer is in a room she does not live in, and there is no office in the room, and the drawer is the reason.
