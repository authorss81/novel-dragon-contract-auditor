# Chapter 74: What a Document Is For

Nevin Trost's room is the third of three rooms off the corridor behind the third gallery and it is about the size of a cart. It has four desks; he uses one. The other three belong to a man who was made an examiner in the second month and has not been in since, and whose desk has a plant on it that is not dead and is not alive, and which the whole corridor has agreed not to discuss.

Marek Kest went up at about the tenth hour of the twenty-seventh of the third month and stood in the doorway for a moment before he came in, which he did not need to do and which he did because of what he was going to ask.

The stove was in. Trost had had a cold since the second month. There was a sheet of foolscap on the desk with nine lines of a copying exercise on it that was not a document and was not a file, and it was the only thing on that desk that was not in one.

"Mr Trost."

"Mr Kest." Trost did not put the pen down, and then he did put the pen down, which was the correct thing and took him a second longer than it should have. "Sit down if you are going to. There is one chair and it is not a good one and it has been here since before I came."

"I am going to ask you one question and then I am going to ask you for something, and I am telling you the order so that you do not have to wait for the second one."

"All right."

"What is the leaf for."

Nevin Trost looked at the door, and then at the middle distance about nine inches in front of his own face, which is what a man does when he is working out whether he is going to answer.

"I have been asked that question once," he said, "by a woman in a copying alcove who wanted to put it in a book, and she asked it in about four seconds and then she said a thing that I have not stopped thinking about, and I am not going to tell you the answer and I am not going to tell you that I do not know, because I do not know, and the two of those are different and you are the second person in two days to want the one that is easier."

"You do know that I know you were asked."

“I know you know, because she is the only person in this building who has copied a thing out of my hand and written her name on the back of it saying she holds it and no office. If you have been in this matter for fourteen months you have been in a room with that woman.”

"I have."

“Then you know that a man who gives a thing away and says *I am not going to be thanked* has made a decision, and the decision was not to keep it. Asking me what it is for is asking me to make a second decision nine days later about something I have not understood since the eighteenth of the third month.”

Marek Kest stood in the doorway of a cart-sized room and let that be true for about four seconds, which was more than he had allowed himself anywhere else in thirteen months.

"Fair," he said. "Second thing. I want the leaf."

"No."

"I have not asked properly yet."

“You have not asked properly and you were not going to. I am going to save you the reasons, because you can work them out and so can I and they are the same reasons.” Trost turned the pen over once. “There is no form in the empire that lets a clerk of the Received Room take a document out of a file of the Examinations office. I have looked. I did not look hard; I looked in about four minutes, and what I found was that the question I was actually asking was not how to get it out of the file. It was what I would have to put in the file if I did get it out of it. There is a line in the day-book of this office for that line, and I have never once in six years had to use it for anything but a refusal, and I have thought about writing it four times since the eighteenth of the third month and have not written it, and that is the answer.”

"You are the examiner in this matter."

“I am the examiner in two other matters and neither of them is the matter in which a retired scale notary put a folded leaf on this table and told me to decide for myself what it was for. I have no file for that. If I had one I could answer you. I do not have one, because he said do not put it in one, and I have gone along with him, and the price is that I am sitting here refusing a man I cannot give a reason to, because the reason is a leaf in a drawer with no index number on it, and if I tell you the drawer then the drawer is a place a person can be sent to.”

---

Then Marek Kest took a sheet of foolscap out of his coat and put it on the desk, squared, and stepped back from it.

It was four lines and two boxes. He had never seen it filled in. He had seen it filled in.

*One. The documents transferred, listed.*
*Two. **The office of the receiving person, if any.***
*Three. The receiving person signs here.*
*Four. The date.*

And in the second box, in a copyist's hand, in ink: **None**.

"Mr Trost," he said. "Sign this."

Trost did not touch it for a while. He read the first line and then he read the second line, and then he put his finger on the second box and on the word **None**, and he did not touch the word and he did not touch the ink.

"That is the copyist's hand of the third gallery," he said.

"It is."

"And the second box is blank and it is not blank."

"It says None."

“It says None, which is a thing about a person, and the thing a person is, is that they cannot be instructed.” He read the first line again. “One. The documents transferred, listed. So this form is for a person handing over custody of documents, and if that person has an office, the office is entered, and the office may then be written to and asked what it did with the documents.”

"Yes."

"And if the person has no office, the second box says None, and then there is nothing anybody can be written to, and the documents are outside every office in the empire, and the reason the copyist wanted that was that an office holding the public copies in a matter is an office that can be managed."

"Yes. And it works. It is the best thing anybody has produced in this matter and I have built about half of my own position on it."

“It works,” said Nevin Trost, “for the thing it is for. It does not work for this.” He put his hand flat on the desk, not on the sheet, which is a thing a man does when he is about to explain a form and does not want to touch it while he does it. “This form is a custody form. It protects a holder. It says: I am holding this, here is where, here is when, and there is no office over me, so there is nobody you can ask about me. That is what None does. None is a door with nothing behind it, and the person behind it cannot be instructed.”

"And the leaf."

“The leaf is not custody of anything. I am not holding a copy of the Cinder Court's record for a matter. I am holding a piece of paper a man gave me in a room and told me to put where it can be found and not to ask him what it was for.” He picked the sheet up and held it out at arm's length, which is a thing he does with documents. “The moment I put the Examinations office in that second box, the leaf becomes an exhibit in a matter, and an exhibit may be asked what it is. And the answer to *what is it* is: a copy of a schedule, made about forty years ago, by a copyist whose name is on it, which the man who gave it to me will not say in this building, and which is in a building where a person with a tray can get to it in a day. And as soon as that is on a form with an office at the top of it, the document belongs to the office, and the office will be asked, and the office will answer, and the answer will be that the office does not know what the document is for, because the man who does not know is me, and I am in this room.”

He held the form out and Marek Kest did not take it.

"So it protects a holder and not a document," he said.

“It protects a holder,” said Nevin Trost. “Every protection I have ever read about in this office protects a holder. The Docket protects a register of persons; the Chancery a locked cabinet with the key on a chain; a district itself by saying it is powerless. Every one is a wall with a man in a room behind it, and every one is a wall against a person with a tray. Not one of them has ever been a wall against an office.”

He put the form down.

“And I would like it written down that in six years in this office I have not seen that before. A woman wrote that form to protect herself, not a sheet of paper. It has worked, and it has worked so well that it has put her whole method out of reach at the exact moment the method needed to be in reach. Neither of us did that on purpose. It has happened anyway, and that is the third time in six years that something has happened in this office that nobody arranged.”

---

Then he went to the drawer.

It is the second drawer of a table in a room with four desks. It is not locked. It belongs to the man who was made an examiner in the second month and has not been in since, which is how a drawer can be somebody else's and still be in use.

He took out a file with nothing written on the outside of it. Not the name of a person, not a number, nothing. And on the inside of the first leaf, in a hand about forty years old, there is a copyist's name.

Marek Kest looked at the name, looked at it a second time, and then did not say it and did not write it down and put his hands behind his back, which is what a person does who is being careful.

“I am not going to say it either,” said Nevin Trost. “I have looked at it forty times and it is not a name I have in this building in any book. That is the whole of what I know about it.”

"How many sheets."

"It was folded in four when he gave it to me. That is how a thing is folded to go in a coat."

He unfolded it. It is one leaf of good cream stock, about the size of a long envelope, folded in four, and it has been folded in four for a very long time, because the fold has gone through the paper in two places and worn a channel in it about the width of a thumbnail. The channel is at the bottom of the last quarter. Which means the last quarter has been folded and refolded and carried about in a coat for forty years, and what was written on the bottom of it is not there any more.

"Read it," said Nevin Trost. "You may not take it, copy it, or write down what is on it, and I am saying that as a man and not as an examiner, because I have no file and there is nobody to say it to."

Marek Kest read it standing up. It took him less than a minute.

It is a schedule of a term. The heading at the top is:

> *Schedule of the term. The days named. The term held for seven years and no longer.*

And there is a rule at the foot of it, which is the rule a copyist writes on a copy, in the ordinary form of words:

> *This is a copy. Where it differs from the book, the book governs.*

And the copyist's name is at the foot of the last line that is left. And there are two lines after the rule. There is only one line after the rule.

“There were two when it was given to me,” said Nevin Trost, “and I did not know it, because I did not unfold it. He gave it to me folded and said hold it. I have had it in a drawer for nine days and I unfolded it about an hour ago because you were going to ask me for it and I wanted to know what I was refusing.” He put his thumb on the channel. “That is a fold. Not a tear. Not a burn. Forty years of a man carrying a thing about in a coat and taking it out about four times a year, and the fold has gone through the bottom of the last quarter, and the bottom of the last quarter is where a copyist writes what the copy does not carry, and the line is gone, because paper does not remember ink.”

He looked up.

"Mr Kest. Do you understand what that means about the man who gave it to me?"

“Yes,” said Marek Kest. “It means he has handed a room a copy that is short by a line, and he knows it is short, and he did not tell me, and he will not tell me what it is for because he cannot, and the reason he cannot is that a careful man does not hand a room a short copy and call it a copy. He has been telling me the truth by handing me a thing about forty years ago, and the only person in this building who could have worked that out from a piece of paper is a clerk of nine years with a certificate in a file short by a leaf, and he worked it out in about four seconds, and I have been the person in this building for fourteen months, and he came into a room nine days before I was.”

"I do not know what he is," said Nevin Trost.

"You said you were not going to tell me."

“I said I was not going to say what it is for. That is not the same as saying I do not know what he is, and I am not going to confirm you on it either, and I would like both of those written down, because a man who is careful in a room gets it wrong about his own carefulness about nine times a year, and one of the nine is always this one.”

---

Marek Kest put his hand on the table and stopped, because he had been about to do the thing he does.

He was about to write it down.

He has a book, and he has written in it every thing anybody has said to him in a room in fourteen months, and there is a copy of it in a book with no office on it in a chest a person has not described, and it is the only reason he can sleep at night.

And he stopped, because of a thing that took him about four seconds and is the whole of this chapter.

A person had said a thing in a room. Two of them, a week apart, and both were true. In the fourth month of the first month he had undertaken, in his own book, to read every sheet that goes out of this matter to a solicitor, a household, or an office outside the Roll Hall, in front of her, before it goes, and to give any reason against one of them in a room and out loud and not on a page.

There was no line in that undertaking about a thing that was going nowhere.

A man had put a folded leaf on a table and said *I want you to decide for yourself what it is for* and *I am not going to be thanked*. If Marek Kest wrote that down and sent a copy down the river, a thing handed over in a room by a man who was not an officer of anything would be in a book nobody can be instructed about — and the *reason* it cannot be instructed is a form with **None** in the second box, and the reason that form exists is that an office holding the public copies in a matter is an office that can be managed, and what would be managed, in about four years, by about four hundred people who are not in this room, is a retired scale notary of about sixty-one with a burn-scarred hand who has told this building one sentence and refused everything else in a form.

The form Tamsin Rook signed on the eleventh of the ninth month protects a person by putting an office out of reach. It does not protect a person by being passed on. He had spent nine days assuming it did one of those. It did neither, and the second of those is the one he needed.

"You have caught me doing the thing," he said.

"I do not know what the thing is."

“I was going to write it down,” said Marek Kest. “In my book, and send a copy to the woman in the alcove, which is what I have done with everything anybody has said to me in a room for fourteen months, and it is the reason I have any case at all. And if I do it with this then in four years there is a record of a man who came into this room unasked and said one sentence and refused four things, and the record will be in a chest, and a chest can be sent to.”

"Take it," said Nevin Trost.

"What?"

“Write it,” said Nevin Trost. “In both books. And I would like it to say, in about nine years, which of the two you sent it to, and what the answer was.”

---

Here is what happened in the last four minutes of that room, and it is in Trost's hand in the day's book of the Examinations office on the twenty-seventh of the third month, and it is four lines long, and it is the only thing in that book for fourteen months that is not a refusal.

> *The applicant asked for the document held in the drawer under the dead man's desk. I have declined, and I have entered the reason, in which I am obliged to name the document and its holder, and I have put my own hand at the foot of it, and the reason is the whole of the matter and I will not be pressed on it.*
>
> *The applicant then asked for one line. I have copied one out, in my own hand, with the date, and given it to him: a schedule of the term, the days named, the term held for seven years and no longer. That is the heading. There is nothing beneath it I can give him, because the copy is short by a line and the line cannot be recovered, and he is aware of that, and I am aware that he is aware of it, and we have both said so in this room with the door shut.*
>
> *The line is not to be entered in any register, minute, list or file. It is not to be sent to any person. It is to be held by him. — N.T., the twenty-seventh of the third month, at about the eleventh hour.*

Marek Kest read that back, which he does, and then he asked one question, which is the only question he asked in the room, and he had it ready before he came up the stairs.

"Mr Trost. You said holding that leaf is not custody of anything. If it is not custody, and you are keeping it in a drawer, and there is no form, and there is no file, and no office — what is the drawer?"

“It is the only thing in this office that is not a record and not custody and not a file,” said Nevin Trost, “and I have never had one before. I am going to keep it. I am not going to be thanked. If you come back in nine years and ask me what I decided it was for I will tell you what I decided, and that is the whole of the arrangement, and neither of us will ever be able to show anybody a thing about it.”

He said it without any expression at all, and then he picked up the pen, and the nine lines of foolscap with the copying exercise on it were not a document and were not a file, and he went back to them.

---

At about the seventh hour that evening Marek Kest sat in the Received Room with a sheet of paper in his own hand, and there was no office at the top of it, and there was a heading in the middle of it, and the heading is a schedule of a term and there is nothing under the heading at all, and he wrote four paragraphs and one of them is the sentence of the batch and it is the sentence he is going to have to be able to say in a room in nine years.

> *Twenty-seventh of the third month. The copy is short by a line. That is all the mystery in it. A fold has gone through the last line of a copy made forty years ago, and the last line of a copy is the line a copyist writes about what the copy does not carry, and it is gone, and paper does not remember ink. Everything else in this matter that has looked like a wall for thirteen months has turned out to be a lock. This one is a crease.*
>
> *And here is what I have got, in the plainest words I can find, because it is the whole of what I have learned in nine days and it is going to cost somebody something later.*
>
> *The form with None in the second box protects the person who holds the paper. It is a door with nothing behind it, and the nothing is a person who cannot be instructed, and I have built about half of what I have on it. It is the only instrument anybody in this book has produced that has been any use against a document that cannot be compelled.*
>
> *It does one other thing and nobody has found out until this morning. It protects the holding. It does not protect the loss. The moment somebody asks about the paper instead of the holder, the door is behind them and they are in a room, and the second box is no use at all against a question about the paper.*
>
> *Which means a copyist can protect herself absolutely and cannot protect a document by a single line; and that the man who handed that leaf over knew it; and that he is the only person alive who knows it, and will not say, and nobody can make him, and the reason nobody can make him is a form I watched a woman sign nine days before he walked into a room and said one sentence in it.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the twenty-seventh of the third month, at about the seventh hour. There is one line on the paper above this and it goes nowhere and it is in my own hand, and there are two of them in the empire, and I am one of them.*
