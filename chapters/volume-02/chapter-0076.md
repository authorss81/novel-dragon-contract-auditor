# Chapter 76: What the Figure Is a Number Of

The papers from the morning were on her desk at about the sixth hour in two piles, and the clerk's room was empty because the clerk was in the other pile's pile of work, and Joane Tranter has been a magistrate of that court for nine years and has never once sent anybody to fetch a thing she could go and get herself.

She had read the works' return four times since the twelfth month. It is a printed sheet with forty-one returns on it and the fortieth and something is the crossing, and the figure against it, in a hand of about fifty years, is three hundred and eighty-eight.

She had never once asked what it was a number of.

She had a fairly good reason for that and the reason is not flattering. She is a court and not an ally, and she is aware of it, and she has said in a room that a ruling that is morally satisfying and materially useless is a failed ruling, and that the one that costs more is the one that is true. She has entered things in her own minute on her own motion and asked for by nobody, and she has done that three times in nine years, and she knows exactly what a line in a minute is worth, which is that in nine years somebody reads it and forms a view.

A number in a return that nobody can explain is a worse thing than a number in a return that is wrong, because a wrong number gets corrected and an unexplainable one just sits there being true.

She put on her coat.

---

The crossing at Lowcross is a timber cart bridge over the Weir Cut with a bar's end on this side, and at the head of it there is a lean-to about nine feet by six made of tarred board, and in the lean-to there is a stove, a stool, a shelf, a kettle, and a card in a tin frame, and in the fourth month the stove is in at about the sixth hour because the man in it is sixty-four and has been cold in this district for fifty-one years.

His name is Emrys Rudd and he is not in any record of that district, or of the works, or of the court, and he is paid a penny a day, and the penny does not depend on anybody crossing.

"You will be the court," he said.

"I am the magistrate of the district court of the lower district. I am not the court; there is a court and I sit in it four days in ten."

"Yes," said Emrys Rudd. "You will be the one who sent the boy."

"The clerk of the intake desk came on his own time and I did not send him."

"I know he did not. I want to say that before anything else, because he said he was not anybody and he is, and I have been thinking about that for about two weeks and I have decided he is a clerk of something."

He got up off the stool. It took him a while, and he did not pretend it did not.

"Three hundred and eighty-eight," said Joane Tranter.

"That is what I write."

"I have read it four times since the twelfth month. I have never asked. I have wanted to say that out loud before I asked, so that it is in the room, and it is, and I have said it."

Emrys Rudd looked at the card in its tin frame for a while, the way a man looks at a thing he has been looking at every day for nineteen years and has never had a reason to look at it as a question.

"It is a number of times the bar went up and down in four weeks," he said. "That is the whole of it. It is not a number of carts and it is not a number of people and it is not a number of houses. It is a number of a bar."

"That is four lines in a return and I have never known it."

"Nobody ever asked."

"Nobody ever asked," said the magistrate, "and I want to be exact about who that is, because I am the one who is standing here and it is not the works and it is not the district. Nobody in this district has asked you in nineteen years."

"That is correct and I am not blaming a district," said Emrys Rudd. "A district has about nine things to ask about in a year and this is not one of them, and I have never once gone to a court or an office or a works to ask for anything and there is nothing here to complain of. I get a penny a day and the penny is for sitting."

"Twelve threes," said Joane Tranter. "Three hundred and sixty-six days a year, less a market day and the days the bridge is shut, is about three hundred and sixty. Three hundred and sixty pence is three pounds a year. Nineteen years of that is fifty-seven pounds and something, and in nineteen years the crossing has been repaired twice at about nineteen pounds each, which is about thirty-eight pounds, and it has lost about a pound a year in the years in between, which is about seventeen pounds more, and the whole of that is about fifty-five pounds, and I have been in this district nine years and I have known about two of those repairs and neither of them was in front of me."

"You have not lost any money," said Emrys Rudd. "I want to say that before the other thing, because I know what a figure like that sounds like when a person says it. Nobody has lost anything. The money was never anybody's. It was in a fund and the fund is a pot and the pot is the same pot every quarter and it fills and it empties and in the years in between it empties into nothing much, about a pound a year, and nobody ever noticed because nobody ever had to."

"Yes," said Joane Tranter. "I know that gap. I have it in a file and it is the reason an application of mine was refused this morning, correctly."

"I heard the works man in there. He said the fund holds six pounds and eightpence for the quarter."

"About six pounds on the average of the four quarters. The eightpence is one quarter."

"Then the pot is about six pounds and the cost is about a penny a day and that is about three pounds a year," said Emrys Rudd, "and the pot holds two years of the crossing and the crossing is the only thing in it that has ever needed anything at all, and it is not in it as a line, is it. There is no line."

"There is no line," said the magistrate. "I have looked. The crossings are not in the fund as a heading. There is a heading for the fourth month and a heading for the ninth and a heading for the eleventh and there is nothing for a bridge, and that is because a bridge is not a fund's business, and a fund that had a line for a bridge would have to answer for the bridge."

"That is the whole of the nineteen years," said Emrys Rudd, "and I have said it out loud to a magistrate and it has taken nineteen years and about ten minutes, and I would like it recorded that I have thought about it a good deal and I never had anybody to say it to, and I am not complaining, because the pot fills up and empties and the bridge has never gone down except for the twelve days in the twelfth month, and that was not the pot. That was nine people."

---

She asked him the other thing, and she asked it because she had the two numbers in her head and she had been carrying them for three months and had never put them in the same room.

"The works return says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings in four weeks. I have a schedule in a file that says four hundred and eleven people live on the far side of that water."

"Yes."

"Are those the same thing."

"They are not the same thing and there is no way to make them the same thing, and if you were to try you would end up with a number that is about half of nothing." Emrys Rudd had not moved from the stove the whole time. "That is a number of people. That is what it says on the paper. And this is a number of times a bar went up and down. There is four of us on a cart with a load on and there is a woman going over on a plank with a sack three times a morning who would be three in your number and is one in mine, and there is a man who crosses twice going and once coming back and I never could tell you which of those three is the crossing that counts, and I do not count them, I count the bar."

"So a headcount of people could be produced from your figure, and it would be wrong, and it would be wrong in a way nobody could see."

"You could produce something and it would not be a headcount, it would be a guess with a division in it," said Emrys Rudd. "And it would be a very good guess. That is the trouble. A guess with a division in it that comes out near the right number is the most dangerous kind of thing there is, because it is right enough that nobody checks it, and then in nine years there is a figure in a fund's book with a line under it and a decimal in it and nobody alive remembers that it came out of a tin frame in a lean-to on a bar's end."

---

She stood in the lean-to for about a further nine minutes and asked him four more things, and three of them were things she could have found out from a return, and the fourth was this.

"Mr Rudd. If I were to write in a minute that the figure in the works' return is a number of the bar and not a number of people, and that you have kept the tally for nineteen years at a penny a day, would that be a thing that could be used against you."

"No."

"Why not."

"Because it is true," said Emrys Rudd, "and because nobody has ever needed to use a thing about me, and because a man who has been paid a penny a day to count a free bridge for nineteen years and has been asked nothing is not a person anybody has a use for yet, and if you write it down it will be the first thing anybody has written about me, and it will be in a file in this city, and in nine years there will be a line in that file with a man in it, and he will not be able to get at the lean-to any more than he can get at a document in a drawer, and I have watched four people in this city try to get at a document in a drawer and I have been told about all four of them and I have learned that the ones who go looking get a person and not a paper."

"You have been paying attention."

"I have had a lot of it," said Emrys Rudd, "and I have counted a bar, and there is a great deal of difference between a person who is paid to count and a person who is not."

---

She entered it in the minute at about the ninth hour, and it is four lines, and it is the third thing in nine years she has entered on her own motion and asked for by nobody, and it is on the second side, under the refusal, and it does not appear in the operative part and cannot be read by anybody but somebody who opens the file.

> *Entered the third of the fourth month, on the magistrate's own motion, asked for by nobody.*
>
> *The figure of three hundred and eighty-eight in the return of the district works for the four weeks is a count of the times the bar at the crossing at Lowcross was raised and lowered in that period. It is not a count of people and it does not correspond to any count of people, and it is not to be used as one. The tally is kept by one man, aged sixty-four, at a penny a day, paid whether any person crosses or not, and it has been so kept for nineteen years.*
>
> *The court enters this as a question asked and answered and not as a finding. It makes no order upon it and it affects nothing in this matter and nothing in any other matter, and it is entered because a figure in a return which nobody can explain is a figure which will be copied by somebody who assumes it means what a figure usually means, and the assumption will be wrong, and in four years nobody will be able to find the moment at which it became wrong.*

And then she wrote a fourth line under it, on her own account, and this one is the reason the other three exist.

> *I have read this figure four times since the twelfth month and I did not ask, and I sat in this court in that month and said out loud that this district has never once been asked by an officer of that works to stand the cost of a crossing, and I said it as though the not being asked were the whole of the trouble. It is not the whole of the trouble. The crossing has been kept for nineteen years at a penny a day by a person, and the person is real, and the district has not asked him either, and the fact that his figure is in a return and my figure is in a return and neither of us asked the other is the same fact twice.*
>
> *A court that finds that a number is a number of something has found nothing and has made a rule out of a man in a lean-to, and I am not going to do it. I am going to enter that the question was asked, and that it was answered, and that I am the magistrate who did not ask it for four months, in the same minute, under the same line, with the day on it.*
>
> *— J. Tranter, the third of the fourth month, at about the ninth hour.*

She read all of it back, because they all do, and then she put it in the file and shut the file, and then she sat for a while with her hands on the desk and did not do anything else, which is a thing a magistrate of nine years does perhaps twice a year.

At about the tenth hour a clerk came in and asked her whether the works had sent in the certificate, and she said that the works had not and would not and could not, and that the question had been asked of the works and answered, and that the clerk should not draft a letter about it.

Then she said: "Find out whether the works' return for the four weeks is the one the count came off, or whether somebody in that office copies the number out of a card in a hut. It will be the card. I want it to be the card and I want the card to have the day on it in somebody's hand, and I want that to happen before anybody in this court writes to the works about it again, which will be never."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And find out how long that hut has been standing at the head of that crossing, and put the answer in the file with the day on it, and do not put his name in the file."

That is the whole of what happened at the district court of Auremar's lower district on the third of the fourth month, and it is four lines in a minute and two instructions to a clerk, and neither of them is a remedy, and the crossing is open, and nobody is liable for it, and a man of sixty-four is in a lean-to about nine feet by six counting a bar at a penny a day and has been asked one question in nineteen years and it is now four lines in a file in a city four miles away with no name on them.
