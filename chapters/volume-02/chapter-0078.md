# Chapter 78: A Date and an Initial

It came out of a sum she did on the back of a form, which is how the two best instruments in this book have come about and neither of them was designed.

She was in the copying alcove on the twenty-second of the fourth month with a dead hour in front of her and a thing she could not get round. The thing she could not get round was this:

The Cinder Court's first order of the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year says, in words anybody can read, that no guarantor duty attaches to a person who has not been heard and has not signed. It is the best sentence anybody has produced in fourteen months. It is a shilling a copy and four hundred and forty people have read it and about two hundred and ninety of them kept it.

And in the four hundred and thirty days between the seventeenth of the sixth month and the twenty-second of the fourth month of this year, Tamsin Rook could not name one household that was spared anything by it.

Not because it is wrong. Because of the thing she and Marek Kest wrote down on a sheet on the eighteenth of the second month, with a name on it and the day on it and the word *accountable* under the name, in her hand, in a copyist's hand, and the word was his and she insisted on it.

*The person was heard. The record answered first.*

---

She did the sum on the back of a form.

It came to about four lines and it came to this. In the matter that made her write it down, a woman was in a room, in a river court, with a lawyer's husband on a wall and forty people in it, and she had been warned — properly, by a man in a good coat, and the warning was true — and on the morning of the twelfth of the ninth month a third issue of a witness order went up on a board with a certificate pressed into the first line, and the widow was defeated, and everybody in the room agreed afterwards that she had been heard.

The hearing was not the thing that failed. The *order of the day* was the thing that failed. Somebody had put a document in front of her before anybody asked her anything, and the document said the thing she was afraid of, and a person who is afraid of a document cannot get in front of it, and the room they built for her was in the wrong place in the day.

So: a person has to be in the room *before* the document exists.

That is all. It took her four lines on the back of a form and it is not a new idea and it is fourteen months old: it is the trestle table in the Venn lane, where a paper may be brought and read out loud with no advice given. It is a girl of eleven at a bridgewarden's table, reading a works note aloud and stopping on the fourth line before anybody asked her to. It is nine days in a furnace town advising nobody. It is the third hour at a clerk's window so as not to be seen in a queue.

The whole of this book's method is: **be there first, and say nothing, and do not wait to be asked.**

The problem had never been that they did not do it. The problem was that all of it depended on a person being in a room, and a person in a room has to have got there, and getting there costs four pence and a day's wage and a reason to tell somebody where you are going, and a person who cannot read cannot be handed a form.

---

She went down the corridor at about the fourth hour on the twenty-second and stood at the end of the intake counter for about nine minutes and did not say anything to anybody.

The intake clerk of the Chancery is twenty-five and has been at that desk long enough to have written a file number in ink and initialled it, and to have written the time a thing came into the gallery in the margin in ink and initialled that, for about two years, for everything that comes into that gallery and is not addressed to a desk. Time, and the initials of whoever took it. That is the whole of the column. There is no name in it. There is no heading at the top of it, and there has never been one, and he wrote the sheet himself in the first week of a job nobody explained to him.

Nobody had ever asked him what the figures in it were a number of. She had not. It had come up twice this year — once in a corridor on the eleventh of the first month and once in a finding about a woman called Kest that a clerk of the Inheritance Desk made her account for — and both times it had been about a line, and never about the column.

She looked at it upside down from about four feet away for nine minutes, because that is how a copyist reads a thing, and then she went back up.

Marek Kest was in the Received Room with the stove going and a sheet of foolscap in his hand on which he had written nothing for about forty minutes.

"I want to put a thing on the table," she said, "and I want to say first that it is not an instrument and it is not a rule and it is not going into a court, and I want to say it second that I have been working on it since the eighth of the third month and I have not shown it to you, and I am showing it to you now because it is in your room and because you are the only person in this building who will tell me the true thing about it inside a minute instead of over a fortnight."

"Go on."

"There is a clerk at the end of this corridor with a book that has been kept for two years. It has one column. The column has a time in it and an initial beside it and nothing else and no heading at the top. It is the cheapest piece of evidence in the empire and it has never been given a name and it has never been asked for, and it is the only thing in this building that can prove a person was here before a thing arrived."

He put the pen down.

"That is a fact about a clerk," he said, "and not a finding."

"It is a fact about a clerk and it is the finding of this month," said Tamsin Rook, "and I will show you why in a minute and then you can tell me I have made a rule out of a man, which is what the magistrate said to herself on the third of the fourth month, and she was right, and I have thought about it for two days."

---

Here is what she put on the table. It is a sheet of foolscap, ruled by hand, on both sides, and it has no number, no series, no seal, and nothing at the head of it at all, and the only two things on the face of it are two boxes and a rule, and the rule is three lines long.

> *Box one, ruled: **what the person was told, in the person's own words.***
>
> *Box two, ruled: **the office of the receiving person, if any.***
>
> *And under the two boxes, in the same hand that ruled them, three lines:*
>
> *You are not asked anything and nothing is asked of you.*
> *You are not to be given advice.*
> *Write the day and your initial on the other side and hand this back.*
>
> *And on the other side, ruled once, at the top, in the same hand: **the day. Your initial.***

And a column, in a separate book, at a separate counter, with a heading, and the heading is one word.

> *Given.*

Two words. She had argued about the heading for two days and she had got as far as *Handed in*, and *Asked at*, and *Enquiry*, and *Received*, and she had written all four out on the back of the same form and then crossed them out, and the reason is on the page in her book and it is this:

*Received is what the clerk does. Given is what the person does. And the only thing this is evidence of is that a person gave something, and if I put the clerk's word at the top of it then a clerk's word is what a person will be taken to have done, and we are back where we started.*

---

"Four lines," said Marek Kest. "And no office at the head of it."

"No office at the head of it, no number, and no series."

"Why no series."

"Because a form with a run on it is a model waiting for a district," said Tamsin Rook, "and that is not a thing I learned in this room. That is a sentence written on a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river in the first month of last year by a woman who has kept a flood docket running for nine years, and what she said is that a form with a run is a rule waiting for a district, and she was right, and I have not stopped being frightened of it since. The four-box form at the Mosswake window has no number and no series and no seal and no office at the head of it for that reason, and it is the best thing anybody in this book has ever printed, and it is printed by hand about forty times a year by one woman."

"And the second box."

"The second box is the box that has been in this matter since the eleventh of the ninth month," said Tamsin Rook, "and it says *the office of the receiving person, if any*, and when there is none the person receiving it writes **None**."

She looked at him.

"Do you know what `None` does on a form?"

"It puts an office out of reach," said Marek Kest. "It is a person who cannot be instructed. It is the best instrument anybody in this matter has produced and I have built about half of what I have on it."

"It does that," she said. "And I have had three weeks to think about what else it does and here is the rest of it, and this is the part I brought it in here for.

"**`None` in the second box is the reason a person who cannot read can fill this in.**

"Not because it helps them read. Because it is the second box, and the second box is a box about a person, and the clerk at a counter writes it, and a clerk at a counter writes it in about four seconds without asking anything, and a person standing at a counter who is looking at a box with their own words already written in it does not have to sign anything and does not have to give a name and does not have to be able to read the rule. **A person who cannot read can put four words in a box and be handed a piece of paper back with a day on it.** And that is the whole of what this instrument is for, and it took me three weeks and a form with two boxes to find out that the thing I built in the ninth month to protect *me* is the thing that makes this form usable by *her*."

---

"Then the cost," said Marek Kest. "Four pence a sheet."

"Four pence a sheet," said Tamsin Rook, "and I have costed it, standing up, and here is the arithmetic and you can be angry with it. It is a sheet of foolscap, which is about a halfpenny, and it takes about a minute and a half to write out with a pen because the boxes have to be ruled by hand, because a ruled box cannot be bought in this city in any shop I have been to in a year.

"The price is the second thing and it is the whole thing. Four pence is the price on the glass in a shop on the Greyfen road, and it is a price that lets a woman with four children and no candle walk sixty miles and come to a counter and not owe anybody anything, and it is the price at which this book has been able to work in four provinces.

"And a penny is what a girl of seventeen gets a sheet for at a chandler's counter in Candlewright Lane, and I have thought about that for two days, and a penny is a *trade* price, and a trade price is a price that a person who is not in a trade cannot use, because they do not know the counter exists and cannot ask for it, and a penny is also about two days of a washerwoman's earnings and five days of a bad week for a woman with a child. Four pence it is. I have written *four pence* nowhere, because the price is not on the sheet and it must not be on the sheet, and it is in this book in my hand, and the reason it is in my hand is that in nine years somebody is going to want to know why the rule that everybody is reading was priced and I am not going to be able to remember."

---

"And the limits," said Marek Kest. "Say the limits, and say them in a room and out loud and not on a page, and if I find one you have not said I am allowed to find it."

She said them.

"It does not stop a document. Nothing in the world stops a document."

"It does not put a household back in a field, and it does not open a door, and it does not lift a bar, and it is not evidence in any proceeding, because it is evidence of a time and not of a thing, and a court is not interested in times."

"It cannot be used at Lowcross. It is not adopted anywhere. It has not been sent to the Docket of Powers, or to Examinations, or to a district court, or to a wall, or to a solicitor, or to anybody, and it is not going to be, and the reason it is not is on the page and it is the undertaking of the fourth of the first month: a sheet about this matter in an office's hand is a document with an office at the top of it. I have not retired it. I have used it as a reason not to send something, which is what it is for."

"You are not sending it to the woman in the alley at Candlewright."

"I am not sending it to anybody, and the girl of seventeen is seventeen and has a page of a list of four pages in a hand that is hers and knows a copy is a weapon and a target, and if a form about this matter turns up in that building in her hand in nine years then somebody has worked out that she is the one to ask."

"And the fourth," he said, "and it is the one that matters and you have been not saying it since you came in."

"It is beaten by the case it was made for."

"Yes."

"The widow in the river court had four months' warning and was in a court with forty people in it and a lawyer's husband on a wall, and a young man in a good coat came to her door beforehand and told her the truth, and it did not help, because being told the truth is the warning." Tamsin Rook put her hand flat on the sheet, which she does. "If a person is warned about a document, they are afraid of the document, and an afraid person goes to the hearing and sits down. This instrument is no use to that woman. It would only have been any use to her in the four months *before* the third issue of that order went up on the board, and in those four months there is no reason in the world for her to walk into four hundred buildings and hand a piece of paper to every clerk at every counter, and I have not been able to write one word on this sheet that makes her do it, and I have not been able to write one word that makes anybody do it."

Marek Kest did not say anything for about a minute and a half.

"Then it is not a weapon," he said at last.

"It is a date and an initial."

"It is a date and an initial, and it is smaller than a hearing and cheaper than a hearing and harder to put in front of a person, and a document cannot be warned about a date, because a date has no threat in it." He picked the sheet up and turned it over and looked at the rule for the other side. *The day. Your initial.* "And the thing that beats it is the thing that beats everything we have. If the warning comes first, the person is already afraid, and the sheet is in a bag somewhere, and the hearing is in a month, and the document is in the room. It does not fix that. Nothing fixes that. It fixes the *next* thing, and the next thing is the one where nobody warned anybody, and there are going to be a great many of those, because a warning is expensive and takes a man in a good coat and a day of walking."

"Yes," said Tamsin Rook.

"So write that on the sheet."

"No. Nothing goes on the sheet. The sheet is a box and a rule and a day and an initial and if there is anything else on it then it is a form with something to say, and a form with something to say is a form somebody can agree with or refuse, and this one cannot be agreed with or refused because nobody is asked anything." She squared it. "It goes in my book, with the day on it, and the day on it is the day a person gives it, not the day I made it, and the limits go in the book and not on the paper, and if you do not agree with me about that then say so now and I will put it on the paper."

"I do not agree and I am not going to stop you," said Marek Kest. "I want it written that I do not agree, and I want it in your book and not on the sheet, and I do not know what a form with nothing on it is and I have spent fourteen months producing forms with something on them."

---

They made the first one at about the sixth hour on the twenty-sixth of the fourth month, in the Received Room, and it took about forty minutes, and it is in her hand and it is in a book of her own with no office on it, and neither of them filled it in.

That is the finding, and it is on the page, and it is the last thing in the chapter.

"I am not going to fill it in," said Tamsin Rook.

"No."

"You are not going to fill it in."

"No," said Marek Kest, "and I want to say the reason out loud, because I have found out in three weeks that a reason I do not say out loud is a thing I have decided on my own, which is the whole of what I have been for fourteen months.

"I am in the Docket of Powers' register. I am in this court's minute book, three times. I am the person named at the foot of a grant of a limited audit. I am the person who asked for an itemising in a building of four hundred people. There is a return at a door in this city with my name at the foot of it and a man of twenty-five is proud of it. **If I gave this sheet to a counter, and somebody kept the day and my initial, then in nine years there is a line in a book with a day on it and my initial on it, and the person who gives it to a counter is *me*, and it is a document about me that I made about myself, and it is the best thing in this book and it would be a trap with my own name cut into it.** And you are in a register in two buildings and you are on a list of forty-one names in four hands and you are the person who entered two unauthorised readings into a public register on her own initiative and wrote down that she had not asked.

"So no. Not us. Two people cannot carry the first one because the two of us are the two people in this matter that a document is about."

"That is not a finding, that is a fright," said Tamsin Rook.

"It is a fright that is correct," said Marek Kest, "and I have been living on frights that are correct for fourteen months and so have you, and I would like it written down that on the twenty-sixth of the fourth month the two people who built the best instrument anybody in this matter has produced worked out that they are the last two people in the empire who may use it, and that this is not modesty, it is arithmetic, and I would like it in your book with the day on it, and then I would like to go and stand at the end of a corridor and watch a man of twenty-five not write a heading on a book for about a quarter of an hour, because that is what this is, and he has been doing it for two years, and nobody asked him to, and I am not going to ask him now either. Somebody has to leave one thing in this book without asking."

She wrote it down, and read it back twice, and did not put his name on it, because he had not asked her to.

Then she put the sheet on the top of the folder with the forty-one pages in it, on the end of her own bench, in the alcove, where it is.
