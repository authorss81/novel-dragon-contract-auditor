# Chapter 56: What a House Does, and What It Costs in Pounds

The finding came back from the guild's own examiner on the fourth of the eleventh month, three and a quarter pages, with the four houses named at the head of it, and the four houses were the four that had signed, and the Caudles were first because they had signed first on the twenty-ninth of the tenth month of last year, and two pounds five and a farthing a quarter, and the farthing was Nery Caudle's own money and had gone in the same coin out of a cloth.

Nell Kest read all three and a quarter pages out loud at the trestle on the eleventh of the eleventh, at about the fifth hour, and she did it in the lane in the open, standing on the flat of the table with her own hand flat on it, and about a hundred and forty people were there because it is a dyers' lane and a thing read out on a table gets into a lane the way water does.

She read the names of the four houses. She read the Caudles' two pounds five and a farthing. She read the farthing again.

And then, because she had promised herself in the lane in the sixth month that she would stand there while it was read and would not soften the other half, she read Roke's half.

> *Eight pounds and a penny a quarter on four houses, four times a year, for a year, is thirty-two pounds and fourpence out of a lane. Every one of the four of us can do that. The other houses in this trade will find out about it in about eleven months. And then the next four houses are going to be the ones who are out, and it is never going to be the four of us again.*

"It is the fifth of the eleventh month," said Nell Kest, when she had read it, from her own writing on her own board from the ninth of the sixth, and about forty people in that lane said something at once and then stopped, because they had all worked it out. "Roke said in about eleven months and it has been five. I am not going to stand here and say it is early. It is five months and it is the same quarter and there are four houses in this trade that have not signed and two of them are the Pry and the Nine Elms and I have read their books from this table and neither of them has four pounds a quarter of slack in it, and the Nine Elms has a boy on a term."

She had put the four houses' children in the room for that. Not out of sentiment. Because the number is a wage, and the wage is in a kitchen, and she had worked out in the fourth month of last year while copying a finding for a district court that a number nobody has heard of is a number that gets made true by whoever is standing in the room when it is read out.

They stood at the back. There were nine of them between the ages of nine and nineteen and every one of them belongs to one of the four, and one of the nine is a girl of seventeen who works the same bench as her mother and who is in the middle of deciding whether to take the master's offer at Roke's, and who said afterwards, at the trestle, with nobody near her:

"That is going to be me. That is going to be me in a year, and I have got about a fortnight to have a different opinion about leaving."

Nell Kest wrote that down on the back of a copy and sent it to the professional copyist on the Greyfen road for four pence and told her it was worth nothing.

---

The fifth question is what a house does when the assessor says no, and Bettin Rahl had written it under his own thirty-one-year-old rule at the second leaf of his trade's book in a furnace town four hundred and forty miles off, with his own answer beside it, which is that there is none.

There is one in Greyfen, and it took Nell Kest about nine days to see it, and it is not a door.

When a house in a trade that keeps one door and one assessor runs into a thing it cannot get out of through that door, the house does not stay in the lane. It goes to another district. It goes to another association, in another town, where there is a bench and a wage and a hall, and where nobody has ever heard of the lane's history or the levy or the finding, and it starts again at the bottom of a list that is written up in a good hand in a room where a person has never been told to be frightened of a form.

The price of that is a coach fare, a removal, a tool, a bench or a bond, and a trade standing that took years.

"Tell me the number," said Nell Kest, at the vat, on the ninth of the eleventh, to a woman of thirty-four at the far end who has worked the same bench for nine years and has been in that lane since she was eleven.

"Four pounds each way," said the woman. "That is the coach, and it is four pounds ten if you go on the day they go and eleven if you go on your own, and there is two days lost either way at four shillings a day, and there is a bond for a bench, and the bond is eleven pounds and a house has to find eleven pounds or get a man to sign, and I have signed for four other women in this lane and I have not got eleven pounds and neither has any woman in this lane."

"Do you know of a house that has gone?"

"One. About eleven months ago. She went to Thornfen and she is on a bench and she is not sorry and she does not know anybody here." The woman stopped working for a second. "And she cannot come back for about four years without a second bond, and everybody knew that when she went, and she went anyway, and I am not saying anything about her."

"That is the fifth question answered."

"That is the fifth question answered and I did not know it was a question until a woman off the vats asked me the other four." She went back to the bench. "You are going to write it down."

"I am going to write it down with your name on it or without it."

"Without it," said the woman, "and you are going to write the number, and the number is the whole of it, and I want it written that I gave you the number and did not give you my name, in case anybody comes and asks me whether I have said the fourth one."

---

The eleventh door in that lane is Ottoline Bray, who is fifty-two, and who in the ninth of the sixth month had read all four questions back unprompted and then said she had a paper of her own about her boy and had nobody to ask it of and had been carrying it since the second month, and had given Nell Kest her name on the understanding that she would be told to ask her own boy first and to say *the lane* if he asked who told her.

A mill bench, and about that. A bench engagement in a mill four miles off, in a house that is not in the trade, on a good paper with the boy's own name on it at the top of it, spelled right, in the boy's own hand, where a name goes.

Nell Kest asked her the second question about it at the vat flat on the twenty-second of the eleventh, and Ottoline Bray had it out of a cloth before Nell had finished the sentence, because she has been carrying it since the second month and she carries everything.

> *Is there work in it.*
>
> *Yes. There is work in it. He is on the top bench and he is the fastest on the top bench and he is seventeen and it is the only thing he is good at and he wants it.*
>
> *Whose name is on it — yours, or a word that walks about.*
>
> *His. His own. He wrote it himself at the top and I have watched him do it and it is his spelling and not mine and I cannot read it properly but I know his e from his name on a bill.*

"And the third one."

"I asked him. In the kitchen, in the eighth month, with the cloth on the table like you do." Ottoline Bray did not look up. "He said: if I stop, nobody answers for it, it just stops. And then he said the thing I have not been able to put anywhere, and he said it in the kitchen and he did not know what it was, and it was this. He said, *then it is mine while I am on it, and it is nobody's after, and that is the whole of what a mill is*."

"And the fourth one."

"He read it to me."

"He read it to you."

"He is seventeen and he read it to me and he said it meant he had to give a week's notice and that his week's notice was four days because the mill counts from the second, and he read it to me out loud at the table and he said *there is nothing in this about after*." She folded the cloth over it. "He is seventeen and he read it to me and he did not know that I have been carrying this since the second month because I did not want to be the house that is always asking."

Nell Kest sat down on a bench she had not been invited to sit on.

"That is the only paper in five months that the four questions have made better," she said. "Do you know that? Do you know that in a lane in Greyfen, and in a furnace town four hundred and forty miles off, and in a shop in Reed Cut, there are about two hundred people holding a piece of paper and the four questions have made every one of them frightened, and there is one paper in a cloth on a vat flat where a woman of seventeen read a thing to his mother and the four questions have made it all right?"

"I do not know that."

"You do now." She took out the copy of the finding and turned it round on the flat of the table. "And I am going to say the other half out loud because I said in the sixth month that I would. It is the other half, Ottoline, and it is this. I have had a paper in a cloth in a drawer since the second month and I did not take it to anybody, and I wrote four letters to a trade four hundred and forty miles off and kept one of them in a drawer for seven months because I was frightened of a letter, and I said that out loud to a man in a corridor in a room with a stove in it in the sixth month, and he did not tell me what to do about it, and I did it anyway about three weeks later."

"That is the fourth question answered about you," said Ottoline Bray.

"That is the fourth question answered about me and I am going to write it in my own book with today's date on it, and the answer is: I am paid, and I was not paid for the answer, and the person who read the paper to you was your son, who is not paid, and there is not one of us in this lane who is paid for the answer."

---

The professional copyist on the Greyfen road had nine names on a waiting list inside her window on the twenty-fourth of the eleventh, four of them trades, and the four trades were not the dyers' one, and one of the four was a trade in a third district that she had never heard of and that had come forty miles.

A woman from that trade had come sixty miles to Auremar in the fourth week of the ninth month to ask the four questions of a clerk at a counter, because a woman she had never met had put a number of four things on a stone under a table in a lane and she had wanted to know whether the stone was still there.

The clerk had not been in. The woman had waited two days. She had written the four questions on a sheet of foolscap and left them on the step of the counter with her own name on the top and the name of the trade and the day on it, and had gone home, and the sheet had been found on the fourth day and had been put in a drawer by a clerk who did not know what to do with it and who told the woman, when she came back for it on the sixth day, that it had not been thrown away, which is the only thing an office can say.

There is no name on the page and no trade and no name on the trade, and a fifth question has not been asked of the person who left the sheet, and the sheet is in a drawer in Auremar, and Nell Kest has written the address of the counter on the stone under the trestle in her own hand, which is the whole of the fifth door.

Nell Kest wrote a fifth question of her own that night in her own book, and posted it on the twenty-sixth of the eleventh to Emberwatch, and to three other trades, one of which she has never seen, and it is the first thing she has ever written that is not a question anybody asked her:

> *And what does it cost, in pounds, to leave, and who has paid it, and what did you have on the day you went that you did not have on the day before.*
>
> *A woman in my lane has given me the first part and would not give me her name and asked for it written down that she gave me the first part and did not give me the name. Four pounds ten each way, or eleven, and two days lost at four shillings a day, and a bond of eleven pounds for a bench, and four years before you can come back without a second bond. That is the answer to the fifth question in this lane and the answer is that you move and it costs money and everybody knew it.*
>
> *If you are in a trade and your assessor has said no, I would like to know what your trade does, and I would like it in pounds, and I would like the name of the trade at the top so that a person can write to it. — N.K.*

And the fourth of the four trades had sent the answer back before the question, which is what the four women of the fourth house past the weigh-house have started doing in a furnace town, and the answer was two lines and a name at the top of it and the name was a trade.

> *We asked ours. It is not a door and it is not a woman. It is a bench, and if the holder stops, the bench is the holder of the paper, and a bench is a thing a person can stand in front of and say the holder of the bench is the bench. Eleven of us have asked and eleven of us have got that, and we have written it down at the second leaf of our own book, under a rule that is thirty-one years old, because there is nowhere else to put it. — B. Breg, for the combination, and I am the fourth of the four and I have put my own name at the top because I am tired.*

---

She wrote to her brother on the twenty-eighth of the eleventh month, at about the ninth hour, off the vats, on the flat of the trestle with the lamp off, and it is four lines and it took her about nine minutes, and she did not put a single line of it into her book first.

> *M —*
>
> *The levy came back against the four houses on the fourth and I read all three and a quarter pages out in the lane on the eleventh with the children at the back, and Roke was right in the sixth month and it is five months not eleven, and the next four houses are going to be the ones who are out, and I have written the number of the children in it because the number is a wage.*
>
> *I asked a man in this lane the fifth question and he could not answer it and a woman at my vat could, and the answer is four pounds ten each way and eleven pounds for a bond and four years, and I have written it down and posted it to four trades and one of them is four hundred and forty miles off and I have never been there in my life.*
>
> *I have read a sheet of yours. It is on the wall at four hundred and thirty miles down a river and it says thirty-one of about sixty and it says the other number at the top in letters four inches high, and somebody in a furnace town has read it out to a room. That is your doing and I am not going to pretend it is not. I have had it since the second of the seventh and I did not write to you about it and I am not going to say why, and I have written that sentence down twice now and I am still not going to say why.*
>
> *You did not write about the ninth month of last year and I have read the four lines about the levy four times and I have not written back for three months and there has been a cloth with eleven shillings in it in my drawer since the second month of this year. I have not forgiven the ninth month. I am not going to. Come and be told about it if you want to be told about it and I will say it to your face and it will take about four hours. — N.*

She posted it on the morning of the twenty-ninth and then went to the vat, and it is four lines, and it is the first letter she has written to him since the twenty-ninth of the first month of last year, and it does not mention the ninth month except to say that it is not mentioned, which is the same thing and worse.

And the twenty-sixth went on a cart to a furnace town four hundred and forty miles off, where a woman of twenty-two has put her own name in a box headed *the person who holds* on her own house's own amendment, because the man who brought it said the box takes whoever is on the bench, and who said that out loud in a room with somebody in it on the fourth day of a nine-day visit, and who has a bad chest and has not been to a doctor about it.
