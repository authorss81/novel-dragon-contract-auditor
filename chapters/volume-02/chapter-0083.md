# Chapter 83: The Respondent That Cannot Be Served

The request came up the great stair of the Crown Kiln on the twenty-sixth of the fifth month, and it is on the form, and the form is the one this court published in the reign of the third of the Ashfall line and read out in full in open court in the sixth month of last year, and it has been used about twice in a decade for a case nobody can now find.

A seat is entered as a respondent. A seat is entered by its device. A seat's device goes in the space at the head of the entry where an office goes. And there is a column for the person served, and the column wants a name.

He read it twice at the desk with the file open, and then he said out loud, to the clerk standing at the end of it, the thing he says before he does anything that has happened before.

"I am going to do this," he said, "and I am telling you I am going to do it before I do it, and I am telling you the reason, and then you will write the reason in the day's book and initial it, and if anything comes of this in nine years you will be able to show me that a person in this room knew and I did not hide it behind doing it quietly. Is that the practice here."

"Yes, sir," said the clerk.

"Then write this. Refused. Cannot. The form is the form and I have no power to decline a form this court published, and a judge who declines a form her own court published has to write down why, and there is no why, and an invented reason in that minute book cannot be told in nine years from a real one. Entered by me, and I will sign it at the foot of the page and not at the end of the file."

It is worth saying here, because it is the whole of the man, that **he is an ordinary official with a heavy file that has been heavy for years**, and that he has never had a document from a provincial court in four hundred applications, and that he is not frightened of anything, and that nothing in this chapter is his finding. **The finding is the file's.** He is the man who reads the file and writes down what is at the bottom of it, and he has been that for about twenty-six years, and there is nothing else about him now and there is not going to be anything else about him.

---

The column for the person served is the whole difficulty and it took four minutes to get to the bottom of it.

A seat's device goes in the space where an office goes. That is proper; a device is a thing a seat puts in the place a person would be, and every court in this empire has a space for a body and a person cannot go in it, and that is not an abuse. It is the design.

But the register of parties has, after the device, a line that reads: *this respondent to be served at* — and a place.

And there is no place.

That is not a difficulty anybody in that building has ever had to solve. The estates of a dissolved parish, a trading house of four words, a water holder's office, a firm of attorneys, a company, a chapter, a guild, a bench: every one of them has an address and every one of them can be served at it and every one of them has a person behind it who can be embarrassed, which is the entire historical purpose of service. A device has no address and no person and no embarrassment behind it and cannot be served at all.

"You would serve this on the mark," said the clerk.

"I cannot serve anything on a mark," said the registrar, "and I am not going to enter an address on this register that is a description of a shape, because in nine years somebody will read this entry and go to a street looking for a mark, and there is no street."

---

The third thing took another four minutes and it is the finding.

There are three documents in this matter with one mark on them, and he had all three on the desk, and he did not put them side by side on a table for a drama; he had them in a file and he took them out one at a time and read the head of each and put it back.

The first is an instrument of nineteen years back, sealed with the fire-and-water device, countersigned by a water holder, under which a timber cart bridge over the Weir Cut is held and its bridge-side duty is held against the present holder.

The second is an instruction sealed with the same device, standing in a matter in the district of Mosswake, of which the court has said, in three separate forms, that it does not know and cannot compel the authority.

The third is the notice of a seat, given on the tenth of the fourth month, in six numbered lines, with the same device at the head of it and a seal and a name at the foot.

The name at the foot of it is **Seryn Oris, First Seat**, and the name has been in this file for a month and a half, and in this chapter and in this room it is treated exactly as the file treats it, which is as a seal with a person written under it and nothing else. She is not in this room. She is not in this building. She is not examinable, because the second line of her own document says that no person of or belonging to that seat is examinable, and she is the First Seat, and the First Seat is the seat. She is not the author of the substituted wording, of the public-necessity sentence, of the altered witness order, or of the sealed instruction, and nothing that has been said in this chapter or in any room about the three marks above is a finding about her, and no court has found anything about her and none is going to. What she has done is what a seat's administrative wing has been doing for a hundred and forty years, and what the notice says in its sixth line, in its own words, is that the First Seat holds the old authority to call a succession convocation, stated without any claim upon the matter and without any threat.

"I am going to say a thing in a room," he said, "and it is not a finding and it is not going in the file, and I am saying it to you and not to the applicant because the applicant is not a party to any of the three and I have refused him four questions in a form in the last year and I am not going to make it five in a corridor.

"The word that walks about was put into a return of a works in the fourth month of the sixth year, and into a box on a form at a gate in the ninth month of this year, and the instrument under which the crossing is held is the instrument it was read out of, and that instrument is sealed with that mark. And this court has said, three times and in three forms, that it does not know who authorised the sealed instruction. And a seat has now come up my stair and entered itself in the matter by that mark, and has asked us to hold the question, and has offered to pay the carriage of anybody this district court thinks should be heard.

"So there are two authorities in that alteration and neither of them is a person. One of them is a standing office of the Chancery that prints a sheet and puts it out on an ordinary list to forty-six addresses and has nobody at the top of it, and I entered in open court in the sixth month of last year that this court knows of no person at the head of any office whose standing authority sets that sheet in type. And the other one is a seat.

"And I want the two of them written down, because in nine years the only two things anybody will be able to say about the alteration is that a Chancery office did it and a seat has now been heard on it, and both of those are offices, and a man who reads that in nine years will conclude that somebody did it, and nobody did it, and the correct conclusion from those two facts is that there is nobody to conclude anything about. That is a worse answer than a man in a good coat and I want it on the record as worse."

---

Then he did the second thing, and the second thing is his, and it is on his own motion, and it is the fourth thing he has entered in that minute in nine years that nobody asked for.

He read out what he was going to read out first, which is the practice of that room and which he has never been thanked for.

> *Entered the twenty-sixth of the fifth month, on the registrar's own motion, asked for by nobody.*
>
> *This court has entered a respondent in the matter of the crossing at Lowcross. The respondent has been entered by its device. No person of or belonging to that respondent is party, cited, or examinable, and no inference of any kind may be drawn from the fact that a person has not been named. The form on which the respondent was entered is the form this court published, and the registrar has no power to decline it, and has said so at the time and not afterwards.*
>
> *The register of parties provides a place at which a respondent is to be served. There is no such place for a device. The order made on the fourteenth of the fourth month holding the matters touching this instrument was not served upon the person named in the eleventh line of the schedule of persons who may be answerable in the matter, was not served upon any other person, and could not have been, because there is no address at which this court can serve it and no person who can receive it.*
>
> *The court enters that it has entered a respondent which it cannot serve, that it has no means of notifying any person that the matters touching this instrument are held, and that it has no power to make that respondent a party, and that the form does not permit it to be made one. The court makes no finding that any person is not liable for anything. The court makes no order.*
>
> *The registrar wishes to record, for the person who reads this in nine years, that the fifth line of that notice — that the seat will pay the carriage of anybody this court thinks should be heard, without asking to know why, and will pay it twice if the person comes twice — is the only line in it with a person in it, and that this court has not asked for the carriage of anybody, and that the person to whom that carriage would be paid is a person and not a device, and is a person who may be written to. — Entered the twenty-sixth of the fifth month.*

He read all of it back to the room. He put it in the file. He shut the file.

And then he did the small ordinary thing, which is the reason the whole of this chapter exists, and which is four lines long.

In the register of parties, in the column headed *this respondent to be served at*, he wrote: **no person. no address. this court cannot serve it.**

---

Marek Kest was on the great stair at about the fourth hour of the afternoon of the first week of the sixth month, and he was not a party to anything and was not permitted to be in the room, and the registrar came out of it and stood on the step above him, which he has not done in a room in about nine years and which was not a thing anybody had asked him to do.

"You have been up a stair in this building for fourteen months," he said, "and I have refused you four questions in a form, and I am going to give you the finding and then I am going to go back to my file, and I am giving it to you because it is not mine and it belongs to nobody and I would like one person in this city to have heard it, and if I put it in the file it becomes a thing the court said and a court that has said it may be asked about it."

"Go on."

"A seat that is not a person cannot be refused," said the registrar. "It cannot be examined. It cannot be cross-examined. It cannot be embarrassed. It cannot be sued, it cannot be fined, it cannot be committed, and it cannot lose. It can be entered in a register and it can hold a bridge for as long as I am prepared to be the only person in this empire who could say no and am not. It does not have to be polite about it and it does not have to be sorry. It has an authority that says a question is for a higher court, and that authority is real, and nobody has ever tested it, and it does not expire.

"That is worse than the man in the good coat. It is worse because the man in the good coat was frightened and frightened men make mistakes, and a seat is not frightened and cannot make one, and it is worse because we could have refused him. He was a person. We could have said no to him, and it would have cost us a hearing and an afternoon, and it would have been a *no*, and a no can be appealed. I have not in twenty-six years entered a thing into a register that I could not serve, and I have done it now, and I have written down underneath it that I cannot serve it, and that is the whole of the resistance this court was able to offer.

"And I am going to say the other half and then I am going to go back to my file, because you have been carrying a sentence for fourteen months and it is the best sentence anybody in this matter has produced and I want to correct one word of it in your ear and nowhere else. A seat that is not a person is also **more honest** than a man in a good coat, and it is more honest in a way that costs us, and the reason is that the form says in its own second line that no inference of any kind may be drawn from the fact that a person has not been named. A man in a good coat puts a name at the top of a thing and the name tells you he is the person it is about. A seat puts a device at the top of a thing and tells you, in writing, in advance, that there is no person behind it and that you are not to draw anything from the absence. That is a more honest document than anything in your file. It told the truth about itself on the first of six lines and it did not pretend, and it cost it nothing to do that, and I would rather be dealing with the honest one that cannot be refused than the dishonest one that can, and I understand perfectly well that that is the worst of it and that if I say it out loud in a room it sounds like a man who has decided to lose well."

He went back in. The door of room four does not close on a stair; there is no door on the stair.

Marek Kest stood on the great stair of the Crown Kiln for about a minute and a half after that, and then went down it, and the thing he is carrying is not a name and not a seal. It is a sentence about what a register is for, and he wrote it down that evening in the Received Room with the stove in and the bar on the inside of the frame, and it is on the page because it is the finding of the third week of the fifth month and it is the reason the next four months go the way they do.

> *The twenty-ninth of the fifth month, at about the seventh hour in the evening, and the sitting is on the eighteenth of the seventh, and the notice for it carries the device and nothing at the foot of it, and this court published the form, and I have now had it explained to me by a man with a file that he says is not his own finding, on a step, standing over me, and I am going to write it down because I asked him for it in my own hearing and he gave it to me and I would rather be wrong about this in writing than have got it right in a corridor.*
>
> *A register is a place for people. Every entry in every register in this empire has a person or a body in it, and where there is a body there is a person behind the body, and the whole of what a register has ever been for is so that somebody can be found about a thing.*
>
> *There is a respondent in one of them now that cannot be found, about a thing, ever, and I have spent fourteen months learning that a name on a document is the one thing that makes a thing findable, and the finding I have been given is that I have spent fourteen months learning it in the one direction that works on people, and that a document with a person's name at the top of it is a document about that person, and that a document with a mark at the top of it is a document about nobody, and that about half the paper in this matter is about nobody and has been for fourteen months and I have been sharpening the wrong end of a sentence.*
>
> *And the other half, which I am putting down because a judge in a corridor has told me it in the right words and I want it where I cannot lose it. A document with nobody's name at the top of it cannot lie about a person, because there is not one. It can only be about an office, a body, a device, a form, or a series, and a form and a series and an office and a device are all things that anybody may inspect and that anybody may show to a room. I have had one sentence about documents for fourteen months and I have never once turned it round, and I am going to write it down now and I do not know what it comes to and I am frightened of how short it is.*
>
> *I do not know what to do with that yet. I have written it down four months into a matter in which I have been wrong by being clever for fourteen months, and I have not been clever for about a minute and a half, and the two sentences I have just written are the first two I have written in fourteen months that I do not know the end of. That is a new thing and I am going to leave it alone for a week and see whether it is still there.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the twenty-ninth of the fifth month, at about the seventh hour.*
