# Chapter 65: Every Sheet Accounted For

The order of the fifteenth of the seventh month of last year says that the defendant is to be given no paper except paper of the court's own, and that every sheet is to be accounted for at the door by an officer.

The young clerk of the intake desk was the officer at the door.

He had worked at the intake desk long enough to have written a file number in ink and initialled it in the ninth month, and he had written the time in the margin in ink and initialled it **seven** times for the same man, and had never once been thanked for it, and had stopped, in about the third month, thinking about the not being thanked, which is a thing a person does when the work is good and there is nobody in it.

He had a sheet of his own. It is ruled in five columns and the first column is a figure and the second is a time and the third is a sheet or a bundle and the fourth is where it came from or where it went to, and the fifth is his initial, and there is no heading at the top of it at all, because he wrote it himself in the first week of a job nobody had explained to him.

The first hour was the easy part. There were nine leaves of a ledger in a bundle tied with string, and the bundle was in a bag, and the bag came from the district house, and the bundle went on a table and the bundle came off a table at the end of the day and went back into the bag, and that is one figure and not nine, and he had to learn that in four minutes on the first morning of the first trial he ever stood a door for, which was a trial about a debt in the second month, and he had made a mistake of about nine sheets and had been told about it by a registrar in one sentence.

The second hour was the count of the exhibits, and the count of the exhibits was the whole of the morning.

Forty-one exhibits and a book. The exhibits go in in the order they are given, and the exhibits come out in the order they are finished with, and those are not the same order, and the order they are finished with is the order the speaker used, and the speaker does not think about the door.

So he wrote the order down, both of them, in two columns, and against the second column he wrote the order as they came, which is what the count is for. He had been told that by a copyist of the third gallery at a counter in the first month of this year, in about eleven words, and he had written it down in a book of his own and had not shown it to anybody.

It is, he has worked out since, the only thing he knows. A count is not for the court and it is not for the prisoner. A count is for the next person, in nine years, who has to say how many there were, when everybody who was in the room has a different number in their head and every one of them is certain.

One of the forty-one exhibits is a certificate, and it is Exhibit 12, and the officer at the door had counted it in at the second hour without knowing what it was, and it came about halfway through the morning.

It is a piece of card about the size of a playing card with an impression on it that has been pressed cold with a screw press, and the impression is three words, and there is a notch cut out of the top left corner of the impression, and the notch is the whole of the crime and it is about a sixteenth of an inch.

The clerk at the table described it into the record, because a mark cannot be read into a record, it can only be described into one, and the description is four lines long and took about ninety seconds.

> *A card of good commercial quality. In the centre, an impression pressed cold and not laid, being a filled square with a bar through it and a notch out of the top left corner. The impression has no rim, no second impression, and no variation in pressure across its width, and the card does not appear to have been damped at any time, and a seal laid and spoken takes about eleven seconds and leaves a rim and a second impression and a pressure that varies with the hand.*
>
> *The card is produced by a certifier named in the schedule and is not produced with any ledger or day-book and is not produced with the plate from which it was cut.*

The officer at the door wrote the description out in full in his column, including the words *pressed cold and not laid*, and he wrote them because a copyist had told him at a counter in the first month that the order a thing arrives in is evidence of something, and because a thing you can only see once should be written down while you are in the room.

Nobody in that room looked at the card. Everybody in that room has thought about it since, and about four hundred people have thought about it since, and there is a plate in a drawer in the Kiln sheds that will be handed in on the ninth of the second month by a man who is not sorry about any of it.

At about the fourth hour the magistrate asked the defendant one question about the card, and it was the only question about the card that anybody asked him in nine months. She asked him, in the flat voice, whether he ever cut the matrix himself, and he said no, and she asked him whether he ever used a plate and he said no, and she asked him how a person who does neither of those things comes by a card with an impression on it and he said *somebody in a house brings it round*, and the clerk wrote it and the officer counted the sheet, and the sentence is nine words long and it is the only sentence in that record that says where anything came from.

The man in the dock had spent about the first two hours looking at the ceiling and had spent the middle of the morning looking at his own hands, and at about the fourth hour he took his hands off the rail and put them flat on the table in front of him, which is a thing a person does when they have decided to stop performing for a room, and the officer at the door was the only person in that chamber who noticed, and he noticed because he had been looking at hands all morning, one pair of them, on the end of a rail, for three hours, because counting is looking.

At about the fourth hour the pleading came.

---

The plea was a **limited plea**, and it was put by the court, and it is a thing that can be done in a district court and is not often done in one, and the reason it was done in this one is that the man in the dock asked for it, in about nine words, in the second hour, and the court granted it and the clerk at the table wrote it down and read it back.

It is a plea that admits the counts and reserves everything else. In words, from the form:

> *The defendant admits the counts of forgery set out in the schedule, being the entries in his own ledger of ninety-one leaves and the thirty-eight lines in three districts against which a mark appears. He admits no other thing. He neither admits nor denies any matter touching any person or office not named in the schedule, and he says nothing about any such person or office, and his appearance to be tried is not an admission touching any person or office not named.*

It is not absolution. It is not a pardon. It is a plea that says: I did that, and I am not talking about anything else, and if anybody tries to make me talk about anything else you have my words on the record that I did not.

The clerk at the table asked him whether he understood it. He said that he did.

The minute of the eighteenth of the ninth month was then read out. It was four lines long and it said that a plea offered in that matter had been refused, and it was read out because the file had to show that the refusal was not made a second time, and the reading of it is the only four minutes of that morning that was not about forgery.

The officer at the door counted the four lines of it as a sheet, because it was a sheet, and it came off the table and went back onto the file.

---

At about the fourth hour and a half the magistrate put a question of her own, and it was not in anybody's script, and the officer at the door wrote it down as though it were in somebody's script because that is what an officer at a door writes down.

"Has any person in this court asked you for the name of a client."

"No."

"Has any person in this court asked you for the name of a house."

"No."

"Then I am going to say a thing in open court, and the clerk is to enter it, and it is entered because I am saying it and not because anybody has asked me to." She looked at the minute book and not at the dock, which is a thing a magistrate does when she is about to enter something she has thought about for four months. "No person in this court has asked this man for the name of a client. This court will not ask him. It will not ask him in this matter and it will not ask him in any other matter before it, and no officer of this court is to ask him, and if any person does, it is to be entered and it is to be put to me. The reason is on the record and the reason is this: a man who cannot be got to name a client can be made to say anything by a person who wants him to, and there are people in this matter who want something from him, and one of them has a tray, and a tray is not a motive but it is a method."

The clerk at the table wrote it down and read it back and the magistrate said *that is right* and it is in the minute, and it is the only line in that minute that nobody had asked for, and the officer at the door wrote it in his column as four lines and did not know why he had bothered.

The man in the dock said: "I have nothing to add to that, and I would like to say that I have been in this building since the ninth month of last year and the only person who has ever said anything to me in a room is a man from a district works who put a bar across a bridge two days early, and that was not in a room."

Nobody laughed, because a court of nine people at the fourth hour of the second of the second month does not laugh at a thing like that, and the magistrate wrote *the defendant makes an observation* and did not enter the observation, which is what a magistrate does with an observation.

---

The mark went in at about the fifth hour, and it went in as a mark.

It is a filled square with a bar through it and a notch cut out of the top left corner. It is against the thirty-eight lines in three districts in the carry column with the word *paid* beside it. It has been in a public minute book since the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year, where a registrar read it out in open court in the words *the mark goes into the record as a mark and not as a name, and putting it in a public minute book will not alter one thing in that building except the number of people who know it is there.*

The clerk at the table read the description aloud, so that it went into the record as a description, and the officer at the door wrote it out in full in his column because a mark that is described in a record is a mark, and a mark that is a name in a record is a name.

And then the finding, and the finding is a single sheet, and it is the shortest sheet he counted all day.

> *Found. On the counts in the schedule, the defendant is guilty. Guilty of forgery, thirty-eight lines in three districts, and of the sale of a third witness mark in ninety-eight instances, of which twenty-two are in the Reed Cut district, six are in the Venn file and one is in the Dunn file. Not guilty of nothing; no other count was before this court and none was tried.*
>
> *The mark is entered as a mark and not as a name. The court does not know what house keeps it and has been told by a higher court in open court that it does not know, and does not repeat that to any person in this room as an excuse, and finds that the effect of the mark is proved and the author of it is not before this court.*

That is the conviction, and it is on the local counts only, and it is exactly the thing the order of the fifteenth of the seventh confined the trial to, and the confinement is the reason the finding is a half a page instead of a book.

---

The sentence is four lines and the officer at the door counted all four.

> *Three years in the district house, from the date, of which the period from the ninth month of last year is credited.*
>
> *The ledger of ninety-one leaves is retained in the custody of this court. A copy of it is to be made and sent to such persons as this court directs.*
>
> *The court has no power to restore the sixty-one seal certificates suspended on the seventeenth of the sixth month by the registrar of the court sitting in room four, and no person in this court has asked it to, and the question of them is not before this court.*
>
> *The defendant is to be examined on the question of the mark and of the practice, and his answer is to be used.*

And then there were two more lines, and the magistrate made the clerk read them twice, and the reason she made the clerk read them twice is in the minute in one line and the line is that the first of the two had not been asked for by anybody.

> *The eleven persons named in the order of the seventeenth of the ninth month of the ninth year are not named in any account of this trial, in this court or in any published account of it, and their names are not to be entered in any copy of this record made for any person.*
>
> *The original of the ledger of ninety-one leaves is directed to be produced to this court. — It is the clerk of the third gallery, not this court, that has certified that the copy is short by one leaf against the figure on the wrapper from which it was made, and the copy is not this court's to make, and the original is.*

That second line was the last sheet of the day. The officer at the door counted it and it went down to the district house in a bag at about the sixth hour with a direction on it, and it would take a week to come back because it was in a strong box in another building and a strong box is opened by two people and neither of them is anybody he has ever met.

---

At about the half hour after the sixth hour he wrote his return, because it is the only thing an officer at a door produces, and he wrote it at the back table of the corridor with the corridor going past him the whole time.

It is two sides. It has a heading at the top of it, and the heading is an office:

> *Return of sheets accounted for at the door. Chamber of the Auremar district court, the second of the second month. Officer: the intake clerk of the Chancery.*

And then, at the foot of the second side, in a line by itself, where a name goes:

> *Taken by the intake clerk of the Chancery, this second day of the second month.*

He has never seen his own name at the top of a piece of paper in a form like that. He has had his name in a margin with a time against it seven times, and his name in a register once, in the ninth month, when he wrote a file number in ink and initialled it and nobody thanked him for that either.

He read the return about four times, and then he read the second side and checked the two columns against each other, and they came out the same, and he was proud of it in a way that he could not have explained to anybody in the building, and that pride is going to be the subject of a conversation in a press room off the Crown Kiln stair about seven weeks later between two people who are not going to be careful with him, and he has no way of knowing that, and it is the only thing in this chapter that anybody is going to lose anything by.

He thought about the name at the foot of it for a while longer than four times, standing in the corridor, and worked out in about the ordinary course of a man thinking, because he is not a slow man and nobody has ever thought so:

A document with a clerk's name at the top of it is a document about that clerk.

He has heard it said about somebody else. A supervisor of the Notaries' Table said it in a referral about a clerk who had a view about a series, and put the clerk's name in the second line instead of the first, and the difference between those two things was the difference between a matter and a campaign. He heard it at a counter in the ninth month of last year and wrote it down because it was a good sentence and he was in a room with a copyist in it.

And now there is a piece of paper, in a drawer at the end of a corridor, with his own name at the foot of it, and it is a true record of what he did at a door, and if in nine years somebody wants to know whether a sheet went into that chamber in the second month of the eleventh year, the answer is written down and it is written down correctly, and the person it is about is him, and that is what a good record is and also what a bad one is, and there is no column on a return for a clerk to write *I would rather this were not here*.

He put the return in the tray. The tray goes to a registrar. The registrar will initial it, because that is what registrars do, and it will be kept, and there will be a copy somewhere, and in about four years there will be a person who has never met him who will read it and form a view about him from about two sides of foolscap, and the view will be reasonable, because the paper is reasonable, and he will not be able to do one thing about it.

He decided, standing there, that he would rather have the return than not have it. That was the whole decision and it took about nine seconds and it is the reason a copy of that return exists.

At about the seventh hour a woman of forty-eight came along the corridor from the gallery and stopped, and she was not a court person and she was not a lawyer.

"You have been standing at that door for two hours," she said.

"Yes."

"Since the second hour."

"Yes."

"All right," said the woman of forty-eight, who was on a copy list four hundred miles from a river and was not on any board, and went home.

That was the entire conversation. He thought about it for about a week afterwards, and what he took from it was not that a person had been kind to him, and it was this: that a woman who had walked into a public gallery on the strength of a printed notice had been able to tell, at a glance, from about eleven feet away, that he had been on his feet since the second hour, and that this is a thing faces do, and that he could not stop it, and that from that morning onward he was a person in a building who was known for something, and it was not a thing he had done, and it was the first thing in twenty-five years that had happened to him which he had not asked for and could not have arranged and would have to go on being true about.
