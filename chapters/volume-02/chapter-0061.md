# Chapter 61: A Bar Is a Hole in a Book

The stove in the Docket of Powers' room was lit at about the half hour before the fourth hour by somebody who came in early and did not sign for the wood, and the fire had been in it long enough by the time Marek Kest came up the corridor to have stopped being a fire and started being a warm room, which is the whole difference between an office that is used and a room that is visited.

There is no sign on the door. There is a card in the window at the height of a man's chest and the card says what the room is for, and standing in front of it at the fourth hour of the eleventh of the first month were two people who had never done this, and a third who had said in advance that he would not be one of the two.

Oswin Marrow stood with his back to the register and his hands behind him.

"I am going to say one thing before you go in and I am going to say it once."

"Go on."

"You are not getting a fair hearing. I am not able to give you one and neither is anybody in this building, because there are no rules for this and there have never been any rules for it, and the two people in there are going to do the best they can with what they have got, which is a form of words they will have to make up this morning." He did not move off the book. "I say that so that nobody can say afterwards that I put my thumb on it. In about twelve hundred years of this office, the men who came in here have mostly been frightened, and I have never once had anybody tell me a thing I did not already know, and I would like it recorded that you did not."

Then he went out and shut the door behind him, which is not a thing a man does who is sitting on a hearing.

---

The two of them had been arguing about the order of the questions for about four minutes and had got nowhere, because there were no questions, and because the intake clerk of the Chancery had a sheet of foolscap with three headings on it and could not find a fourth, and because the copyist of the third gallery had brought her own book and had not opened it.

"You are not an examiner," Marek said. "You have written the time in the margin in ink and initialled it for me seven times and you have never once been thanked for any of them and now you are going to ask me questions."

"I am an officer of this court for the purpose of a door," said the intake clerk, "which is a different thing, and the order says an officer, and there was nobody else, and I have told the registrar that I have never read a book of procedure and he said that he had not either and would not be helpful in the afternoon." He put the foolscap down. "Do you want the chair by the stove or the one by the door."

"The stove."

"It is what I would have taken."

The copyist of the third gallery was Tamsin Rook, and she was a person Marek Kest had been working beside for eleven months, and on the fourteenth of the ninth month she had entered two of his acts into a public register in her own hand without asking him and told him at the twelfth hour that night, late on purpose, and had said she had broken the first half of a rule they had made with each other and would not help him pick which half he wanted to argue with.

She was now going to examine him about them.

Marek Kest sat down by the stove and worked out, in about the time it takes to put a coat over a chair, that neither of them had thought about it, and that it was the same oversight twice, and that in nine years the intake clerk of the Chancery would be a supervisor of something and Tamsin Rook would be — and he stopped, because he did not know what she would be, and because the not knowing was the first honest thing that had happened to him in four months.

"Before we start," said Tamsin Rook. "I have been in this room for four minutes and I have not written anything, and I am going to write one line before I ask you anything, and I am going to read it to you, and I am not going to argue about it afterwards."

"Write it."

She wrote it in the copyist's hand, small and fast, on the inside cover of her own book, and turned the book round.

> *Entered by me, before any question is put, on my own initiative, and not asked for: the person who entered two of the applicant's acts in this Docket's register on the fourteenth of the ninth month is one of the two persons appointed to examine him, and no one in this room has been told that by anybody except me, and I did not think about it when I said I would do it, and I am recording it now because if I do not record it now and somebody reads this file in four years there is a version of it in which I examined the man I put in a register and nothing on the face of it says otherwise. — T. Rook, the eleventh of the first month, at the fourth hour.*

"Read it back," she said.

"You have read it back. You wrote it four seconds ago."

"I have read it back," she agreed, "and that is the practice at Mosswake, and I do it now, and you may put in your book that I did it in this building as well as in that one."

Marek Kest looked at the line for about as long as it takes to read a line.

"That is the third time," he said. "It is the third thing you have done that I did not ask for and that turned out to be right, and I have got no procedure for what a person does about the third one, and I would like it on the page that I have noticed that I have no procedure for it."

"Noted," said the intake clerk, who had been writing a heading.

---

The first question was not a question about authority.

"Mr Kest," said the intake clerk. "I have got three headings and I cannot find a fourth, and I am going to ask the first one and it is going to sound stupid, and I would like you to answer it properly because if I put it in wrong the next person who reads this file will put it in wrong as well and it will be my fault and I will not be here."

"Ask it."

"What is a scale reading."

It was the question nobody had asked him in eleven months. Not the Cinder Court, which had been asked four questions and taken one. Not the Docket, which had a form of words about it and did not want a definition. Not his own office. Not Tamsin, who had been in the room at the reading on the twelfth of the ninth month and had not asked it then because there were forty people in a river court and the sentence was being ruled.

"I am going to answer it badly and then I am going to answer it properly, and you are to write down the second one and not the first," said Marek Kest. "Badly: it is a thing I can do to a piece of paper that a dragon has signed, and it shows me who the paper thinks it is talking about. Properly — and I am saying this slowly because you are writing — it is not a reading of the paper. It is a reading of the record the paper has been entered in. The paper does not change. The record moves."

"And the record moves how."

"Three lines," he said. "It says who the record recognises as a party or an heir. It says the duty the record treats as promised. And it says the consequence the record currently assigns to a successor." He stopped. "And it cannot tell you whether the promise was fair, what a witness privately intended, or whether a person will consent, and it can be perfectly correct about a document that is a lie, because it reports the record and not the world, and if the record it is standing in front of is a false copy then the three lines will be perfectly true about a false thing and completely silent about the thing that is false."

The intake clerk wrote it down. It took him rather a long time and he got the order right.

"You have said three lines and there were three headings on your foolscap," he said. "Is that a coincidence."

"It is not a coincidence, and I have not thought about why it is not a coincidence, and I would like to leave it in the file as a thing nobody has looked at."

"Leave it in the file," said the intake clerk, and did.

---

The second question came from Tamsin Rook and it was about the fourth of the fourth month.

"Tell me about the second one," she said. "The one at Reed Cut. I have it as a line in a register and I have the permission, and I have never asked you about it, and I have wanted to for four months and I have not asked because I did not think it was mine to ask."

"You had it in a register a month before you had the right to ask," said Marek Kest. "So neither of us is going to be the one to say this was the proper order."

"No. It was not."

He put his hands on his knees, because that is what he does instead of putting them flat on a table, and Tamsin Rook noticed and said nothing, which was the correct thing to do with it.

"It was the sixth of the fourth month, at about the seventh hour, alone, on a step at the Reed Cut gate," he said. "It was under the same limited audit as the first one, which has never been discharged and which nobody has discharged. I had walked down from the town because the sheet of the fifth term is nailed to that gate rail and I have never once seen a person read it, and I went down to read it and I was on the step, and I was not asked. That is the whole of the authority. I was not asked and nobody stopped me."

"What did it show you."

"Ask me what I want it to have shown me first."

"I am not going to ask a clerk a question that a court has not asked him."

"Then it showed me that the record carries nothing." He said it in about two seconds and then he stopped, because the fire had moved and it took him a moment to find where the rest of it was. "The field had a term on it and the term had run out on the nineteenth of the second month and nothing had been decided, and I went down there in the fourth month expecting — I do not know what I was expecting. I have written that down once and it reads *I expected the field to be mine and it is not and that is correct*, and I have crossed the second half out, because the second half is the finding and the finding is not a reading's business."

"And the reading said that."

"The reading said the record carries nothing, which is a different sentence, and it took me eleven seconds to understand that the two sentences had been sitting in the same place in my head for four months and I had not noticed." Marek Kest looked at the stove. "Nobody else has ever heard me say that out loud. It is in a book of mine in one line and it is in a register in four, and I have never said it in a room to a person, and I would like it recorded that the reason I have never said it in a room is that a person who says *the record carries nothing* in a room is a person who has been told what to think about by a thing that cannot be cross-examined, and I could not stand up in a room and say that I had been told."

The intake clerk wrote for a while, and then stopped, and read what he had written, and then said:

"I am going to put in that you said you had never said it in a room, because that is a fact about the room and not about you, and I have never seen a file with that in it and I want to be able to say that I put it in the first one."

---

The third question was the question, and it came at about the fifth hour, and it was the only one that cost anybody anything, and the intake clerk asked it because he had the foolscap and did not know which of the three headings it went under and therefore asked it out loud.

"How does a person get entered in that book."

Marek Kest did not answer for about four seconds, and the four seconds were the answer, and the intake clerk of the Chancery — who was twenty-five years old, who had worked at the intake desk long enough to know where the ink was, and who had never read a book of procedure in his life — looked up from the sheet.

"There is a procedure," said Marek Kest. "It is one line long and I know it is one line long because I wrote to the Docket about it in the fourth month of last year in a document, and the answer came back sealed with a seal and no name at the foot of it, and it answered two of my five questions and referred three to a district's standing instructions. A person applies in writing to the Docket of Powers and states the authority under which the person reads. That is the whole of it."

"And you did not do it."

"I did not do it." Marek Kest put his hand flat on his knee. "I asked Examinations to tell me the procedure. Examinations is nine rooms away and has no officer for a series, and what I got back was a decision on the scope of a question, and I read the second paragraph of it twice and I did not ask the question a third time, and I went and did the reading, and the reading was accurate, and it decided nothing, and it cost me a nosebleed and two days of not being able to look at print, and it is the only thing I have ever done in this building that anybody used."

"You did not send one line to three rooms off this corridor."

"No."

"Why."

"Because I was in a hurry, and because a clerk who is in a hurry and asks an office for a procedure is a clerk who is telling the office that he does not know how to do his job, and because nobody had ever asked me for a permission and I did not want to be the first." He stopped. "And because I did not want to be entered. If I had been entered, there would be a line with my name in it and it would say *under a limited audit of the Venn file*, and a person who reads under a limited audit of a file can be asked by anybody who holds the file whether he read it fairly. I did not want that on a page. So I did not ask to be entered, and then an office found out that I had not been entered, and the not being entered turned out to be the same fact as the not asking, and the not asking is the only part of it that is mine."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The stove ticked.

"Write that down," said Tamsin Rook. "All of it, and write *the applicant has said the not asking is his* at the end of it, in his words, because in four years somebody is going to read this file and work out that a young clerk in a room with a stove took four months to say that he was afraid of a line in a book, and I want the file to be the place where that is written and not a memory."

---

The bar was not lifted and he had not asked for it to be lifted and he said so twice, out loud, before anybody asked him, which the intake clerk entered as *asked and answered* and underlined, because he had learned that from somebody in another building in another province and did not know where.

The record was made out in the form used. It is a page and a half. It carries the finding, which is the finding of the third week of the eleventh month and is not disturbed, because a man who is being examined does not get to un-find a thing in the room where he is examined. It carries his account of both readings, and against his account of the second reading there is a line in Marrow's hand that says *this is the account of the person examined and is not a finding of this Docket*, and Marek asked for that line specifically, and Marrow countersigned that line specifically, and did not countersign anything else, and said so.

Marek asked for two things and got them.

The first was that the record be made out in the form used and filed in the Docket's own file, and not in the register, because the register is a register of persons recorded as having performed a scale reading under a recognised authority, and putting an examination into that book would be a lie in the only book in the building that anybody trusts. Marrow agreed with that in four seconds and it is the only thing either of them agreed to that quickly all morning.

The second was a copy.

"Sent where," said the intake clerk, because it is his sheet.

"Here," said Marek Kest, and wrote an address on a slip, and pushed it across.

It was not an office. It was four rooms above a cooper's shop near the Weir Cut side, and it was the address of a man of twenty-nine who paid rent to a woman whose name was on a lease, and there was no office in it and no seal on the door and nothing on the door at all.

"That is a lodging house," said Tamsin Rook.

"It is a room with a bed in it and a table and a window that looks at a yard, and there is no office in it, and no office can write to it and ask what is in it." He put his thumb on the slip. "I learned that from a form with two boxes in it in the ninth month. You write *None* in a box and it turns out that *None* is a person who cannot be instructed. I have no office. I have a bar. If the copy of this record is in a building with an office over the door then any person in nine years who wants to know what I did in this room writes to the man who runs the building, and the man who runs the building is a person with a tray and an address and no obligation to me whatever. If the copy is in a room above a cooper's shop then a man with a tray has to knock on a door to get it, and a man with a tray does not knock on a door, and I have watched a man with a tray not knock on a door for eleven months."

Nobody said anything about it for a bit.

"That is going to be in my book as the second time I have been told I was wrong by somebody in this matter," said the intake clerk, "and I want it noted that the first time was a copyist telling me I was going about it wrong in a room over a weigh-house office and I did not take it, and this time I am."

At about the sixth hour Marrow came back in and took the record off the table, and read it, and signed it at the foot, and then read it a second time with his thumb on the middle of the page, which is a thing he does.

"There is no entry in the register for this morning," he said, "and there is not going to be one, and I want that written at the foot so that in nine years nobody can say a register of persons was kept short. This morning was not about a person reading anything. It was about a person answering questions in a room with a stove in it, and the book for that is a file, and the file is a different book, and the difference between the two is the whole of what this office is."

Then he said one more thing, at the door, with the record in his hand.

"You asked for a thing and I have given you two. Nine years ago a man came in here and asked me for a thing and I gave him one and it was wrong and he did not come back, and I have thought about it for nine years, and the thing he asked for was to have his page in this book put right, and the thing I gave him was a page with a line through it, and he took it and did not come back and I never knew whether he understood it or took it for an answer." He put the record under his arm. "You have asked for a copy of the whole of it, in the form used, to a place nobody can be written to. That is the first time in twenty-two years that anybody has asked me for a copy of a record for a reason that was not a reason I would have given."

---

Marek Kest walked down the corridor behind the third gallery at about the seventh hour with a bar on him that had been examined and had survived, and a sheet of paper in his coat with a copy of a record on it going to four rooms above a cooper's shop, and he worked out, in about the length of the corridor, that the examination had decided nothing about whether he had any authority in the empire, which he had not had since the third week of the eleventh month and did not have now, and that it had told him one thing he had not known, which was that the instrument which had taken his work out of his hands was not a punishment and was not a ruling and was not anybody's opinion of him.

It was a line in a book with a gap in it, and the gap had been there since the fourth month of last year, and it was a gap in a book three rooms off the corridor he had just walked down, and he had had eleven months and a letter-writing habit and had never sent one line to it.

Marek Kest went back up the stairs and wrote one line on a piece of foolscap in the Received Room, and it took him about four minutes, and it is on the page in his own hand and nobody has ever read it but her:

> *Eleventh of the first month. Examined and not discharged, and I asked for no relief and meant it. What the examination told me is that I was not shut out of a room. I was never in it. There is one line in a book about how to be in it and it has been there for about twelve hundred years and I did not write it, and I did not write it because I did not want to be in it, and a man who does not want to be in a room is a man who has already decided what is in it for him. I have been doing that since the ninth month of last year and I have called it prudence in four different books. It is not prudence. It is a preference and I have found it in a room with a stove in it in front of a man of twenty-five who asked me what a scale reading was.*

> *I am not going to write to the Docket. The bar stands and I asked for no relief and I mean that, and the copy of the record is going to a room with no office over the door because I would rather be hard to find than easy to explain. I do not know yet which of those two things is the man I want to be in nine years. I have got four months to find out and there is nothing in this building that will make me hurry.*

> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the eleventh of the first month, at about the seventh hour.*
