# Chapter 75: A Bill That Was Never the Problem

The application had been made, and the papers were in a satchel on a table in a yard, and the granddaughter read them first.

That is the whole of what changed between the second of the twelfth month and the third of the fourth, and it took about nine minutes and nobody asked for it.

Silas Sowle had come at the half hour with the papers in a satchel he has carried to that gate nineteen times for a woman who has never once gone to a court. He has a solicitor's way of arriving early that makes the arriving look incidental. He put the satchel on the table under the lamp, and took out a printed form of about two sides, and turned it round so that it faced her, and then he stopped with his hand on it.

He has known her for two years and has read things to her about four times, and the four times were because the other three people in that yard could not read. He has read her a letter about a thatch, a letter about a boundary stone, and twice a letter about money. He read the letter about money by putting two fingers on the first line and going down the page with his finger and saying *this one here says*, and she followed down the page and then took the page and turned it over and said the sum herself, out loud, at arm's length, and he has thought about that since.

This time he did not put his fingers on it.

"Rose," he said. "Will you read this through to yourself first, and then read me the second page."

And Rosamund Crane, who is eleven and goes to the mill on the far side on market days and there is no school on a market day, read it through, twice, in about four minutes, and then said:

"It says here that the applicant is the person in whose interest the application is made."

"It is."

"It's her. That's her. It says her name and where she lives." The girl put a finger on the second page. "And it says there is a second person who has consented and it has got a line for the name, and her, and the line is empty."

"It is."

"It's empty because she hasn't."

"It is empty because you have not put it in yet," said Silas Sowle, "and I would like to ask you to put nothing in, and to say so out loud, and I would like to say so out loud too so that your gran hears it from two people."

Hester Crane has signed one instrument in sixty-one years. She read a form at arm's length on the ninth of the ninth month of last year, in a yard, with a girl of eleven at the gate, and the three words on it were *the present holder*, and she said *that is me, then*, and signed.

She is sixty-one. She has been the bridgewarden of Lowcross for nineteen years and she has done the whole of the public work of it out of a box in her kitchen, because the amount is about a penny a day and that is too small to be a line in a fund and too large to be a household's money, and she is about to be sixty-two and she is frightened of the fourth month.

She has refused a second instrument twice. She gave the reason the first time in full and the second time shorter, and both times a copyist in a good coat wrote it down in a hand that was not hers and read it back.

She said it now before anybody put a paper in front of her, standing in her own yard with her hands at her sides.

"No."

Nobody wrote anything down, which was Silas Sowle's doing and the best thing he did that morning.

"No," she said again, to her granddaughter. "Say it for me, and then you may write it down if you have a pen, and if you have not a pen I shall not mind."

"My gran has a pen," said Rosamund Crane, "and it is a bad one, and it is the one that comes with the rent."

---

The chamber of the district court of Auremar's lower district sits at the second hour on a morning and there is a window on the Weir Cut side, and the window has been open since the first week of the fourth month of last year and the magistrate has been told about it three times by three different people and has never shut it.

Joane Tranter has been a magistrate of that court for nine years. She refused a continuance with reasons and with a date in the twelfth month and the refusal was correct, and she has said so in open court, and it is the only thing anybody in that district has ever seen her do that cost her.

The application on the third of the fourth month was made by Silas Sowle, of this city, solicitor, nineteen years of this district's poor business, on behalf of the household of Hester Crane, and it asked for two things.

The first was a direction that the crossing at Lowcross is a work of the public of this district and is not the work of a holder, and that the district works be directed to certify it and to maintain it.

The second was a direction that the district stand the cost of the bank, abutment and eleven feet of new kerb, estimated at **nineteen pounds three and fourpence**.

The works sent **Gideon Harrow**, district engineer, about fifty, nineteen years in it, a hundred and forty returns a quarter of which he does himself, and he came with a schedule in a leather case, and every sentence he said aloud ended with a small tick of the hand that people who are used to being quoted can hear and other people cannot.

He put the works' answer on the table himself and did not read it out, and then he waited about nine seconds, and when nobody read it he said:

"I will not read that document in a room. It is my office's paper and not mine, and the clerk will read it, and if there is anything in it I have said that is not in it, I will say so at the time, and not before."

The clerk read it. It is two thirds of a page and every line of it is correct.

> *District works, lower district. Answer filed, the third of the fourth month.*
>
> *First. The office cannot stand the cost of the work described. The fund for this quarter holds six pounds and eightpence. The office cannot commit money beyond the quarter in which it is spent, and the quarter in which it is spent is not the quarter in which the work is needed.*
>
> *Second. On the nineteenth of the twelfth month this office asked this district, in writing and at the foot of a statement read aloud in this court, to stand the cost of the crossing at Lowcross, on the ground that the crossing is a work of the public of this district and is not the work of a holder. That request has been before the district. It has not been answered. The office does not know whether the district saw it and has declined it, or saw it and has not decided, or has not seen it, and the office is not able to find out, and it has not asked, because the office has in nineteen years never once written to a district to say that it cannot do a thing.*
>
> *Third. This court is not the district. A district court may not direct a district to stand a cost. The office says so respectfully and with no opinion upon whether the district ought to.*
>
> *Fourth. The office will not certify the crossing. To certify is to undertake to inspect at stated intervals and to record the result. The office cannot undertake to inspect what it has not money to keep sound, and a certificate given now would be a promise the office has no means of keeping, and the office has given a certificate of that kind in one year in nineteen and the office is not going to give another.*
>
> *Fifth. The crossing is open. It has been open since the twenty-fourth of the twelfth month, on the labour of nine unpaid persons, of whom one is an officer of this office, and the office has not asked any of them for anything, and does not know what any of them are owed, and is not going to ask.*

Nobody in that room said anything for about eleven seconds, which is a long time.

Then Joane Tranter said: "Enter the answer as filed and not as a concession."

The clerk looked up.

"Ma'am."

"Enter it as filed. Not as an admission and not as a concession and not as an acknowledgment." She had a sheet in front of her and she was not writing on it. "Nine years. I have read nine files in this district in nine years in which an office has put something in writing that was not asked for, and on the first of the sixth month of last year this district put a paper in front of me in which it told me in terms that it was powerless, and I said out loud in that room that the district gained nothing by it, and it went in the minute, and it is the most honest paper this district has produced in two years. That is the standard. I am not going to have this one read as a man wriggling."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And enter a second line in my own motion and asked for by nobody," said the magistrate, "which is that the answer of the district works, filed this day, states that this office has in nineteen years never once written to a district to say that it cannot do a thing, and that it has now asked, and that the request has not been answered, and that the court notes the second of those three facts and does not propose to do anything with either of them, because a court that does something with them in the fourth month is a court that has found a way of making this district's problem into this court's work."

---

The application was refused, and the refusal is four lines, and it is correct.

> *The application is refused.*
>
> *The first direction asked for cannot be made by this court. A district court may not certify the construction or the upkeep of a crossing under a scale instrument, and may not direct a district to stand a cost. The second direction asked for cannot be made by this court for the same reason, and the reasons given in the filed answer of the works are not contradicted by any document before me.*
>
> *The household may apply again on a date to be fixed, at the second hour, on giving seven days' notice to the clerk of this court and to the district works. Nothing in this refusal is a decision that the crossing is a work of a holder. The court has made no such decision and could not make it.*
>
> *The clerk is to send this refusal to the household free, and a copy of the answer of the works with it, and is to send both to the office of the works for its file, without a fee in either direction.*
>
> *— J. Tranter, magistrate, the third of the fourth month, at about the fourth hour.*

Silas Sowle read it out in the yard at arm's length that afternoon, twice, which is what he does, and then he read the works' answer, all of it, twice, which took about eleven minutes and which nobody in that yard had ever had done for them.

Then he read the second line of the magistrate's minute, which is the one she entered on her own motion, and he read it twice, and Hester Crane's granddaughter read it after him, out loud, from about four inches away, at a speed that was faster than his.

They got to the end and the girl said: "It says she is not going to do anything with them."

"She is not."

"Who is going to do something with them, then."

"Somebody," said Silas Sowle. "Not a court, and not today."

---

Jonas Cray came up the yard at the seventh hour, which is the hour he undertook in the twelfth month, and he was not in a good coat, which was the other part of it, and he had said in the twelfth month that he would come once a month and not in a good coat and say whatever it is that is bad to say, twice.

It is about half past seven. He stood by the bar's end, which had been taken down in the twelfth and is a bar in a lean-to in a yard now and not across a bridge.

"Two things," he said. "I said I would say the bad thing twice so I'm going to say it twice and they're the same thing twice and not two things, and then I've got one more thing and that one's not bad, it's just news, and I said I'd say whatever's bad twice and I never said I'd only say what's bad."

"Say it," said Hester Crane.

"Nothing has happened."

He said it, and waited, and said it again.

"Nothing has happened," said Jonas Cray. "In four months. The bank hasn't moved. The kerb's the same. The district got my statement in the twelfth and the district hasn't answered it and nobody's told me it's been answered, and the works has had four months of me coming up here once a month and I have had to say the same sentence about forty times, and I did say to you in the twelfth month that I would come once a month and I have, and the reason I am telling you it is the same sentence is that I would rather you heard it from me once a month than heard it from a foreman in the spring."

"And the news," said Silas Sowle.

"The news is that a clerk of the intake desk in the city came out on a cart on his own time on the twenty-ninth of the third month and stood in this yard for about an hour and a half and asked me a question I have never been asked, and I have been asked what a number is a number of four times in four years and three of those were this year."

"What was the question."

"He wanted to know what the figure on the works' return is a number of." Cray shifted his weight. "It's a number of times the bar went up and down in four weeks. That's all it is. That's all it's been for nineteen years and nobody ever asked me and I have known it every day of nineteen years and there was nobody to say it to, and now there's a figure of it in a minute of a court in this city and my name is not in the minute and the number is, and I have never in nineteen years had a number be in a building that anybody could read without me being stood next to it."

He looked at the bar in the lean-to.

"I am not complaining," said Jonas Cray. "I have got eleven hundred and forty returns a year in front of me and every one of them is a number of a thing, and about one in six of them I could not have told you what the thing was if you'd asked, and you have not asked me that either, and I am not going to sit here and be a lesson. I am telling you because a man of sixty-four in a hut at the top of that crossing is paid a penny a day to count it and nobody has asked him either, and I have been the one writing the number on his card for nineteen years, and I have known for eleven months that somebody in the city would come along and ask, and I did not know whether they would come to him or not."

"Somebody did," said Silas Sowle.

"Somebody did," said Jonas Cray, "and he came on his own time, and he is twenty-five, and he asked it at the wrong end of the bridge instead of the right end, and he did it in about a quarter of an hour, and he wrote the answer in a book he had made up himself, and I do not know his name and he did not give it and I did not ask, because he said he was not anybody and he was, and if you want me to be angry about it I can be, but I have been counting a gate for nineteen years and a woman of sixty in a lane in this city cut four of the plates off the run and carried them to a court herself when nobody asked her to, and it seems to me that a city where about nine people have done a thing nobody asked them to do in the last four months is a city where you do not sit in a lean-to being angry with a boy."

---

He read the form out on the step. Hester Crane listened with the lamp behind her and her hands on the rail of the step, and she did not interrupt him, and at the end she said the thing she had said in the twelfth month, and the reason, shorter.

"And I am sorry," said Silas Sowle.

Hester Crane turned her head.

"I am sorry," he said, and it came out badly, and he knew it came out badly, because he is a man of fifty-five who has said sorry to about four hundred clients in nineteen years and has never once said it to one of them afterwards. "I have wanted to win this since the second of the twelfth and I have not thought about a bank once."

"Then you have not been thinking about a bridge either," said Hester Crane. "You have been thinking about winning. It is a thing to want. I would rather have a man who wants it than a man who does not, because a man who does not want it is doing it for his own reasons and mine are better than his."

"Solomon and the Sad Woman," said Silas Sowle.

"I do not know the name and I do not need it."

---

At about the ninth hour he walked back up the Weir Cut with the satchel and did the arithmetic, because he could not help it, and it is what he is for, and he has known since the second of the twelfth that it is the wrong thing to be for and has not been able to stop.

The bill is nineteen pounds three and fourpence. The fund for this quarter holds six pounds and eightpence. The average of the four quarters is about six pounds. The crossing has taken about nineteen pounds out of that fund twice in nineteen years and has lost about a pound a year in the years in between. Nineteen years contains two of those repairs and not four. The real cost of the crossing is about a penny a day.

And the gap — the difference between what it costs and what there is — is too small to be a line in a fund and too large to be a household's money, and it has lived in that gap for nineteen years, out of a box, in a kitchen, and he has been carrying that number to a room since the second of the twelfth and it is not the number.

Four hundred and eleven people live on the far side of that water. The district's own return for four weeks says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings, and that is a number of a bar going up and down, and the two are not the same figure and cannot be reconciled, and the district has never counted its people and is not going to.

And the third number is the only one he has never written down, and he wrote it down on the back of a form at about the ninth hour on the third of the fourth month, sitting on a wall at the head of the Weir Cut with a bad pen.

> *The application has been refused and it was refused right. The bridge is open. Nobody is liable for it.*
>
> *The bill is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and I have carried it to four rooms and it has never once been the difficulty, and I did not know that when I started and I know it now, and what I have learned is not a thing a man can take into a court.*
>
> *What the application needed was a name. Not a person — a name. A body that can be made answerable for a penny a day, and there is not one in this district, and there has not been one since a parish was dissolved in the sixth year and nobody in Lowcross knew, because the notice went to the parish.*
>
> *So the application will be made again on a date to be fixed and it will be refused again on the same four lines, and the date is the right thing a court gave me and I have not earned it and I am not going to waste it.*
>
> *What it needs instead is a line in a fund, and the line does not exist because the amount is about a penny a day, and an amount of about a penny a day is not a thing a fund is built to hold, and I have found that out twice now in four months and I have the arithmetic in my own hand in three places and not one of them is worth the eleven minutes it took to write it.*
>
> *There is one thing on that file which is worth something and it is not in pounds, and I have had it in a coat since the second of the twelfth and I did not put it in because I did not think it was evidence. I am going to put it in, and the clerk is to read it out at the second hour, and it is nineteen words on the back of a form that came back to me on a post, and it says that the form is returned because the form cannot be entered and not because the work is refused.*
>
> *Nobody has ever read that sentence out in a room. I have had it in a coat for four months.*
>
> *S. Sowle, solicitor, the third of the fourth month, at about the ninth hour, at the head of the Weir Cut, on the back of a form.*
