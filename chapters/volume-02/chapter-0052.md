# Chapter 52: None, in the Second Box

The form had four lines and two boxes and it came to the copying alcove of the third gallery on a square of paper with a covering line on the top of it in a hand that was not Tamsin Rook's, and the covering line said that it was required of the person holding the public copies in this matter during an inquiry, by the Docket of Powers.

She read it twice standing up, which is how she read everything, and then she read the two boxes.

> *Custody of public copies held during an inquiry. To be completed by the receiving person.*
>
> *One: the documents transferred, listed.*
>
> *Two: the office of the receiving person, if any.*
>
> *Three: the receiving person signs here. Four: the date.*

The second box was the whole of it and it was not a formality, and she worked out in about four seconds why it was there, standing at the frame with a ledger open in front of her: an office that holds the public copies in a matter is an office that can be asked what it did with them, and a person who holds them and can be asked about them is a person who can be managed. The Docket was not being unkind. Somebody, at some point, in some decade, had been burned by a copy that went missing into a building with a name over the door.

And the alternative to a name is not nothing. The alternative is a woman in a copying alcove who cannot be managed, because there is no office to write to, and the only way to get anything out of her is to ask her, in a room, in words, out loud, in front of somebody.

She wrote **None** in the second box, in her own hand, and put her name on the third line, and dated it the eleventh of the ninth month, and then read it back twice and went to find the forty-one pages.

They were in the third gallery's press in a folder with a label in her own writing from the eighth month, and the label had a shilling on it and a line under the shilling that said *one is a copy, forty is a copy, and the original is in a minute book in room four of the Cinder Court and is not in this building.* She carried the folder down and put it on the alcove table and counted it, because she counts everything, and it was forty-one pages and it had been forty-one pages since the sixth of the seventh month.

"Forty-one," she said, to nobody. "It was forty-one yesterday and it is forty-one today, and I have never once in my life been able to make a number come out the same way twice, and this is the first time, and I do not know what to do with it."

---

The four pages came back on the same morning, and they came back separately, and that is the part that is worth having.

The chandler's counter page came in a fold of paper with a copyist's price on the outside of it: Bryn Mawer's page, in the hand that writes small and fast and had been taught in a copying room, and it was the only one of the four that had been added to, and the addition was a name she did not have and had never had and was never going to be told by anybody.

*S. Roke, dyers' lane, Greyfen. Says that on the ninth of the sixth month, after a woman off the vats had read a finding out in this lane in front of ninety people, she said the other half of it out loud, and that the four houses named in it are not the four who will be out. Says that two people have since asked her whether she wants a copy sent to her house, and that she has said no twice, and that she wants it written on this page that she was asked twice and said no twice, and that a person being asked twice is the whole of what she has heard about and does not want to be it.*

Sarn's page came down the river with the packet and had four first names in it against a surname and a landmark, which she had put there on the third of the seventh month off a copy of a copy that had come out of that lane in the sixth, and it was correct, and it was still correct, and it had been correct for two months.

Wendt Sayer's page was a leaf torn out of a works book with a wet ring on one corner where somebody had set a cup down, and it had nine men in it and a foreman's arithmetic in the margin, and the arithmetic was wrong by a penny and she did not correct it because it was his pencil and his book.

And the fourth was Nell's, and it was the only one of the four that had been written in a room with somebody in it, because the rule they had all four agreed was that a page is a page, and a page is not a letter, and a letter has a person at the top of it.

Three of the four disagreed with hers about the ninth non-responder, and hers disagreed with Sarn's about the four women, and the disagreement was still on all four pages, written on all four pages, in four hands, with the date, and nobody had tidied it, and she read all four standing up in the alcove and did not write a fifth page and did not merge them.

> *Four hands, the eleventh of the ninth month. Three of the four disagree with me and one of the four agrees with me about one thing and disagrees about another, and none of the four has been tidied, and I want that written down because in a year somebody will want to know whether the four hands were a network or a mess, and the answer is that they are a mess with a rule in it, and the rule is on all four pages, and a mess with a rule in it can be read and a mess without one cannot.*

---

The letter from Glasswater came on the same packet and it is the answer to the four lines she filed over her own signature on the ninth of the fourth month, and it took five months to come, and it is two thirds of a page, and it is signed.

> *The district of Glasswater, office of the district court, in answer to a request filed on the ninth of the fourth month over the signature of a roll-keeper of the third gallery at Auremar, asking what the standing instructions of this district say about the itemising of a filing made under flood authority.*
>
> *The standing instructions of this district say nothing about it. They have been amended four times since they were written and none of the four amendments mentions itemising, or the entry of filings, or any question of how a filing is put in a book. We have no practice on it and we have never had a question asked about it.*
>
> *We should say, because the request names a district and not an office, and because a person is entitled to know what she has got. This district has held a flood docket since the second year. This court has never had a flood-season continuity filing entered in it. Not one, in six years, in any quarter, of any year. If one had been made here it would have been on a book that we keep, and the book is in this office, and you may ask for it.*
>
> *And one further thing, because it is in a provincial record and it will turn up in your hand sooner or later. The filing of the tenth month that was itemised, and that has been sent up to somebody, was entered in a river court of this district, which is a different office from this one. This court has never seen it, this court does not know how it was itemised, and this court is not able to tell you anything about it, and we would rather say that in a letter than have you find out in a corridor.*
>
> *The clerk of this court has signed four hundred and some filings in six years and cannot remember making one of that description and does not think one was ever offered.*
>
> *For the district court, — A. Kesk, clerk, and I have written *Keske* because two people in the last year have written it Kesk and I would like it right on the paper at least.*

Tamsin Rook read it twice, and then a third time, and then she did the thing she did with a finding that she could not use, which was to go and stand in the passage behind the gallery for a minute and a half without doing anything.

She had asked a district to say what its standing instructions said about a practice. She had got a complete, signed, dated, courteous, correct answer. She had got the third province of a pattern that has three provinces in it.

Glasswater has never had a flood-season continuity filing.

That was the whole of the answer and it took five months and it was worth more than the pattern. The MC/FS amendment sheet went to forty-six addresses holding a flood docket, and Glasswater holds a flood docket, and Glasswater is a delta where a river court entered an endorsement on a delta landing into the filing book in the third of the tenth month and posted it to a lane in Reed Cut with nobody's name on it — and the *Venn* series is a Chancery series, and the substituted four lines in the *Sallow* file were prepared in a Mosswake court building, and a Glasswater landing officer is not the same thing as a flood-season continuity filing in a district court at all, and she had known that for four months and had asked the question anyway because she wanted the province to be a province.

Glasswater was clean. Glasswater was clean because the thing had never been offered to it, and the two hundred and some households she had been hoping to put on a board in a delta have never been in a file, and never were going to be, and a woman with a bad hat and eleven shillings in a cloth is not going to be able to make them be.

She wrote it down in her book that night in eight lines, and the last line was: *and the third province is a district that has never had one, and the finding I have wanted for four months is that the empire is not full of this. The empire is mostly full of districts where the form never came, and the harm is where it did.*

And she did not send it to Marek Kest, on purpose, and wrote down that she had done it on purpose, and the reason she wrote down was not the reason she had done it.

> *Not sent to K, and I want the reason in the book because I did not do it in a hurry and I want to be able to tell the difference in a year. The reason I did not send it is that it is a letter I asked for over my own name, and an answer to a letter I asked for is mine until I have decided what it is for, and I have known for four months that I would not be able to hand him a province that has nothing wrong with it without hearing the thing in his voice that I do not want to hear, and the thing in his voice would be *so there is less of it than we thought*, and I would have had to say no to him, and I am not going to spend the rest of a case on saying no to a man who has been given a worse answer four times in eight months and has done the same thing with it every time.*

---

The reading of the two entries into the Docket's register was not a decision she made in a room. It was a decision she made at about the ninth hour of the fourteenth of the ninth month, standing at a table in three rooms off the corridor behind the third gallery, with a clerk of the Docket she did not know bringing her a register that ran back to about sixty years and a sheet of foolscap and a pen.

She had gone there because the Docket had written to her.

The letter said, in four lines, that the Docket of Powers keeps a register of persons recorded as having performed a scale reading under a recognised authority, and that a copy of the register is available at the counter to any person, and that in the opinion of the Docket the person holding the public copies in a matter under inquiry is a person with a proper interest in the state of that register.

There was no threat in it and no request in it. It was the most threatening document she had read in a year, because it did not need either.

She looked at the register for about nine minutes. It is a bound book, one page to a year, and each entry is four lines: name, the date, the instrument, and the authority. Sixty years of names. She counted the entries in the last thirty years, because she counts, and got about four hundred and ten, which is about fourteen a year, which is a very small number for an empire with nine dragon Houses, and the smallness is the finding: a scale reading is not something that happens often, and when it happens it is written down by somebody, and a person who has done it twice and is not in the book is a different kind of animal from a person who has done it once and is.

And on the page for last year, under the twelfth of the ninth month, and on the page for this year, under the sixth of the fourth month, there was nothing at all: two gaps, and neither of them at the end of anything, one of them in a year with eleven entries already in it and one of them in the middle of a stretch of forty years in a fair upright hand, so that neither could be mistaken for a book nobody had kept up.

She asked for the sheet of foolscap. She asked for the pen. And she wrote both of them in, in her own hand, with the date, the place, the permission and the disposition, in the form the register uses, and initialled the entry, and then did the second thing, which was to write underneath, in the same hand, on the same sheet, the reason.

> *Entered by T. Rook, roll-keeper of the third gallery, on the fourteenth of the ninth month, on my own initiative and not on the request of the person named below, and I have said so on the face of the entry because an entry made by one person about another person's act is a thing the second person must be able to see and dispute, and there is no other way of it.*
>
> *The two readings are entered because a register with a gap in it is worse than a register with an entry in it, and because a gap in a register is a thing somebody can find in nine years and use, and because I am the person who would be standing next to the gap in nine years and I would rather be standing next to an entry I made than next to a hole I did not.*
>
> *This is not a finding that either reading was authorised. I have no power to make one and I have not made one. It is a finding that they happened.*

The clerk of the Docket read it, and asked her whether she understood that her name was now on a public register as the person who had recorded another person's unauthorised act, and she said that she did, and he said that nobody had ever entered anything in that book on their own initiative, and she said that she was not surprised, and he said that it would have to be countersigned by the master of the Docket, and she said that she would wait for that, and she waited forty minutes in a corridor, and the master of the Docket came out at about the eleventh hour and countersigned it and asked her one question, which was whether she had read the book at the back, and she said that she had, and he said *then you know it is a good book and it is a bad register*, and would not say more than that, and she went down the stairs and stood in the passage behind the gallery for a minute and a half.

Then she went and found Marek Kest, in a room with a barred door and a cold stove, at about the twelfth hour on the fourteenth of the ninth month, which was late, and which was chosen.

"Sit down," she said. "I have put you in a register and I want to tell you about it before you hear about it anywhere else, and the rule we made in the twelfth month of last year says one of us puts a choice in writing with a date on it and the other answers in a room in words with a date, and I have broken the first half of that rule, and I am not going to pretend to you that I did not, and I am going to answer the rest of it now."

"You put me in a register."

"I put two dates in a register in my own hand, both of which are yours, both of which were on the page in the public record already, and both of which were not in that book." She had the slip with her and she did not offer it. "The reason is that a gap in a public register is a shape. A shape with two holes in it, in the middle of a year, where two readings by a clerk of the Roll Hall are supposed to be, and in nine years somebody who wants to say that a man in this building reads instruments he has no authority to read does not have to do any work at all. He points."

"And the cost."

"The cost is that my name is on the face of the register next to yours, in a building I have no office in, and that when this becomes a thing that is argued about I am a person who put another person's act into a public book on her own initiative and without asking him." Tamsin Rook put her hands flat on the edge of his table, which was a thing she did and had never once explained. "And I want you to understand that I would do it again and I would still have told you first, and that both of those are true, and that you are going to want to argue with one of them and I am not going to help you pick."

Marek Kest looked at the slip for a long time, and then at the wall of the light well out of the window that could not see anything, and then he said:

"Ask me for it next time and I will say no."

"I know."

"And do it anyway."

"I know that as well." She picked the slip up and put it in her own book, which is where it lives. "Twenty-fourth of the ninth. I will bring the form back. It has two boxes and you have to sign the third line and I have written *None* in the second one and I want you to see that before I sign it in front of anybody."

"Show me now."

She showed him now. He read the second box for about four seconds.

"None," he said.

"None is not nothing," said Tamsin Rook. "None is a person who cannot be instructed. I want it written in the file, in your hand, that I wrote *None* in the second box and why, because in four years somebody will say a copyist took the public copies of a decision of a high court to protect a clerk who is under an inquiry, and the answer to that is that a person who can be instructed does not protect anybody, and I am not a person who can be instructed, and I have spent eleven months learning that and this is the first time it has ever been worth anything to anybody but me."

And then she said the other thing, which was the thing she had been holding since the sixth of the seventh month, and she said it without sitting down, because it was not a thing to sit down for.

"And the forty-one pages are in the alcove and not in this room, and the four pages of the list are in my chest under a winter coat in a room I do not live in, and the Docket asked me today, at the counter, in four minutes, whether the independent copy of the evidence in this matter could be produced, and I said there is one, and I said where it is not."

"What did you say."

"I said: it is in a book of my own with no office on it, in a chest I am not going to describe, and I said it in the voice I have, which is the voice of a woman telling a man a fact." Tamsin Rook squared the forty-one pages on the sill of a window that faced a wall. "And he wrote *independent copy exists, not produced, no power* at the top of a card and filed the card, and that is the whole of the answer and it took eleven months to be able to say."

"Nobody can reach a private book."

"Nobody who works in a building can reach a private book," said Tamsin. "Not the Docket. Not the Chancery. Not a registrar with a fire-door key on a chain, and I have wanted to say that sentence in a room since the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year and I could not say it because I was the only one who knew what I had."
