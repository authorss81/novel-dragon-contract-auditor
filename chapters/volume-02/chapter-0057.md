# Chapter 57: The Hearing That Was Right

The application was not an application for money. Nobody in that room had any money and it is the first thing a person would expect to be the point and it was not the point at all, and that is the whole reason the day went the way it went.

It was a continuance application: a request that the court treat the crossing at Lowcross as a matter it was already in, so that the works would have to come and be heard and could not go on doing what it had been doing, which was returning a form every quarter with a note on the reverse.

The works of the lower district is not a party that can be compelled. It is a district office. It has no standing in a court and no counsel and it sent two men: **Gideon Harrow**, the district engineer, about fifty, with a schedule in a leather case and a warding tick at the end of the sentence he said out loud, and Jonas Cray, thirty, of a good coat, who had read four questions out of a form at arm's length in a yard in the ninth month and had not enjoyed any part of it.

The household sent **Silas Sowle**, a solicitor of about fifty-five who has done this district's poor business for nineteen years and who said so, in the room, unasked, in the first four minutes.

Hester Crane was in the room. She had wanted not to be and had been told by Sowle that a person who is not in the room is a person who has not been heard, and she had said, at the door, in a voice about four feet high, that she had been in enough rooms and that she was here to be got through.

Magistrate Joane Tranter sat in the lower district court with a window on the Weir Cut side and a clerk at the back of the room and a form of words she read out at the second hour.

Marek Kest was in the room because he was a person and not an office, and he noted that on the way in, standing at the back with Tamsin Rook, who was in the room because she was the person holding the public copies and had come to be seen to have not interfered with them.

"Take your coat off if you are going to argue," said Hester Crane, without turning round.

"I am going to argue."

"Then take your coat off, because you have never in your life argued with it on."

He took his coat off.

---

He argued it for the length of an afternoon.

He argued it against the works, because an office that cannot pay is not an office that has decided not to pay and the difference is the whole of a person's case. He argued it against the district, which had entered that the sentence it used to rely on was no longer available to it on the authority of a high court it had never been heard by, and had not withdrawn its own practice because only that court can withdraw its own practice, and which therefore had one foot in each of two positions and would not put either down. He argued it, badly, at length, with the same fluency he had used on a stair in the sixth month of last year when a copyist told him he had argued the case for four hours when there was no case.

And he argued it partly against the household, which did not want to be in a room and had said so at the door, and which he argued for about seven minutes in a way that he knew at the time was wrong and went on doing, because the words were coming out of him and the words were true and true is not the same as helpful.

"The household says—"

"The household is in the room," said Silas Sowle, "and will speak for itself, and the clerk will read for it if it cannot, and you are not the household, sir, and I have been in this district nineteen years and I have watched about nine men make that mistake in this room."

That was fair and Marek Kest sat down for about four minutes and then got up again, which was the mistake.

Harrow did the thing that a district engineer with a schedule in a leather case does. He read the schedule. It is four lines and it is correct.

> *Crossing at Lowcross, public timber cart bridge over the Weir Cut. Bank at the Lowcross end failed in the first week of the eleventh month. Bank and abutment works, kerb eleven feet, estimated nineteen pounds three and fourpence. District works fund for the quarter, six pounds and eightpence, committed. District works is not able to commit money beyond the quarter in which it is spent. District works has not received an application from this district for the crossing, and has not made one, and does not know that the parish named in the works' book was dissolved in the sixth year.*

"That last line," said Joane Tranter, without looking up. "The clerk at the counter said on the ninth of the ninth that the column wanted a body."

"The column wants a body," said Harrow. "The column has wanted a body since the crossing went in and it has had one for nineteen years and the body went in the sixth year and nobody has been able to tell me who to put in it. I have a hundred and forty of these a quarter. I do forty-one of them myself. I am not a man who has an opinion about a word. I am a man who cannot enter a form, and a form that cannot be entered is a form that comes back, and a form that comes back is a bridge that does not get mended."

"And if the court were to hold that no guarantor duty attaches to the household," said Tranter, "what happens to the crossing."

Harrow did not answer for about four seconds, which is twice as long as a man in a leather case usually takes.

"Then the crossing is a public thing with nobody holding it," he said, "and the district has a schedule with a hole in it where the crossing was, and on the fourteenth of the twelfth month the works will put the crossing out of use, because the works cannot certify a public crossing it has not got money for, and a bridge you do not certify is a bridge somebody goes over and is killed on."

"Somebody goes over and is killed on," said Hester Crane, from the back.

"Somebody goes over and is killed on," said Gideon Harrow, "or four hundred and eleven people go four miles to a market, or a mill's carts take a day longer, or a woman of eighty-one does not come over. I am not saying any of that to frighten anybody. I am saying it because it is in the schedule and I have read it out."

Joane Tranter refused the continuance at about the seventh hour, with reasons, and with a date in them, and the reasons were short and the date was the fourteenth of the twelfth month, and Marek Kest heard the date and understood the whole shape of the day about four seconds too late.

"Refused," said the clerk. "The application for continuance is refused. The household may apply again in the first week of the fourth month of next year. The crossing is out of use on the fourteenth of the twelfth month unless the works certifies it before that day."

Nobody said anything. Harrow closed the leather case. Sowle put his hat under his arm and looked at the floor and then, unexpectedly, at Hester Crane, with an expression of the plainest human dislike, because he had wanted to win this and had thought for about four minutes that he was going to.

Then everyone began to do the thing people do at the end of a hearing, which is to gather papers.

---

It had not gone the way it went in about four minutes. There had been three hours before that, and the three hours were the part Tamsin Rook wrote about afterwards in nine lines and did not send to anybody.

The district had filed an answer of its own, and the clerk read it out, and it is the most honest piece of paper any office in this district has produced in two years, and it is honest the way a rope is straight.

> *The district has been asked to say what it will do in place of the sentence of the twelfth of the ninth month of last year, that sentence being recorded by the Cinder Court of the seventeenth of the sixth as model wording and not as a decision of any court, and not to be the recorded reason for a decision in any district without a hearing of the person it names.*
>
> *The district has not withdrawn its own practice, and cannot, because this court is the only body that can withdraw its practice, and it has not been asked to and does not offer to. The district is aware of a high court's order and is not bound by it, and will say so in this room, and the district's position is that it will apply the law as it stands and that the law as it stands does not permit a person to be joined as guarantor without a hearing and a signature.*
>
> *The district will therefore do nothing in this matter, because there is nothing in it that the district may do.*

"Read the last four words again," said Joane Tranter.

"*Because there is nothing in it that the district may do.*"

"That is the answer of an office that has understood the law and drawn the only conclusion available to it," said the magistrate, "and I want it on the record that I have read nine files in this district in nine years and this is the first one where an office has told me in writing that it is powerless. The district should know what that costs: nothing, and nothing gained, and the two are not the same thing."

Hester Crane said, from the back: "Then somebody has to do it, and there are three people in this room and the third one is sixty-one."

"I am not going to sit a woman of sixty-one and a district engineer and a clerk of the Roll Hall in a row and ask which of you it is," said the magistrate. "That is not how this room works. I am going to ask the engineer a question and get an answer, and then I am going to rule, and the ruling is going to be short."

The question she asked Harrow was whether the works could certify the crossing for the fortnight before the fourteenth, and Harrow said no, and the reason he gave was that a fortnight is not a fortnight in the third month of a year when there is ice on it, and that the works puts up the bar when the bar is needed and not on the day of a letter, and that in his experience a bar is put up about two days before anybody plans it, and that the district's notice period is a courtesy between offices and not a fact about a bridge.

Nobody in the room had thought about the two days. Marek Kest had thought about the fourteenth of the twelfth month for about nine minutes and had not thought about the twelfth, and neither had Hester Crane, and neither had Sowle, and it is not a stupid thing to have missed. It is what a date is when there are no people standing under it.

And then Marek Kest made the mistake, and it was the last mistake of an afternoon full of them, and it was the one that Tamsin Rook wrote about.

He stood up and said that the public-necessity sentence was model wording and not a decision of any court, and that no district might use it as a recorded reason without a hearing of the person it names, and that the district's own answer said so in its own words, and that the application ought to be granted on the footing that the only instrument that could take the crossing out of the works' schedule was a printed model of general wording which the high court had said was not a law.

And Silas Sowle said, "Sir, with respect, and I say it as a man who has wanted to win this: that is a very good argument and it is about a sentence, and this case is about a bank."

Nobody spoke for about four seconds.

"Take that down and read it back to him," said Joane Tranter.

The clerk read it back to him.

"Does he withdraw it."

"I do not withdraw it," said Marek Kest. "I withdraw the submission that it entitles the application to be granted, and I do not withdraw the observation, and I would like both of those in the minute in those words."

"Both are in the minute," said the magistrate.

That is the afternoon. Seven paragraphs, four of them about a printed sentence, one of them about a form that cannot be entered, one of them about a woman who had been in enough rooms, and one of them true, and the true one was not the one that won.

---

And in the four minutes after that, Marek Kest stopped arguing.

He had it in his own book that the day he argued four hours when there was no case, a copyist had come and found him on a stair and said so, and had said she would go on saying it because she had run out of other ways. He had heard her on the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year. He heard her again, in the middle of a room with a magistrate in it and a district engineer putting a warding tick at the end of a sentence, and what he heard was not the sentence. What he heard was that he had done it again, and worse, and that two people had come out of it worse than they had gone in, and that one of them was a woman of sixty-one who had said at the door that she had been in enough rooms.

The court rose. Harrow was collecting the schedule. Sowle was talking to the household about a further application in the fourth month. Tamsin Rook was at the back with a copy of the order in her hand and her pen out and she had not written anything for two hours, which is the longest she had gone without writing in the whole of his acquaintance with her.

Marek Kest said, to a man with a leather case, four feet away, in a voice that had nothing in it that had been prepared:

"Mr Harrow. One question and it is not mine, it is the court's, and I am going to ask it badly and you may say no."

The engineer stopped.

"Has the district works ever asked this district to stand the cost of this crossing."

"I have no idea what you mean."

"Not ever. Not once in the works' history. Not in a minute, not in a return, not in a letter. Has any works officer ever put on paper that the works cannot do this and that the district must."

Gideon Harrow stood in a court with a schedule in his hand and did not answer for long enough that the clerk at the back of the room looked up.

"No," he said. "We have never done that."

"Why not."

"Because we are a works," said Harrow. "We are an office that builds and repairs and we have a schedule and a fund and a quarter. We are not an office that reports on itself. A works that writes down that it cannot do a thing has written down that a thing is not being done, and then somebody in the district reads it, and then it is not the works' decision any more, it is the district's failure, and the district will find a reason to do nothing about it, and the crossing will be a hole in a schedule in a book in a building and everybody will be satisfied." He put the case under his arm. "I have forty-one of these a quarter and I do them myself, and I have never once in nineteen years written to a district to say that I cannot. If I had, I would have been right and I would have been thanked by nobody, and the crossing would have gone the same way, and there would be a piece of paper in a file about it."

"Somebody would have had to answer it," said Marek Kest.

"Yes," said Gideon Harrow. "Somebody would have had to answer it, and that is precisely why nobody has ever done it."

And Joane Tranter, from the bench, in a voice that was not aimed at anybody, said:

"Clerk. Put that in the minute as a question asked and not answered. And put in that no officer of the district works has at any time asked this district to stand the cost of any crossing, and that I believe it, because I have been in this district nine years and I have never had such a paper and I would have kept it."

The clerk wrote it. It is in the minute. It is one line and the magistrate's name is at the foot of the order and not at the foot of the line, and the line is a question, and nobody answered it in a room, and it went down the river that night to be printed with a hearing that was correct and a date on it.

---

On the steps, at about the seventh hour and a half, with the river on the right and the wharf on the far side of it and the wind coming up off the water the way it does at that hour:

"You argued it for five hours," said Tamsin Rook.

"I know."

"You argued it for five hours when there was no case, again, in a room, out loud, in front of a woman who told you at the door that she had been in enough rooms." She had the order in her hand and she had not looked at it. "And in the fourth hour you argued for the household that you were not the household, and a solicitor stopped you, and he was right, and you sat down for four minutes and then got up again."

"Yes."

"And the last thing you said in there was the best thing anybody said in there and it was to Gideon Harrow and it was not addressed to the court." She folded the order once. "You have now lost a hearing in a room and won a line in a minute in the same afternoon, and the line is worth nothing on its own, and it is the only thing in two days that anybody will still be talking about in the fourth month, and I do not know whether that is a good way to be good or a bad way and I have decided I am not going to tell you."

Marek Kest stood on the steps and looked at the water.

"What was the question," he said. "I have been in that room since the second hour and I have had four of them and I could not tell you one of them, and I would like to be told the one I should have asked first."

Tamsin Rook looked at him for about four seconds, and then she said it in the flat voice, and she said it out loud on the steps of a district court in front of a river, and it is the sentence the whole of this case is standing on and it is hers and not his, and she gave it to him the way she has given him everything since the ninth of the sixth month of last year, which is once, in words, with a date on it.

"Whose name is on it," she said. "That is the second one. That has been the second one since the ninth of the sixth month of last year, and it went on a board in a lane and in a letter in a schoolroom hand four hundred and forty miles off, and there is a child of eleven in a yard in the lower district who worked it out in four seconds on the fourth line of a works note, and you have spent the whole afternoon in a room today and you did not ask it once."

Then they went down to the wharf, and the packet boat had gone, and the crossing was to be out of use on the fourteenth of the twelfth month, and both of them were wrong about that, in the way people are wrong about dates, by about two days.

The order says the fourteenth. A bar went across the Lowcross end at about the second hour of the **twelfth of the twelfth month**, by two men from the district works with a ladder and a length of chain and an ordinary day's order from a foreman, because there was going to be ice in the next three days and a foreman who has put a bar up two days early on a public crossing is doing his job, and because nobody in that room had thought about the two days, and neither had the magistrate, and she entered it afterwards in a line of her own, without being asked, in these words: *the court notes that the works have anticipated the date by two days, that the court has no power over the works in that, and that the court would not have wished otherwise.*

Nobody went across it on the twelfth, because the market was the day after.
