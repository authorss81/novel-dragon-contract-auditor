# Chapter 89: The Half the Man in the Hut Cannot Answer

She goes over twice a week for flour and she has gone over twice a week for fifty-one years, and the bar is up at the end of the lane and not at the end of a bridge, and everybody who uses that crossing thinks the bar is the crossing, and it is not, and she has never said so to anybody because there has never been anybody to say it to.

She is about sixty-five, and she keeps hens and takes the eggs across on the last day of the week, which is how she is known in that district, and the file does not have her in it and neither does the district and neither does the works, and she has never signed anything in her life with a name on it, and she has signed about four things with three fingers on a line, the last of them in a year she does not put a date to, and none of them were about the crossing.

There is a lean-to about nine feet by six at the head of the crossing on this side, made of tarred board, and there is a stove in it and a stool and a shelf and a kettle and a card in a tin frame, and the man who keeps the card has been in it nineteen years and is sixty-four and gets a penny a day whether anybody crosses or not.

She goes in there when it is raining because it is the only roof for ninety yards, and he has never once said anything about that, and there is a second stool in the lean-to that is not the one he sits on and has not been the one he sits on for a long time, and it is there because a person put it there about nine years ago.

---

The magistrate came on the twenty-eighth of the seventh month, at about the eleventh hour in the morning, and she came on foot, and there is nobody else in the district court of the lower district who walks.

She was not looking for what she found. She is quite explicit about that on the page and it is the reason the thing works, because she has entered three things in a minute in nine years that nobody asked for and every one of them was found while she was doing something else.

What she was doing was this. On the fourteenth of the fifth month an officer of hers had served a notice at a yard in this district on the person the file calls the holder of the crossing, and had written four lines on the back of it, and on the twenty-first of the fifth month she had entered that the person was not in court and had not refused and that the court could not tell those two things apart. A notice has to be served and an order has to be made and the order has to be certified, and she cannot certify a service she has not looked at, and there is no procedure in nine years for looking at one except going.

So she went to look at the service, and the yard was shut and the door was on the latch and there was nobody in, and the crossing is ninety yards from that yard and she went to the crossing because she was in the district and because she has been at that crossing twice since the twelfth month and it is four hundred yards from the office.

She went into the lean-to because it was raining.

---

She asked the man one question because she has to certify the cost of a thing and the only number in this matter that anybody can give her is a penny a day and she has had it since the third of the fourth month.

"How long have you been sitting here."

"Nineteen years this month," said Emrys Rudd. "And I will tell you the same thing I told you in the fourth month, ma'am, and you did not come to ask me that in the fourth month, you came to ask me what the figure is a number of, and the figure is a number of times the bar went up and down in four weeks, and that is in your minute with no name in it, and I read it in a copy somebody brought me in a cart, and there is a line in it that says you are the magistrate who did not ask for four months, and I thought that was very good of you."

"I have come to certify a service and I cannot certify a service I have not looked at, and a yard is not a crossing," said Joane Tranter, "and I have about eleven minutes and I have a clerk, and I am going to ask you the same question I asked you in the fourth month because it is the only one that matters, and then I have to go and write something down, and I have come on foot and I am going to be in a wet coat for a day because I have never learned to do this differently."

She asked it.

"Emrys Rudd. If a person crosses that water without the bar moving, does your card know."

And he said no, and he said why, and it is on the page, and it is about a bar and a chain and about the hour of the night.

"The bar goes up at the end of the day when the foreman shuts the crossing for the night, and the chain goes on the gate, and a man comes out from the mill side at about the fifth hour in the morning and opens the gate and goes across and comes back and shuts it again, and there is nobody counting at that hour and there has not been for nineteen years, and there is nobody paid to count at that hour because the penny says *sitting*, and sitting is a daylight thing, and the man that opens that gate is a carter and he is doing his round and he is not paid by this district and he does not know there is a card in a tin frame eight feet from his elbow."

"That is not a person crossing while it is shut," said the magistrate. "That is a person crossing in a lawful way while the bar is up. I am asking about a person who is not lawful."

"I know what you are asking and I have got no answer for it and I have had nineteen years to get one and I have not got one," said Emrys Rudd, "because I have never once seen anybody go over that water when the bar was not going and I did not think it was possible, and I have been wrong, and I have been wrong for a long time and nobody told me, and I am sixty-four and I have been wrong in a hut on my own."

Then he stopped and looked past her at the first house past the bar, which is eleven yards away and has a door in it that opens on the crossing and not on a lane.

"Ask her," he said. "She goes over twice a week for flour and she has gone over twice a week for fifty-one years and I have been here nineteen and I have never once asked her a single question in nineteen years because I have been paid a penny a day to sit, and asking is not sitting."

---

The magistrate had a clerk with a book and it was raining and there was a woman of about sixty-five standing in a lean-to with a man in it, and she asked her.

Her name is not on this page and she is not going to give it and nobody asked her for it, and the minute of this court says so and says that the court did not ask.

The magistrate asked four things and they took nine sentences in all, and the clerk wrote them in the order they were said, and the woman said the second half of the answer twice, in the same words, because the first time the wind took it and the second time the magistrate asked her to say it again and she did without being asked to be sure.

"It is a number of the bar," she said. "Everybody who goes past that hut goes past that bar. The bar goes up for the ones who come through in the day and it comes down and it goes up again, and he counts it. **And I do not go past the bar.**"

"How do you cross."

"I have a gate. There is a gate at the mill end and it has a chain on it and the chain has a padlock on it and the padlock is broken and has been broken since before the War of Nine Bridges or after it, I do not know which, and it has been broken for as long as I can remember and nobody has mended it and nobody has come to take it away, and there is a plank on that side, and you can walk over on the plank when the water is low and you cannot when it is high, and when it is high I wait, and when it is low I go."

"Nobody has ever asked you about the padlock."

"Nobody has ever asked me about anything," said the woman. "I am seventy. I have been going over that plank for fifty-one years and I have crossed it about five thousand times and I have never been asked one question about it by anybody in this world, and I am not complaining, and I am telling you because you asked and because you are the first person who has ever come to that hut and asked me anything and you are not going to like it."

"Tell me anyway."

"Then you are going to hear that the three hundred and eighty-eight is wrong in a way that a man cannot get wrong, because a man counts the bar and the bar is the crossing and the crossing is what everybody thinks of when they think of a crossing, and the plank is not a crossing. There is a plank and it is a plank. **A person can cross that water and the card does not change at all, and it is not a trick and it is not a fraud and I have never once thought of it as anything, and I have known it every single morning for fifty-one years.** And there is a woman at the mill who comes at night with a pail and the bar is up and the chain is on and she comes through the mill yard and not the bar's end, and I have not seen her in about nine years and I would know her in the dark. And there are men who stand on the bank and fish the shallow for two hours and the bar is down the whole time and they are not on the bar's end at all. And there is a boy of about nine who goes over on his hands on the plank and his mother does not know and he has been doing it since the spring."

"Does the man in this hut know about the plank."

"He has never asked me a question in nineteen years and he is paid to sit," said the woman. "He is a good man. He has kept a card every day for nineteen years for a penny a day and he has not once been asked what the number is a number of and he has not once been asked whether he is counting the right thing, and both of those are the same question and both of them were available to about nine hundred people in this city, and none of them came."

---

The court entered it at about the ninth hour of the twenty-eighth of the seventh month, and it is the fourth thing in nine years that Joane Tranter has entered on her own motion and asked for by nobody, and it is the shortest of the four and the one that is going to be read, and here it is in full.

> *Entered the twenty-eighth of the seventh month, on the magistrate's own motion, asked for by nobody.*
>
> *The figure of three hundred and eighty-eight in the return of the district works for the four weeks is a count of the times the bar at the crossing at Lowcross was raised and lowered in that period, and was entered as such in this court on the third of the fourth month. On the twenty-eighth of the seventh month the magistrate further asked, in a lean-to at the head of that crossing, whether a person may cross that water without the bar moving.*
>
> *She is told, and the court accepts for the purpose of this entry and makes no finding, that a person may. A gate at the mill end of that crossing carries a chain and a padlock and the padlock has been broken for longer than any person now living; a plank lies on the mill side; the water is low at some seasons and not at others; and persons cross on that plank, and persons cross at the mill end, and persons stand on the bank to fish, and none of these crossings raises or lowers the bar.*
>
> *The court enters that it asked a question and was given an answer, and that the answer is that the figure in that return omits every person who crosses that water without the bar moving, and that the omission has always been present in the figure and is not an error in the counting and has never been a matter of anybody's conduct.*
>
> *The court makes no finding. It makes no order. It names no person, and it did not ask any person for a name, and both of those are entered. Four hundred and eleven people are on the far side of that water according to a schedule in a file. Three hundred and eighty-eight crossings in four weeks is a number of a bar. Those are different figures and they are not reconciled by this court and cannot be reconciled by anybody, and the court enters that it has now heard, for the first time in nine years, from a person who lives on the far side of that water, and that no person in this lean-to is paid anything for what was said and no person in it is named in this minute, and that the person who keeps the tally has not been paid for his keeping it for nineteen years and has not been named in the minute of the third of the fourth month, and that this court entered both of those on the same line, and is entering them again, and means it.*
>
> *And under the same line, and for the same reason as in the fourth month: I did not ask for four months. I did not ask again in the fifth month, after a notice went out of this court to a yard. I did not ask this month. I have now asked twice since the fourth month and both times I went to the crossing for another reason and found the answer standing in a lean-to, and a court that asks a question once in nineteen years and then finds it has been asking it, has learned that the question was not the difficulty, and I am entering that in the same book, with the day on it, because in nine years somebody will read the four entries and will want to know why they came out in that order, and the order is the finding.*
>
> *— J. Tranter, the twenty-eighth of the seventh month, at about the ninth hour, in the chamber, with a clerk in the room.*

---

What it cost is written down in her own account the same evening, and it is the last thing in this chapter and it is not in the minute, because a minute is a public file.

There is a line in a file in this city now about a woman of about sixty-five who lives in the first house past a bar and who cannot read a paragraph, and there is no name in it. That is the first time in my life or in nine years in this court that a person has been written about in a file in this city and there is no name in it, and I did that, and I did it on purpose, and I did it because the answer was in a lean-to and not in a file and the only way to get an answer out of a lean-to is to write it down, and the only thing about a lean-to that does not travel is a name.*
>
> *Nobody has ever asked that woman anything. I asked her four questions in about nine minutes in the rain and she is seventy and she had the good manners to say that she was not complaining and then told me the worst of it anyway, and what she told me is that a court in a city four miles away has now got a thing about her, and she cannot read it, and she will never know what it says, and in nine years the only person alive who will know that the line is about her is me.*
>
> *And I am fifty-one and I have nine years in this court and I do not have to be here in nine years, and if I am not, then that line is about nobody, and a line about nobody is a line that can be denied by anybody who reads it in a bad light, and the answer to a question asked by a magistrate in a lean-to will be a document that a stranger can take apart, and it will take about four minutes, and nobody will be able to say it is wrong, because there is no person in it to say it is about.*
>
> *That is the finding and I do not like it and I entered it anyway, and the reason I entered it is that the alternative is a line with her name in it, and I have been in this court nine years and I have watched about four hundred people learn what a name in a file is worth, and I would rather leave a true thing that can be denied than a true thing that can be used. And I would like somebody in nine years to tell me that was the right way round. I do not think it was the right way round. I think it was the only way round that did not require me to make a person findable in order to defend her.*
>
> *The crossing is open. The bar goes up at the end of the day and the chain goes on the gate and the padlock is broken. Nobody is liable for the crossing. The bill is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and the fund holds six pounds and eightpence for the quarter and about six pounds on the average of the four, and there is no line for a bridge in that fund and there has not been one in nineteen years, and the real cost of that crossing is about a penny a day and about three pounds a year, and the tally has cost about fifty-seven pounds over nineteen years, and nobody has ever claimed a penny of it, and I have now written in a file that the number of the tally omits a plank and a broken padlock, and none of that puts a penny back into a woman's kitchen at the other end of the bar, and I have to go and tell her that, in person, in about four days, because I am not going to put it in a letter and a letter is a document and she cannot read a document, and I am not going to send a clerk, and that is a day of a magistrate's time and there is no heading in any fund for that either.*
>
> *— The twenty-eighth of the seventh month, at about the eleventh hour at night, at my own table, no office at the top of it.*

---

Nothing has come up that road about the eighteenth of the seventh.

She noticed that on the nineteenth, the way a person notices a thing that should have happened and did not: a carter or a rider off the city road comes down that road about twice a month for one thing or another, and the woman of about seventy has been watching that road from her door since she was nineteen, and there was nothing on it on the nineteenth.

She has never been to a court in a city. She does not know what a court in a city is. She knows that there is a building in this city with a mark on the door of it that is not the mark on the crossing, and that the man in the hut says a notice went into that building in the third month of this year with nothing at the foot of it, and that a day was set in it for the eighteenth of the seventh, and that the day was the day before yesterday, and that nobody has come up the road about it, and that in seventy years she has never once been told anything that came out of a room in a city and does not expect to start now.
