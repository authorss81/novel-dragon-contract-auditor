# Chapter 84: A Question and a Form

The instrument of appearance came into Auremar on the morning packet of the fifteenth of the sixth month and was laid on a notary's table at the Kiln end of the Crown Kiln at about the tenth hour. It is one sheet with two ends. The far end was sealed and spoken about two hundred miles away, in a chamber of a House, on the fourteenth, by a notary and a witness. The near end was sealed and spoken on the morning it arrived, by a notary of this city and a witness at his elbow, because the form has always had a witness at it since about a hundred and forty years ago. Nobody travelled.

An instrument of appearance is the shape a person uses when they are not in the room and the room has to have them in it anyway, and it is the only form in this business that has ever been made for a dragon, and it is made in a house's own chamber and not in this city, and it is worth putting down what it is, because a great deal in the next two months depends on what it does.

At the near end there is a person: a notary of this city, of about fifty, in the ordinary chair, with a witness at his elbow. At the far end there is a device, and a person speaking to it, and a witness at that end too.

The seal is laid. The notary speaks the assent, in the ordinary words, and the words take about eleven seconds, and while they are being spoken the impression is on the paper. Then the witness writes the time. Then the far end has a person and the near end has a person and the sheet is sealed at both ends and it goes on the file.

The eleven seconds is not a ceremony. It is the whole of how a true seal is made and it is the reason a true seal cannot be forged, and the reason is nineteen days away and not in this room, and no clerk in this city has ever needed to know it until the second of the second month of this year, when a card about the size of a playing card went into a district court with an impression on it and no rim.

The notary of House Oris was at the far end. His name is Vaunt Oris and he is a House Oris notary in his own right and not an officer of any wing of anything, and he is fifty-one or two and he has been in that chair for nineteen years and he reads human inheritance records as slowly and as carefully as this city reads a river return.

The notary in this city had the sheet in front of him and had read it twice, and then he said the thing that had to be said, out loud, on the record, before anything else, because it is the practice in that room.

"You will say first," he said, "that you are not the person at the foot of the notice of the tenth of the fourth month, and that you are not sent by her, and that your House does not speak with one mouth, and I am going to write that at the head of this record in your words and not mine. Take as long as you like with it."

"I will say it now," said Vaunt Oris.

"I am Vaunt Oris, a notary of House Oris, in a chamber of House Oris, and I am not the First Seat and I am not her agent and I am not acting at her instruction and I have had no communication with her in this matter. I am the same surname. My House has held the First Seat for a hundred and forty years and I have not held it for a day of that, and the surname is a fact about my family and is nothing whatever about her standing, and I would ask that the second thing be recorded because in about four years somebody will read a sheet in a file and see the surname twice and join them, and they will be joining two people who have never spoken.

"House Oris does not speak with one mouth. I am a notary. I certify what is put in front of me and I refuse what I cannot. I have refused four documents in nineteen years, of which two were my own House's, and both of those refusals are in this city's book as well as mine, and I do not resent them and I have never asked anybody to take them out."

"Good," said the notary of this city, and wrote it, and read it back.

"I will also say, because the notary of this city will not ask me and I have found in nineteen years that it saves time," said Vaunt Oris, "that dragons live a very long time and humans do not, and that in my House it is held as a working opinion, not a doctrine, that a dragon who forgets a promise is a rarer thing than a human who forgets one, because a dragon has to hold the same set of obligations for six or seven lifetimes and a human has one, and that this is the reason a dragon notary is so careful about the order of the day of a hearing and why you will find in my House's procedure a rule that no instrument takes effect in a room before the persons it touches have had the day. It is not a law. It is a house practice of about four hundred years and it is a good one, and I have never once been able to persuade a human court to adopt it, and I have stopped trying, and I mention it because you are about to see me use it."

---

The question is one question and it is on the second half of the sheet and it is nine lines long and it has a form behind it.

> *I. This is an instrument of appearance made and sealed at a notary's table of House Oris in a chamber of that House, and put to this court by a notary of that House in his own right, and it asks the court sitting in room four to answer one question and to enter the answer in its minute, and I am told by the notary of this city that the question is answerable and I am glad of it, because a question that is not answerable is a question about a person and I am not a person.*
>
> *II. The question concerns a form this court published in the reign of the third of the Ashfall line and read out in full in open court in the sixth month of the eleventh year of last year, being the form by which a seat is entered in a matter by its device, in which it is stated that no person of or belonging to that seat is party, cited, or examinable, and that no inference of any kind may be drawn from the fact that a person has not been named. That form has been used in this matter on the tenth of the fourth month. I have read the form and I adopt it and I do not ask this court to depart from it.*
>
> *III. The question is this. Where a seat is entered in a matter by its device, and no person of or belonging to that seat is party, cited, or examinable, and a court is asked to hold the matters touching an instrument until a court of another jurisdiction has addressed the question, then:*
>
> *First. To whom is the order that holds those matters to be served.*
> *Second. If there is no person upon whom it can be served, then by what means is a person who is affected by those matters to be told that they are being held.*
> *Third. If neither is possible, then what is the standing of an order so made, against a person who has not been heard about the matter it holds.*
>
> *IV. I ask the question because a seat's notice of the tenth of the fourth month offers, in its fifth line, to pay the carriage of any person whom the district court thinks should be heard, without asking to know why, and to pay it twice if the person comes twice. My House does not know whom that district court thinks should be heard. Neither, I am given to understand, does the district court. I have asked, and the answer is that nobody has asked.*
>
> *V. There is a person in this matter whose name is on a document and who is not the seat and is not me, and I do not put her forward and I do not speak for her and no inference may be drawn from the fact that she has not been named. If the court finds that an order of this kind cannot be served upon her, then the court will have found that the person whose name is on the document is not served by it, and I think the court will find that, and my House will have to decide what to do about it, and I would rather that it did that on a question properly put than on a difficulty discovered by somebody else in about four years.*
>
> *Given at a notary's table of House Oris, and sealed, and spoken, and witnessed. — Vaunt Oris, notary.*

Tamsin Rook copied it out twice, in a copyist's hand, in plain words, on the fifteenth, and read both back, and the second one is the one in the file and the first one is the one she took up the corridor to the Received Room with, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, because the undertaking of the fourth of the first month says that she brings him every sheet that goes out of this matter to a solicitor, a household, or an office outside the Roll Hall, and this one was going to a notary's table, and neither of them argued about whether a notary is an office.

He read it standing up. He read her copy and then he asked for the original, which is the second of the two sheets the notary of this city had certified, and it has a device on it and a seal and an impression and a witness's time, and it goes in a locked drawer at the Kiln end and Marek Kest read it standing up in a room with a barred door, which is a thing that has happened in this book before and which nobody has thought to write a rule about.

He read both about four times and then he put them down.

"One word," he said. "That is all I have and it is out loud and in a room and you may refuse it."

"Go on."

"You have written *suspend* in four places and the original says *hold*," said Marek Kest. "One is in your first line and two are in your note of the answer and the fourth is the phrase at the top of your own page, and I want all four out. The word *suspend* is a word about a person. A court suspends a person, or suspends a person's evidence, or suspends an officer, and there is a proceeding for it and it is in a form and somebody's name is at the foot of it. **A court does not suspend anything. A court holds a matter.** That is the whole of what it did on the fourteenth of the fourth month and the word on the notice is *hold* and the form's word is *hold*, and if the copy in a file in nine years says *suspend* then somebody will read it and think a court can stop a thing happening by writing a word, and no court in this empire can stop a thing happening by writing a word, and a great many people in this matter have spent fourteen months finding that out at about four pence a sheet."

Tamsin Rook looked at the two sheets for about a minute and a half.

"That is right," she said, "and it is the third time that undertaking of yours has caught something and every single time it has been a **word** and never once a fact, and I have been a copyist for two years and I thought the job was the facts."

"The facts are fine," said Marek Kest. "You cannot get a fact wrong in a way that is true in nine years. A word is the whole of the danger and there is no instrument in this empire for it and I do not know why that is and I have stopped asking."

She changed the four. She wrote the word *hold* four times and read the sheet back a fifth time and then, out of a habit that belongs to somebody else in a corridor, she struck the word *suspend* out in four places and did not ink it, so that in nine years a person could see that it had been there.

---

The answer went back on the sixteenth of the sixth month, at about the fifth hour, and there were three things in it and one of them cost a person a day.

**One.** To whom the order is to be served: to no person. The order of the fourteenth of the fourth month was not served upon the person named in the eleventh line of the schedule of persons who may be answerable in the matter, was not served upon any other person, and could not have been, and the registrar of this court has entered that in the minute of the twenty-sixth of the fifth month on his own motion and asked for by nobody, and the entry is a part of this matter and both notaries have read it.

**Two.** By what means a person affected is to be told: by no means this court has. The notice's fifth line offers the carriage of any person whom the district court thinks should be heard. **The district court has not been asked, and does not know, and the notice's own terms require the district court to be asked.** So the offer is live and inoperable, and it is inoperable in a way that nobody has noticed for two months, and Vaunt Oris noticed it in four days from two hundred miles away, off a form published in the reign of the third of the Ashfall line.

**Three.** What is the standing of an order so made against a person who has not been heard: **this court enters that an order which cannot be served upon a person is not served upon that person, and that it does not bind that person, and that this is a finding about service and is not a finding that any person is or is not liable for anything, and no person is named, and the court makes no other order and lifts nothing.** It is six lines and there is a notary of this city at the foot of it and a notary of House Oris at the foot of the sheet it was answered on.

The counsel of the seat lost a day, and it is entered with a reason, on the notary of this city's own motion, and the reason is on the page because the whole of this book is about what a day costs.

The seat, having been told that its carriage offer cannot operate until the district court says who it thinks should be heard, must now go to a district court four miles away and ask it, in writing, with reasons, and the district court must consider it and answer it and minute it, and that is a day and a half of a lawyer's work and a clerk's, and it was done on the sixteenth and the seventeenth of the sixth month. **And the cost that nobody entered is the one that matters: for a day and a half the carriage was not offered, and the carriage is the only reason anybody has come to that yard since the second of the twelfth, and four women who had said they would come on the twenty-eighth of the fifth did not come on the twenty-eighth of the fifth, and nobody is going to go and find out what that cost them, and I am not going to be the one who does it either, because finding out would mean asking four women why they did not come, and they were owed nothing to explain.**

---

In the corridor afterwards, on the sixteenth, at about the seventh hour, the notary of this city asked Vaunt Oris, through the sheet, whether he was satisfied, and got an answer that took about forty seconds and is in the file.

"I am satisfied with the second answer and not with the first or the third," said Vaunt Oris, "and I will say why and it is not a complaint. Your first answer is that the order is served on no person. It is served on no person, and that has been true since the fourteenth of the fourth month, and my House has had two months of a document that binds nobody and has been behaving as though it bound everybody, and your answer is correct and my House has not noticed, and I have now told them, and I would be obliged if your court would understand that I am not asking it to do anything about that. My House is an institution and institutions are not told things by their notaries in the middle of an afternoon. They are told things in about four years, by somebody else, at more expense, and my question this month has cost my House nothing and your court one day of a lawyer's time, and the day of the lawyer is the whole of the price of this question, and I would like that entered, because I am aware that in about nine years a person will read that this House asked a question and will assume that it cost somebody something considerable."

"And the third," said the notary of this city.

"The third is correct and I am not satisfied with it, and that is not your court's fault and it is mine, and I will say it because your notary will not let it go past. Your court says an order that cannot be served does not bind a person who has not been heard. That is right. But it says nothing at all about an order that **can** be served, and about the day of the hearing, and about what a person is given on that day. My House has this in its procedure as a house practice and not as a law, and your court has it in an order of the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year, and the order says that no guarantor duty attaches to a person who has not been heard and has not signed, and I have read that order four times and it is the best sentence your court has ever written and it is worth more to a notary than any of the four hundred years of my House's practice, and it will not do one single thing about the order of the day.

"Your court publishes its minutes and I have read them, and I am a notary and I read minutes for a living, and what I have read is this: a person affected is to be heard in a room, in a place that recognises the room, and in nearly every one of those minutes the person is heard, and in a number of them the person is heard and the thing has already been made. There is a river court of yours four hundred and thirty miles up a river in which a person was heard properly and warned truly and defeated, and what defeated her was a document that was put in front of her before anybody asked her anything, and your own court has that in an order of the seventeenth of the sixth and it is the best sentence anybody in your city has written. So the order as it stands is a door with the hinges on the wrong side, and it lets a person in after the thing has already happened. I have been reading for four days and I have not found one court of yours that has ever said that in a form, and I am not able to put the word into a court and I have stopped being able to, and I am a notary of a House that cannot be compelled, and I am telling a court of human beings that my House is short one sentence, and has been short of it since the twelfth of the ninth month of last year, and did not know it until about eleven minutes ago."

The notary of this city wrote all of it down and read it back and sealed it, and then said one thing at the door of the room, and it is the last line of the sheet and it is in the file and it is the reason a magistrate of a district court four miles from here has been at a desk since about the fifth hour with a pen and a sheet of paper and has not got to the fourth hour of the afternoon.

"Then I will take it to the only person in this building who can put it in a minute book with a name at the foot of it," he said, "and I will not take it to her as a House's request. I will take it to her as a question, and the question is one line, and the line is whether a person affected is to be heard **before** the instrument takes effect upon them, and not after, and I do not know what she will say and I have been in this chair for twenty-six years and I would be prepared to put money on what she will say being *no*, and the reason I would put money on it is that she will think it is too much to ask of a district court in nine years, and she will be wrong, and I am going to ask her anyway."
