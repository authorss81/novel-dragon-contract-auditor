# Chapter 68: The Fourth Place

There are three places in this matter where a document has been found, and she has been to all three of them.

The first is the Forms Received rack in the passage behind the third gallery, which is unlocked, and which holds everything any office in the empire has sent the Chancery, indexed by the office that sent it and never by what it is. The second is a lane four hundred and thirty miles down a river, where a chalk board stands on the flat of a trestle and anybody may take the four questions off it and write their own name under. The third is another lane in Greyfen with a price painted in a window and nine names on a list inside it and a hand that writes small and fast.

And there was a fourth one the whole time, about a hundred yards from where she works, and it is a room with a press in it off the Crown Kiln stair, and nobody goes there to read anything, because it is the room where a thing is made ready to be sent somewhere else.

---

The answer from Sarn came down on the morning packet of the fifteenth of the second month in four lines, and it is four lines because Sarn has decided that anything longer would be a conversation.

> *I. Sarn. Thank you and no.*
>
> *Not because you are a stranger to this court. Because the fourth cycle of the sixth year was never copied, and so there is nothing to copy, and what you have offered to do is to write eighteen letters now, in the second month, in this year, in a cold room, with the day on them, in your hand. And in nine years a bundle of eighteen letters with a copyist's hand on them is a thing this court did in the second month of the eleventh year, and this court did not, and the whole of what is on my wall is that this court wrote to sixty households and kept nothing whatever.*
>
> *The press is coming off the third flagstone on the fourteenth and the bundles are going into the embankment corridor and the corridor is dry. That is the end of the press and it is not the end of anything else and I am not sorry about the press.*
>
> *Do not write again about this. You may write about anything else. — I.S.*

Tamsin Rook read it twice at the sill, and then she got out the sheet of foolscap where she keeps the things she is not going to be able to do, and wrote at the top of it in her own hand:

> *She is right and I did not see it, and the reason I did not see it is the reason on the second entry of the list. I wanted to do a thing and I had not asked what the thing was. I thought I was offering to copy. I was offering to write, and a copy is a thing about the past, and a thing I write in a cold room in the second month is a thing about the second month, and I would have made the fourth cycle the only one of the four that anybody could date, and I would have dated it wrong, and I would have dated it to a person from another building.*
>
> *The flue was cut on the fourteenth and the press is in the corridor and the eighteen are gone and there is nobody to be angry with. — Fifteenth of the second month.*

---

The plate went into the district court on the ninth of the second month, and a registrar said in a form with a name at the foot of it that a matrix is not a ledger, and the copyist who was in the room wrote it down and read it back and did not ask him a third question, which he noticed.

What she had in her own hand on the evening of the ninth was this:

> *Garren Pyle, of a room over a yard in the Kiln sheds, has held a press plate for eleven years. On the face of it, cut in steel, a filled square with a bar through it and a notch out of the top left corner, being the mark the court sitting in room four entered in its public minute book on the seventeenth of the sixth month and said it did not know the house of. On the edge, cut shallow, a number in a hand he cannot place.*
>
> *He says the number is not his. He has cut about nine hundred plates himself and his own numbers carry a letter in front of them and he knows every one. He says this number is out of the run the Notaries' Table uses for everything it sends and for everything it makes for a thing that is going to be sent, out of one counter, in one hand, for about sixty years, and he says that a number on a plate is a fact about the book the plate was entered in and is not a fact about the plate and is not a fact about the mark.*
>
> *Which I have now written down four times about four other things.*

And then, on the tenth, at about the fourth hour, she worked out where the book was.

There is exactly one book in this city with that run in it. Not one shelf — one book. The Table numbers everything that leaves the building out of one series, and everything it makes for a thing that is going to leave the building out of the same series, because a series with two counters is two books, and a person asked to find a number in a book in nine years will be told there are two.

And the book is not in the third gallery and not on any press and not on a shelf with a label. It is in the **press room off the Crown Kiln stair**, because a stub is not a record and a record belongs in a gallery, and a stub is a piece of cardboard with four lines on it that exists only to prove what went into a bag, and the room where a bag is made up is the room the stubs go in, and the stubs go in a press.

It is four floors and a landing below where she works and it took her nine minutes to find the door, and there is no reason in the empire for a copyist of the third gallery to be in it, and about four hundred people work in this building every day and not one of them has been in that room to read anything.

---

Getting a book out of a press room took eleven minutes, and it is the whole of what she has learned in eleven months, and it took exactly as long as the custody form.

She could not ask for it in a corridor, because a corridor is where a person says no to you. She could not ask for it in a letter, because a letter is a document and a document belongs to somebody. She asked for it in a room, in words, out loud, in front of somebody, which is the only way anybody in this matter has ever got anything out of anybody, and she asked a clerk of the Table with a name over the door and a man from the copying room standing beside her because she had asked him to stand there, which costs nothing and is the whole of a witness.

"May I look at the delivery-stub book for the ninth month of last year."

"For what."

"For a number."

The clerk said, "You are not of this office."

"No."

"Then I cannot let a person who is not of this office take a book off a shelf in this room, and I am going to say that out loud so that you have heard me say it, and I am going to get my supervisor, and he is not going to say no, and I would like you to understand that I have said the no first, because my supervisor will be asked about this afterwards and I would rather he were asked about the whole of it."

"That is the correct order," said Tamsin Rook, "and I would like it written down that you said it in that order."

The deputy of the Notaries' Table is a man of about forty and he is not Pell Vey, and he brought the book down off the top shelf himself with a stool, and put it on the bench, and stood there.

"Which number," he said.

She told him the number.

He opened it in about four seconds, because a sixty-year run in one hand at one counter is a thing a man finds with a thumb, and he found it on the eleventh leaf, and he put his own hand flat on the page beside it, and then took his hand off the page, which nobody had asked him to do.

"Four lines," he said. "That is a stub. Everybody in this room reads stubs every day and not one person in this room has ever read one. If you had asked me for the book I would have asked you for a file number, and there is no file number, because nothing that goes out of this room is ever a case."

"That is what I wanted," said Tamsin Rook.

---

The stub is a piece of good cardboard about five inches by three, and it has four lines on it in a hand of about sixty years, and the four lines are the whole of a thing that left a building:

> *One. Enclosed.*
> *Two. To whom, and where.*
> *Three. On whose instance it is sent.*
> *Four. By whose authority it is sent.*

The Table sends about a hundred and forty things a month, and every one of them has a stub, and every stub is kept, because the alternative is a building that cannot prove what it sent, and a building that cannot prove what it sent is a building that gets told about things it does not remember doing.

On the eleventh leaf, for the ninth month of last year, there is a stub, and the four lines read:

> *One. One sheet, sealed, with a direction.*
> *Two. To the clerk's window, Mosswake Roll Court, in the district of Mosswake. By the midweek packet.*
> *Three. On the instance of a matter in the third gallery.*
> *Four. P. Vey.*

There is no addressee's name at the bottom. There is no file number. There is no description of the sheet beyond *one sheet, sealed, with a direction*, and there is no office at the head of it except the four words that every stub in that book has carried since about the year of the fourth of the Ashfall line.

And the third line is *on the instance of a matter in the third gallery*, which is the form of words used when a thing goes out without a client's name on it, and it is used about nine times in thirty years in a building of four hundred people.

Tamsin Rook wrote the four lines out in a copyist's hand, standing up, with the book in place, and she wrote the leaf on the back of her own hand and not on anything of the Table's, which is the fourth time she has done that and the fourth time it has produced the only document in the matter.

Then she said it out loud, in a room, to a deputy and a clerk and a man from the copying room, because that is the only way a person finds out whether they have understood a thing or only written it down.

"The order on that board in Mosswake on the morning of the eleventh of the ninth month of last year was the third issue of a witness order. It is not in the register. The register has two. It was drawn in the copying room of that court after noon on the ninth and before it went up on the board, and a man had been at that witness bench at half past eleven on the ninth, and the order as it was posted had *Certificate of the Reed Cut district* struck into the first line, pressed deep into the fibre, with the widow's own line ghosted underneath it. And nobody was struck out. A piece of paper was put in front of her."

She put her hand flat on the bench, on the book, which is a thing she does, and the deputy of the Notaries' Table did not move his book.

"That happened in Mosswake, four hundred and thirty miles from here, on the ninth of the ninth month of last year, and this book has been a hundred yards from me for eleven months with a stub in it for the same day."

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds.

"Read line four again," said the deputy, in the flattest voice anybody has used in that room in forty years.

"By whose authority it is sent," said Tamsin Rook. "And the answer is a man's name. And the man's name is the name of the man who is on four documents in this matter, and he is your supervisor, and he is not in this room, and I have not told him that I have been in this room."

"Then before you do anything else," said the deputy, "you are going to tell me one thing, and I ask it because I have been in this room for eleven years. Did you come here for that stub, or did you come here for the plate?"

"I came here for the number," said Tamsin Rook.

"Then the plate was the plate and the number was the number and neither of them is a person, and you have done the correct thing in the correct order, and I want you to know that I have watched eleven years of people come in here and go straight to line three, which is the client's name, and take it, and every one of them took a name out of this building that did not belong to them."

---

She copied the four lines out twice more in the alcove, and then she did the thing the undertaking of the fourth of the first month requires, and which had not been tested until that afternoon.

It is a sheet going out of this matter. It is going to an office outside the Roll Hall, because the finding on it is that a supervisor's hand is in the fourth column of a stub in the Notaries' Table's own press room, and there is nowhere else it can go: the Docket of Powers cannot take it, Examinations cannot take it, and the district court has no jurisdiction over a series and could not get a book out of another district in any case.

So she took it up to the Received Room at about the sixth hour of the eighteenth of the second month and put it on the table in front of Marek Kest, and she did not put it in his hand.

"Read that before it goes," she said. "Read it in front of me. And if you have a reason against it, say the reason in a room and out loud and not on a page. And if you ever decide I am wrong, ask me for it now, before you act, and I will say no, and you will go and do it anyway, and we will both know exactly what that is."

Marek Kest read the four lines twice. It took him about a minute and a half, and he read them standing up, and then he read them a third time, which she had not asked for.

"I have a reason," he said, "and it is a good one, and I am going to give it to you in words, and then you are going to tell me I am right, and then it is going anyway. That is the arrangement. I want to say first that I know the arrangement is mine and not yours, and I put it in a book on the fourth of the first month at about the seventh hour and told you it was worse than the rule we made."

"Give the reason."

"A stub is a fact about a book and not about a man." He turned the sheet round so the fourth line was nearest her. "Line four is a column headed *by whose authority*. A person who signs a column headed *by whose authority* is not admitting authorship of a thing. He is discharging an office. Every sheet that has left that building in sixty years has a line four, and a great many of them have a supervisor's name in it, and a supervisor's name in that column means the supervisor is the person the *Table* acts through and not the person who decided. And if you take those four lines to anybody — to a court, to the court sitting in room four, to a room with a magistrate in it — then what you have put in front of that room is a man putting a document in front of a room, and the document says a name, and a document with a man's name at the top of it is a document about that man, and the room will do the only thing it can do with a document about a man, which is to hold a hearing about the man."

"Which is what you do not want."

"Which is what I do not want," said Marek Kest, "and which is also the only thing anybody can do about a man, and I have been telling myself for eleven months that those two things are different."

He put the sheet down.

"I still want it sent."

"I know you do. Send it. And I will send the first copy to the Docket of Powers, because the Docket has a bar on a person in it and this is the first thing in four months that is about who did a thing rather than about what a thing is. And I will send the second to Silas Sowle, for the household, because a household with a solicitor going into a room in the fourth month is a household whose solicitor is going to be asked in a corridor whether anybody is after anybody, and he will be able to say that something is and not who."

"Agreed," said Tamsin Rook. "Agreed, and read back, and I am writing the word *agreed* on it in my own hand with the day on it, because a rule that only exists in a room is a preference, and I worked that out down a river four hundred and thirty miles away and I am not going to be the one who forgets it in a building."

She wrote *agreed* on it and the day on it, and then she sat down on the end of his table, which she has done perhaps six times in two years.

And then she said the other thing, which was the thing she had gone into the press room to find out and had not expected to find, and which is the finding of the whole of the last five months and the reason the volume cannot be closed on the instrument it has been resting on.

"You asked me for a reason and I have given you one and you were right," she said, "and I want to put the other half on the table while we are both sitting at it, and the other half is this. We have spent eleven months on one sentence. *A person affected may be heard about it in a room, in a place that recognises the room.* That is the Cinder Court's first order. That is Sarn's slate. That is the second question on a board in a lane. That is a girl of eleven working out whose name it was on a line of a works note. That is the whole instrument. And it is a good instrument and it is the best thing anybody in this matter has produced, and it does not fit the thing we have just found."

"No," said Marek Kest. "It does not fit it at all."

"The widow was heard. She was in the room. She had a seat and a lawyer's husband on a wall and forty people, and she spoke, and her answer is in a minute book. And she was defeated, because a third issue of a witness order went up on a board with a certificate pressed into the first line, and when the record spoke, the record was a thing that had been read to her by a court and understood by her, and a woman who has understood a document is not in a position to argue with it. The person was heard. The record answered first."

Marek Kest did not say anything.

"That is the hole in the instrument," said Tamsin Rook, "and it is not a small hole, and it is not a hole anybody can paper over by saying *they will do it again*, because the whole finding of this matter for eleven months has been that nobody did it twice. It was done once, by a person doing his office, in a building four hundred miles away, with a four-line slip, and it worked perfectly, and it worked on a woman who had been warned, and she was warned by a man in a good coat, and the warning was right, and the warning did not help."

"May I say one thing."

"You may say anything in this room at any hour, you know that."

"The warning did not help because a person who has been warned about a document is a person who is now afraid of the document, and a person who is afraid of a document cannot get in front of it." He was looking at the wall of the light well out of the window that could not see anything. "Every thing we have built for eleven months is a rule about the *record*, and it is a very good rule, and a woman in a room is not a record. And the Cinder Court's answer in the last month came back and said, in its own hand, that it cannot answer a question about a record without a person to examine — and the reason it cannot is that there is nobody in it. And now we know what a person in it is for."

"Then write that down and I will copy it out four times, and it will go on a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river, and the man who reads it will be a clerk who is frightened of documents."

"Write it down," said Marek Kest. "But do not put my name on it."

"You cannot have a finding with nobody's name on it, you have said so yourself, in writing, in a form, twice."

"Then put my name on it and put the day on it, and put under my name the word *accountable* and not the word *right*, because I have been in this matter for eleven months and there is not one line of it that says I was right, and if it is going to be a document in nine years I would like it to be an honest one, and this is the only document I have ever put my name to that I am certain will not be held up in a room to prove that I was right about something."

She wrote it out, and the day on it, and *accountable*, and she read it back twice, and it is in a book of her own with no office on it, and it is a copyist's hand, and it is the only finding in this matter that anybody has ever asked to have less strong.
