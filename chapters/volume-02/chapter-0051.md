# Chapter 51: Nobody Can Get At You In Here

The Received Room is on the second floor of the Roll Hall and it has a barred door on the inside of the frame, which is not a lock and is not a bolt and is a bar, and it slides into two brackets that somebody screwed to the frame at some point in a decade when this building was being repaired badly and cheaply.

Marek Kest came up the stairs on the first morning of the ninth month with a hard biscuit in his coat because it was a habit and not because he was hungry, and he stopped on the landing, which is where a person stops, and looked at the bar, and then at the eleven feet of shelf, and then at the stove, which was cold, and understood that he had been given a room that is where things go when nobody wants to be the one who has them.

The intake clerk of the Chancery had said exactly that to him in the ninth month of last year, and had been talking about a corridor and a sealed exhibit and a man in a good coat, and Marek Kest had understood at the time that it was a description of somebody else. It took a room, a bar, and a stove to establish that it was also a description of him.

The table is a table. There is a stool. There is a pen, a blotter that has gone hard at the corners, and a lamp with the wick trimmed by somebody who was not going to be here long enough for it to matter. The window faces the inside of the building, which means it looks at the wall of the light well, which means a man at that window can be seen from the gallery and a man at that window cannot see out.

At about the seventh hour, when the third gallery had filled up and the noise had come up the stair the way it comes up a stair, somebody knocked on the frame of the barred door and then, when nothing happened, opened it, because the bar was on the inside and the man knocking was on the same side of it.

"You are going to want the bar," said the intake clerk.

"I have noticed the bar."

"You have noticed the bar for about four seconds and you have not decided anything about it yet, and when you do, it will be at about the fourth hour of the evening when you have been here ten hours and you would like a document out of a building and cannot get it." The clerk put a hand on the frame. "I am not going to tell you which way it goes. I am twenty-five and I have been at the intake desk long enough that the only thing I am qualified to tell you is where the ink is."

Marek Kest looked at him.

"You have written the time in the margin in ink, four times, for me."

"Four," said the intake clerk. "The fourth was on the twelfth of the eighth, at the counter, and you were standing at the counter, and it did not need a witness that day because there was a Registrar at it. I initialled it anyway."

"Thank you."

The intake clerk went slightly pink at the ear, said that it was nothing, and put a cup down on the sill with a small crash because he had not been told there was a cup, and then went back down the stairs at a speed that suggested he would think about it later.

---

By the third hour he had begun, without deciding to, to do what everybody does in the first week of a room like that, which is to read the shelf.

There is an order to it and it is not an accident. Things arrive in a room like this because a person has sent them somewhere else, and the somewhere else is this room, and the reason is on the label. There were nine bundles and a loose sheaf on the second shelf, and the labels said, in nine different hands and four different decades:

*Received, no office, no reply. — notarial exhibits, undated, three bundles.*

*Received from a district which has ceased to keep a register. Do not return. — district returns, five years.*

*Received, superseded, retained. — Standing Office series, samples, correspondence file.*

*Received wet. Do not press. — water office, one roll, assorted.*

And then four that were not bundles at all, and were the four that made the room what it was.

A slate with a line of chalk on it and nothing else. A man's coat with a list of nine things written inside the pocket and no name. A packet of nineteen return marks with no envelopes, tied with string, in three different hands. And a tray of letters with a card over it that said *answered, please* — not *replied*, not *to be answered*, but *answered, please* — in a hand so old and so hurried that the ink had gone through the card.

Marek Kest counted the bundles twice and got nine the same way, and wrote the list out in a column in his own book, because the room had no book of its own and he had not yet asked for one, and the column had a heading at the top of it that he took four seconds to choose and then chose badly on purpose: *what this building has decided not to chase.*

The nine were not chased. Four hundred and forty were chased, by somebody else, at some point, by a person with a tray or a lawyer, and came here instead, and are in a tray on the sill of the window that faces the light well, under a card, in a room with a bar on the inside of the door.

That is the actual function of the room. Not storage. It is the place a decision goes when the person who made it does not want to have made it, and a clerk who works here is the person who has them, and the difference between the clerk who works here and the clerk who does not is that the clerk who works here cannot be asked what is in the room, because anybody who wants to know would have to want it in front of the intake desk, and the intake desk writes the time in the margin in ink.

He had been transferred to a counter, and a counter is a place a document comes to and not a place a person goes to ask for one. It was not a punishment, whatever the transfer said, and the transfer said only a date and a name, and it had been made in about four minutes by a man who had given the harder box to the man who asked for it.

He had been an Inheritance Desk clerk for four years. In the ninth month of last year a man in a good coat had come up the quay past the Crown Kiln stair and asked, at a counter, whether a decision had reached a person he had never met, and the customs trade does not ask, and the man in the good coat had left with nothing, and a customs clerk who owed him nothing had told him what had happened to a widow's winter for nothing.

Now Marek Kest was the counter. Not the desk. The counter, in a room with a bar on it, where a person brings a thing they do not want.

---

The bundle is at the back of the second shelf.

It is on its end, in the kind of wrapper a branch archive wraps a thing in when it is going somewhere and the somewhere is a different building, and the scorch along one edge has gone brown and has been gone over with a hand at some point, badly, by somebody who was not thinking about it. The label is written twice, once by whoever packed it and once by whoever received it, and the two hands disagree about the number in one of them, and the receiving clerk wrote *four* and underlined it.

*Ashfall branch, fourth shelf, uncollated. Received the ninth month of the year twelve years back. Damaged, four items, uncollated.*

It is four feet from Marek Kest's own table. It has been four feet from him since the first of the ninth month, which is the first working morning of his employment in the room, and it will be four feet from him on every working morning for as long as he is in the building, and there is no rule about it. Nobody wrote a rule about it. Nobody ever wrote a rule about it, which is the reason nobody has opened it.

Eleven first clerks of the year have looked at it, and one of them is him.

On the collated edge there is a red thread. It goes through two places. It goes through the wrapper and it goes through the leaf beneath the wrapper, and the leaf beneath the wrapper is not from this year. It is a stitch. It is the thing a copyist does when she is working through a bundle of thirty pages looking for one passage and wants to come back to it.

He had written that down on the twenty-second of the eighth month and had put it on the file, with the date, and the file had gone to a man he had chosen.

He looked at it for about a minute on the first morning and then he did something he had never done with a document in four years, which was to go and get a fresh leaf out of the drawer at the side of the table and write about it again, on the first morning, in the room, and put the date on it, and read it back.

> *The first day in this room, the first of the ninth month. I have not opened it and I am not going to, and I want the reason on this sheet in a form I can read at about the seventh hour on any morning of the next year, because I am now going to be four feet from it every morning and a man who has decided once not to look at a thing has to have a reason every morning or the decision is a mood.*
>
> *The reason is not the rule about the two red facts. The rule of the eighteenth of the ninth month of last year stands and I have not broken it and I am not going to. The reason is narrower and it is about me. A working stitch marks a passage a person was working on. It does not tell me who was right, it does not tell me when, and it does not tell me anything at all. I have been treating this shelf as a place where an answer is lying about, and it is not, and if I open it in a week or a year or nine years because I have got tired of being the sort of man who does not open things, then I will have opened it for my own reasons and I will have put whatever I find into eleven months of somebody else's work.*
>
> *If it is ever opened, it is opened by a person who has no file, on a day with no argument in it, in a room with two other people in it, and the fact of who they were goes on the wrapper. I do not have such a day. I have this room, which is a room for things nobody wants.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the first of the ninth month.*

That took about nine minutes and it was the first work he did in the building in four years that nobody had asked him for.

---

The decision came on the ninth of the ninth month, on a morning packet, in a single page with a seal on the corner and a file number in ink that he recognised as the number the intake clerk had written on Tamsin Rook's copy in the ninth month of last year and initialled.

> *Received and consolidated. The request of the eighteenth of the ninth month and the supplement of the eighth of the eleventh month are received and are consolidated into one matter, and are accepted as a **limited administrative inquiry into the filing practice of the district of Mosswake**, to be conducted by the applicant in his own time and at his own wage.*
>
> *The scope of the inquiry is: the filing, entering, and itemising of filings made under flood authority in that district between the first month of the sixth year and the first month of this year. The applicant may compare any filing in that district against any other filing in that district, and against the district's own standing instructions, and may enter what he finds in a book of his own.**
>
> *This office has no power under this acceptance to compel any document, any office, any witness, or any return, and none is to be understood as given. This office has no power under this acceptance to touch, and this acceptance does not touch, any numbered series, any standing instruction of any district, or any return made under any instrument sealed with the fire-and-water device. The applicant is not an officer of this office and nothing in this acceptance makes him one.*
>
> *The acceptance is dated the ninth of the ninth month.*
>
> *N. Trost, examiner, Examinations.*
>
> *And beneath that, in the same hand and not in a different one, four lines, and the reason for the four lines is that Trost is the man who wrote them and not a clerk above him:*
>
> *I have put my name to this because I was told to put my name to it and because I have learned since the eighteenth of the ninth month of last year that a decision with nobody's name at the foot of it is worth a fifth of what a decision with a name at the foot of it is worth, and because I would like somebody to be able to say in four years that I wrote it. I did not want to write it. It was put to me as a form of words and the form of words had a decision inside it, which is the thing I have been warning this applicant's supervisor about since the eleventh month of last year in nine words he has never been given.*

Marek Kest read it three times and then went and stood at the window that looked at the wall of the light well.

It was an acceptance. In eleven months he had had a sealed scope decision with no name on it, an offer withdrawn, two refusals entered and signed, a referral that had gone up a river in another court's name, and a high court that had taken one of his four questions and refused three in a shape that was worth something. And what had come back this morning was the first acceptance of anything he had ever filed, and it was dated, and it had a man's name on it, and it could not compel a single person in the empire to do a single thing.

He read the second paragraph again. The filing, entering, and itemising of filings made under flood authority in one district, over five years. That was the practice. That was the whole of what a Chancery officer is fit to inquire into, because a Chancery officer has no officer for a series, and so a request about a series becomes a request about a district, and a request about a district can be granted, and granting it is not a small version of the thing asked for. It is a different thing wearing the same word.

And the thing asked for had been, in nine paragraphs, dated the eighteenth of the ninth month, the sale of a third witness mark and the supply of pressed matrices. It was now an inquiry into how one district filed its own paperwork.

He answered it the same afternoon, in one paragraph, in his own hand, and it is the only thing he has written in four years that nobody asked him to write.

> *Received. I accept the inquiry and I will conduct it in my own time and at my own wage and I will enter what I find in a book of my own as the acceptance provides.*
>
> *Two things and no others. One: that the scope be fixed on this page, and not in a book that may be fuller than this page. Two: that the name of the person conducting it be on this page as well as mine.*
>
> *I do not ask for the series. I have asked for it four times and it is a standing office's paper with a run of nine hundred and forty and no person at the top of it, and I have understood since the sixth of the sixth month of last year that asking for it is asking for a name, and a man who asks a court for a name four times has stopped asking and started wanting one.*
>
> *And at the foot, in my own hand, because he wrote his reason under his and a man who writes a reason under a decision deserves to be answered in the same room: I have read your four lines. You have answered the question I put to the Registrar on the eighteenth of the ninth month of last year, which was whether this office has a person in it. It has one, and he wrote four lines under a decision he did not want, and I have his name.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the ninth of the ninth month.*

Then he put the pen in the stand and sat on the stool in a room with a barred door and a cold stove and a bundle on the second shelf, and the thing he was aware of, sitting there, was that it was the first working morning in four years in which nobody in the Roll Hall had wanted anything from him.

It was a good feeling. He noticed it and then, about four seconds later, noticed the shape of it, and the shape of it was exactly the shape of the thing Sarn had written on a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river in the seventh month of last year, in the first person, about herself: that she would rather be frightening and indispensable at a window with a nail in it than tidy and ignored.

Marek Kest was twenty-nine years old and had spent eleven months finding out that a document which says on its face that no person is behind it is unanswerable in a way that an untruth is not, and that the only thing a person can do with an untruth is prove it, and there is nothing there to prove.

Nobody could get at him in here. That was the first week in four years he would not be asked for anything, and he had to decide, in a cold room, with a hard biscuit in his coat, what he was for.

At about the fourth hour he unbarred the door, because he had decided that at the fourth hour he would be here, and the fourth hour is when the intake clerk came up with a second cup he had not been told about, and put down a square of paper with a form on it that had four lines in it and two boxes, and a covering line in a different hand that said *required of the person holding the public copies in this matter during an inquiry, by the Docket of Powers*.
