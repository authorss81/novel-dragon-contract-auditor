# Chapter 90: Open to Challenge

The notice went out on the second of the eighth month from the office of a solicitor of about fifty-five who has done this district's poor business for nineteen years and says on the record that he is not good at it, and it went to the district works and to the district court and to the household, and it carried seven days, and the seven days were given by him and not by the court, because the court's part of it is still a refusal with reasons and a date in it from the third of the fourth month, and a minute of the twenty-first of the fifth month in which it fixed no date at all and entered that it could not.

The date was the ninth of the eighth month, at the second hour, in the chamber of the district court of Auremar's lower district.

She ordered the day before she wrote the order, and it took her about four minutes on the morning of the eighth, and it is in the day's book in her own hand, at the bottom, under a line she drew, and there is no form in this empire for a magistrate to do that and no heading in any fund for what it cost, and it is the only part of this case that anybody outside this building will ever hear of.

She wrote: *the person affected is addressed before any paper is read into the room, and if a paper is put in front of her it is put after she has spoken and not before, and the magistrate will say out loud what is about to be read and who wrote it before it is read, and if the person cannot read a paragraph the reading is to be by a person of her choosing in the room and not by an officer of this court.*

She read it twice, and then she sent for Hester Crane and told her why in about nine words, in a message carried by a clerk and not an officer, and the message said the whole of it including the words *before any paper is read*, and Hester Crane sent back one line in a copyist's hand and not her own, and the line said: *my granddaughter reads. Tell them she reads it and not you.*

---

At about the fifth hour of the first of the eighth month, the magistrate walked four hundred yards and stood in a lean-to at the head of a crossing and told a woman of about seventy that the court had written a line about her with no name in it, and that it was true, and that it did nothing for her, and that the bill at the other end of that bar is still nineteen pounds three and fourpence and is still not being paid by anybody.

The woman asked her one question, which was whether anybody had been paid for the saying of it, and the magistrate said no, and the woman said, *then it was worth doing and I will tell you why it was worth doing, and then you can go and write it down and get wet again* — and then she said it, and it took about four minutes, and none of it is written down because it belongs to her and the court did not take it and did not ask to.

---

At the second hour on the ninth of the eighth month, the chamber held about thirty people and the front bench was empty, which Joane Tranter had arranged in writing the day before, and which nobody in the room except three people understood.

Silas Sowle, for the household. Hester Crane, sixty-one, with Rosamund Crane, eleven, beside her, and with a chair in the front row that she did not sit in for the first nine minutes and then did. Jonas Cray, works clerk of about thirty, in a good coat, in the fourth bench. Gideon Harrow, district engineer, about fifty, nineteen years, a hundred and forty returns a quarter, who put his office's answer on the table and would not read it, because it is his office's paper and not his. The officer at the door with his five columns. A clerk. About nine people off the far side of the water, who are not parties and came because the crossing is theirs.

And nobody from a seat's device, because a device does not come to a district court, and its notice of the tenth of the fourth month asked that the matters touching the instrument of nineteen years back be held, and they are held, and this court will say so in four lines and then go on.

The magistrate read her own order of the day out loud, in about forty seconds, and then she looked at the front row and said:

"Mrs Crane. You are the person this is about and you are the only person in this room it is about, and nothing has been read. If anybody in this room puts a piece of paper in front of you before you have said what you came to say, I will stop them, and if I do not stop them you are to stand up and walk out and it will be entered that you walked out. Do you want to say anything now."

"I do not read a paragraph," said Hester Crane.

"I know. It is written down and it is in the file and it has been in the file since the ninth month of last year. Is there anything you want to say now, and it can be nothing, and nothing is an answer and I will enter it as one."

"There is a thing I want to say and it is not about the money," said Hester Crane, "and then I will sit down and let you get on, because I have been in a room in this city once in nineteen years and it was this one and I do not want to be here all morning."

"Say the thing."

"A word keeps coming to my gate." She was not looking at anybody. "I do not know who sends it. It is a word and not a person, and it has been coming since about the ninth month of last year and before that, and it comes on a paper with a date on it, and there is nobody at my gate to give it to because the parish it belonged to went in the sixth year. So it comes to me." She stopped for a moment. "And every time it comes my granddaughter reads it to me and I answer it out loud and nothing is signed, because I have signed one thing in sixty-one years and I would like it to stay at that. It has come eleven times. Nine times I said I would see. Twice I said no. I want all three of those written down and I want the nine in with the two, because the nine are the ones I am ashamed of, and I did not know there was going to be a no until the fourth month of this year and before that I said yes to a thing I could not read because a girl of eleven read it to me in my own kitchen."

Nobody wrote that down. The clerk wrote it down. It is in the minute of the ninth of the eighth month at about the ninth line, and it is the ninth line and nothing was argued about it, and the finding of this case in nine years that anybody will read is not the ninth line but the second of the three, and the second of the three is the one that says a person who is afraid of a document cannot get in front of it, and it is the only one of the three that anybody is going to try to get rid of.

---

Then the papers came in, in the order the magistrate put them in, and she said out loud what each one was and who had written it before it was read, which she has never done in nine years, and which is the single ordinary thing in this matter that is going to change anything anywhere.

The works' answer went in first. Harrow put it down and did not read it and said so in about nine words, and the magistrate read it, and it is four lines long and it has not changed in four months.

*That the fund of this office for the quarter cannot stand the cost of the bank, abutment and kerb works at the crossing at Lowcross, estimated at nineteen pounds three and fourpence. That on the nineteenth of the twelfth month of last year this office asked this district in writing to stand the cost, and the request has not been answered. That this court is not the district and a district court may not direct a district to stand a cost. That this office will not certify, because certifying is a promise to inspect, and the crossing is open and has been since the twenty-fourth of the twelfth, on the labour of nine unpaid persons of whom one is an officer of this office.*

"Entered as filed and not as a concession," said the magistrate, "and the clerk will enter, on my motion, that the answer is in the same terms as the answer of the third of the fourth month and that this is the fifth such paper that office has filed in this district in two years and that the first of them was the first paper of that kind this court has had in nine years."

Then the minute of the third of the fourth month went in, and the notice of the fourteenth of the fourth month, and the entry of the twenty-sixth of the fifth month about a respondent that cannot be served, and the minute of the twenty-first of the fifth month that a court cannot tell a refusal from an absence, and the minute of the twenty-eighth of the seventh month about a bar and a plank and a broken padlock, and the minute of the second of the seventh month about an examination of a mark. Six documents after the answer, seven in all, read aloud and described as they went in, and the room of about thirty people heard the whole of them in about twenty-two minutes, and the person they were about was in the room throughout and was not asked a single question about any of them.

Then the solicitor stood up.

---

Silas Sowle said his piece in about nine minutes and it was not good and he knew it and said so, and then he did the one thing in nineteen years he has told her he would do and nobody had believed him.

He put his hand in his coat and brought out a form, and it was the returned form of the ninth of the ninth month of last year, and it was the form Hester Crane had signed in a yard on a box headed *the present holder*, and it had come back nine weeks later on a post with a note on the reverse, and he has had it in that coat since the second of the twelfth month, and he has read it in a chamber to nobody.

"Nineteen words on the back of this form," he said, "and nobody has ever read them out in a room, and I have had them in a coat for four months and I have read them in a chamber to myself about forty times, and I am not a lawyer of any ability and I have lost two applications in this district in four months and won nothing, and I am going to read them out because the person they are about is sitting nine feet from me and cannot read them."

*The form is returned because the form cannot be entered and not because the work is refused.*

He read it twice. The room went quiet in the way a room does when everybody in it has understood the same thing at the same moment.

"That is the whole of this case," said Silas Sowle, "and it has been the whole of this case for four months, and every person in this room has spent four months on nineteen pounds three and fourpence, and the bill is not the bill, and the bill has never been the bill, and what the form wanted was a decision and a decision needs a person to decide and there has not been one in this district since a parish was dissolved in the sixth year and nobody in Lowcross knew, because the notice went to the parish."

"Seventeen," said Joane Tranter.

"That is what I have always called it."

"The clerk will enter seventeen, because I have spent nine years counting a bar up and down four times a week and I do not have to be good at anything else to count a sentence. And let the file go on calling it nineteen, because a file with one figure wrong in it for four months is a thing I have got about nine of, and it is not worth a morning."

"I counted it every time for four months," said Silas Sowle, "and I never wrote it down once, and that is very nearly the whole of what is wrong with this case."

"What do you ask this court for," said Joane Tranter.

"I ask for one thing and it is the smallest thing I have ever asked anybody for and I have been ashamed of it for four months," said Sowle. "**I ask that it be found that a person affected may be heard about an instrument in a room, in a place that recognises the room, before it binds anybody.** And I do not ask that the court decide the words on the back of the form, because the court cannot, and I do not ask for a penny, and I do not ask you to make the crossing a work of the district, because you have told me in writing you cannot, and I have read that paper back to the room, and you could not do it if you tried, and a court that tried would be doing something worse than a court that says no."

---

She refused everything else first, in about six minutes, and all of it correctly, and the refusals are longer than the order and there is no hurry in them, because a court that has nothing to give should not hurry.

She refused to direct the district to stand the cost, and said the court has no power to do it and did not have it in the fourth month and does not have it now.

She refused to make a finding on the construction of the instrument of nineteen years back, and said that a seat's notice asked that no court of lower jurisdiction make one, that she cannot refuse the notice because it is a form her own court published, and that she is not going to get around her own form by finding something else instead.

She refused to order a continuance, and gave reasons, and gave a date, and the refusal is correct: *the works may apply on a date to be fixed on seven days' notice; the household may apply on a date to be fixed on seven days' notice; the office of the First Seat may apply in the form it has used, and this court will hear it, and the court makes no order touching the instrument of nineteen years back.*

And then she made two findings, and the second one is the reason this matter does not end on a bridge.

---

> *I. It is found that a precedent may be challenged in this matter and in any matter in which a person is affected by it, and that the challenge is not that the precedent is void and is not that any person is not liable, and nothing in this finding is a finding that any record has been altered or that any person has done wrong. It is found that where a person is affected by an instrument or by a form of words in general use, that person may be heard about it in a room, in a place that recognises the room, and that the person may be heard before the instrument takes effect upon them, and not only afterwards.*
>
> *II. It is found that the order of the court sitting in room four of the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year, being that no guarantor duty attaches to a person who has not been heard and has not signed, does not by itself provide that a person is heard before the instrument takes effect upon them. A person who has been warned about a document is afraid of that document, and a person who is afraid of a document cannot get in front of it. That sentence is entered now, in this court, with my name at the foot of it, in the plainest words I can find, because in fourteen months the finding that a person may be heard has been in four orders and a form and a sentence on a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river, and nobody has ever yet written down the half of it that makes the first half work.*
>
> *III. It is found that the first of these two findings binds no person who has not been heard, and lifts nothing, and makes no person liable for anything, and that it does not put a line in the fund of the district works of Auremar's lower district, and that the crossing at Lowcross is open and that no person is liable for it and that the bill on it is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and remains unpaid.*

Then she read all of it back, and then she said the thing that is the cost of it, out loud, in the room, because she is a court and not an ally and has been for nine years.

"Now I am going to say what that finding costs, and I am saying it in a room and not in a file, because in nine years the file will say the finding and not the cost, and the finding is the good half.

"**Every precedent in this empire is now open to challenge.** Every district court that has taken a recognition from the court sitting in room four, and every landing office, and every office that has applied a sentence in letters to a household without hearing it, and every house that has relied on a printed model because a printed model is a rule and a rule is safe — all of it is now open, and the people who are safest are the people who were relying on a bad one and will find that out late. About four hundred and forty people in four offices and one trade are a very little safer this morning than they were yesterday. **And every precedent that is right is now open to challenge as well, and that is the price and there is no way to charge it to anybody, and I am the one who has to say so out loud because I am the one who made it.**"

Nobody argued with her, which she expected, and she put it in the minute in her own words, and the minute is what will be read.

---

It was over at about the eleventh hour, and it is the fourth sentence this case has produced that is better than it looks and that pays nobody, and Tamsin Rook counted them that evening at the end of her own bench with the day on it.

> *The ninth of the eighth month, at about the eighth hour in the evening, in the alcove.*
>
> *Four. The third of the fourth month, when a magistrate entered a question asked and answered and not a finding. The second of the seventh, about a die being a mirror, which is not a sentence anybody will read and changed a thing anyway. The twenty-eighth of the seventh, about a plank. And this morning, in a room, with a name at the foot of it: that a person may be heard before the instrument takes effect upon them.*
>
> *Four in fourteen months and not one of them has paid anybody. One has cost a die, a day of work and one pound fourpence out of a fund. One has put a line in a file about a woman of about seventy with no name in it and she will never know it is there. One has made about four hundred and forty people in four offices a very little safer. And this morning's is the best of the four, it pays nothing, and it will be read in nine years by somebody who does not know what it cost, and that is what a sentence is.*
>
> *I am not going to be able to make the next one. — T. Rook.*

---

In the yard, at about the half past four, Hester Crane waited nine minutes and then asked one question at the gate, and it was not about the money and it was not about the word.

"Is my name on it."

"No," said Joane Tranter. "There is no name in it. Not yours and not the crossing's and not anybody's, and the finding will be copied and sold at a shilling and there is a name at the foot of it and it is mine, and the finding says a person affected may be heard before an instrument takes effect upon them, and you are a person affected, and you were heard at about half past the second hour of this morning, before anything was read, and your granddaughter read four things to you and you answered them, and that is in the minute and you cannot read the minute and I am going to tell you what is in it now, at arm's length, in the yard, in about four minutes, and then I am going to go and stand at the other end of that bar for a while."

She told her. It took four minutes and it was in the plainest words she had.

And then the last thing, and it is in the minute because it is the last thing, and the court of Auremar's lower district is not a place where a person is left with an impression.

"Now the other half, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. You came for the nineteen pounds three and fourpence and I have not given you the nineteen pounds three and fourpence. Nobody has. The crossing is open and nobody is liable for it and it will be open in the ninth month and the twelfth, and the bar goes up at the end of the day and the chain goes on the gate, and the padlock is broken and nobody has mended it and nobody is going to, and in nine years somebody will write a sentence about the hinges on the wrong side of a door and you will not be in the room when they do it. I have given you a sentence and a sentence is not money. I have been a magistrate for nine years and I have never once given anybody money and I am not going to start by pretending I have."

"Yes," said Hester Crane. "I did not think you were. I have not had a penny off anybody in nineteen years and I did not think it was going to be different this morning because a man read a bit of paper out loud."

She got her coat. She said at the gate, to nobody:

"He read it out. Nobody ever read it out."

And then she said the other thing, and it is the ninth of the eighth month and she is sixty-one and she does not know how old she will be when and neither does the file.

"And I did not have to be brave," she said. "Nobody made me say anything I did not already think. There was a chair and it was empty and the woman told me it would be empty. That is the whole of it and I have been frightened of this room since the ninth month of last year and I have not been frightened of it today, and I would like whoever writes the fifth sentence to write down that the fourth one was worth four minutes of a magistrate's time in the rain and a solicitor's coat, and I would like it written down that the bill is still not paid, because a person who cannot read a paragraph is the last person in the empire anybody will think of when there is a bill."

---

A clerk of the district court came down from the city on the tenth of the eighth month with a question and she answered it in a corridor in about twenty seconds and the question was the one everybody in this case has been asked four times since the sixth of the third month and which nobody has ever been able to answer in a form.

"Ma'am. Has anything come of the eighteenth of the seventh."

"Nothing that is before me," said Joane Tranter, "and I am not going to tell you what I think, and I am not going to guess at it in a minute book. **That is another matter, in another room, in another building, and I am not in it and I have not been told, and there is no form in this empire for a magistrate of a district court to be told about a sitting in a court above her.** If you want what came of it you will have to ask the court that sat, and if they will not tell you then nobody in this city will, and I have been a court for nine years and I have learned that the worst thing about an answer is that it is somebody else's question."

The clerk wrote that down and read it back and it went in the day's book, and it is the fourth time anybody in this matter has asked about a date and the fourth time the answer has been that nobody in this room knows anything, and the reason is on the form and the form is correct, and it is correct for the reason a man in a press room off the Crown Kiln stair gave in a corridor in the second month of last year: a refusal with a name at the foot of it can be read in nine years by somebody who has never met either of the people, and an answer given in a room cannot.
