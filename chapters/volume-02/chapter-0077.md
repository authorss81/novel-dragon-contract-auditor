# Chapter 77: A Document About a Seal

The limited audit was conducted in the third week of the third month, in the Received Room, in two afternoons, and it is worth putting down in one paragraph what it came to, because everything else in the fourth month would be harder to follow if a reader did not know.

The grant permitted one room in this city and fourteen days and a person. It permitted a document produced of its own motion and nothing else. So Marek Kest sat in a room with a barred door and a stove and read two lines out of a register, being the entries of the fourteenth of the ninth month in Tamsin Rook's hand, and compared them with two lines out of the register in front of him, and Oswin Marrow came in at about the fourth hour of the second afternoon, unasked, and stood at the end of the table, and said that he had come because the room was in his building.

The audit produced one sentence. The sentence is: that the two entries in the register describe two acts done on two dates by a person who was not entered, and that the entries agree with each other, and that no document of any kind was produced by anybody because nobody was asked, and that the finding of the Docket is a finding about the person and the two lines say nothing that is not in the finding.

He wrote it out. He did not enter it anywhere, because the grant said a finding made under it may be used in no proceeding in any court other than the one that granted it, and there was no proceeding, and he was not going to be a man who made a proceeding to put a sentence into.

Oswin Marrow read it standing up and said, "That is correct, and it is one line, and it would fit on the back of a form, and I have had a room of my own for twenty-two years and I could not have made it any shorter."

He did not send it to the court that granted the audit, and the reason is in his own book with the day on it, and the reason is that a court that receives a finding in a proceeding nobody brought has a proceeding.

---

The thing that came on the tenth of the fourth month came to the great stair of the Crown Kiln and not to a room, because a thing with a seal on it is carried up a stair and not down a corridor, and the man who brought it up was wearing the coat for it.

It is a notice of a seat. It is one side of a sheet of good paper and it is not printed, which is the first thing anybody in that building noticed about it, because everything that has come up that stair for thirty years has been printed.

There is a device at the head of it. The device is the fire-and-water mark, the one that is on a scale instrument, and it is the same mark that is on the instrument of nineteen years back at the crossing at Lowcross, and the one that is on the instruction sealed in the matter of the rehearing in the district of Mosswake, and the registrar said the three out loud in that order in about four seconds and then said, "And I am not going to say any more about the three, and I would be obliged if nobody in this room said anything else about them either, because there is a form in this court about a device and about three, and I read it in full in open court in the sixth month of last year and it took seventeen minutes, and everybody in this building was in the room, and I am not going to read it again and I am going to apply it."

What the notice says is this, and it is set out in six numbered lines and a foot.

> *Notice of a seat, given in a matter touching an instrument sealed with the fire-and-water device of nineteen years back, being the instrument under which the crossing at Lowcross is held, and touching a proceeding in the district court of the lower district of this city.*
>
> *One. This seat is entered in this matter and in that matter by its device. It is entered as a respondent.*
>
> *Two. No person of or belonging to this seat is party, cited, or examinable. No inference of any kind may be drawn from the fact that a person has not been named.*
>
> *Three. This seat does not contend that the crossing at Lowcross is the work of a holder. This seat does not contend that it is the work of a district. This seat has no position upon the question and states that it has none.*
>
> *Four. The construction of an instrument sealed with the fire-and-water device is a question for a court of the jurisdiction that may address it, and this seat asks that no court of lower jurisdiction make a finding upon the construction of that instrument, and that the matters touching it be held until such a court has addressed it.*
>
> *Five. This seat will pay the carriage of any person whom the district court of the lower district thinks should be heard, and will pay it without asking to know why, and will pay it twice if the person comes twice.*
>
> *Six. This seat is the First Seat. The First Seat holds the old authority to call a succession convocation. That authority is stated here so that no person shall be in doubt that the notice is made by a seat and not by an individual, and it is stated without any claim upon the matter and without any threat.*
>
> *Given under the device of the seat, and sealed, and signed.*
>
> *Seryn Oris, First Seat.*

She is named, for the first time, in the whole of this book, on a sheet of good paper at the foot of a document, with a seal above her name.

One thing about that name, and it is in this chapter and it is not in her.

She is not the author of anything anybody in this city has been reading for fourteen months. The sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month is the work of a standing office of the Chancery and it is on the ordinary distribution to forty-six addresses and the office that prints it has no person at the top of it, and the registrar in this room said out loud on the tenth that there is no series and no run and no number in the notice in front of us, and that is not an accident of drafting, it is the whole of the case.

She is not in the room. She is not anywhere in this matter. She has not written a word of anything anybody in Auremar has been reading for fourteen months, and there is not one line on any of it that is hers. She did not put the substituted words into a return in the sixth year, and she did not put a sentence into a book, and she did not arrange for a certificate to be pressed into the first line of a witness order four hundred and thirty miles up a river, and nobody has proved otherwise and nobody in this book ever will. What she has done is what a seat's administrative wing has been doing for a hundred and forty years: it has entered itself, by its device, in a matter touching a scale instrument, and it has asked a court to hold a question, and it has offered to pay for anybody anybody thinks should be heard, without asking why, twice.

The registrar set the sheet down and squared it, which nobody in that room noticed except the applicant.

---

Marek Kest was not a party to the matter at Lowcross. That was the first thing and it was the thing that stopped him for about nine minutes.

He had never been a party to it. He had been a man in a room at a hearing in the twelfth month who argued seven paragraphs of which four were about a printed sentence, and had been stopped in four words by a solicitor, and had withdrawn the submission that the sentence entitled the application to be granted and had not withdrawn the observation, and both of those are in the minute, and both of those are in a district court four miles from this building and not in this one.

"You are not a party," said the registrar.

"I am not."

"You appear in the matter of the instruction sealed with the device, which is a different matter, and this seat has entered itself in that matter by the same device, and the two are now in the same room on the same paper because a seat wrote six lines and the sixth one is the name of a seat and the name of a seat is a thing two rooms can be in at once."

"Yes."

"Are you going to object."

"Yes."

"Then I will tell you what happens, because I did it in the sixth month of last year and I did it to a judge and it took four minutes." The registrar put a hand flat on the sheet. "You will be heard for about nine minutes. The objection will be entered verbatim and read back to you. She will decide against you because she decided against you in the sixth month of last year on a motion of her own, and she will say why in a room afterwards and it will cost her something to say it, and she will be right, and you will be wrong, and both of those will be on the page. That is what the form gives. The form gives a person the right to be heard and gives him nothing whatever to be heard *with*."

Then he said, in the flattest voice anybody has used in that room in about sixty years:

"And I will tell you the other thing, because you have been in this building fourteen months and you have earned it. Two nights ago a person asked me in this corridor whether this notice has anything to do with the sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month. Not a seat's officer. A person with a tray, who is not in this room now and whose name I am not going to say in this room."

"And what did you say?"

"I said: search it. Read it four times, the way you read things. There is no series in it. There is no run, there is no number, there is no sheet, there is no office, there is no seal but a seat's, and it mentions one instrument and it is the instrument at Lowcross, which is nineteen years old and is in a file four miles from here with a hole in it. It mentions a district court and a crossing and a carter." He looked at the sheet. "That is why it is the most dangerous piece of paper that has come up this stair in fourteen months, and it is dangerous because the whole of the last four months has been about a series and a run and a sheet with nine hundred and forty copies of it on the ordinary distribution to forty-six addresses, and this document has not got a series in it and it is still going to hold up a bridge."

---

He was heard for about nine minutes and he used them badly, and he has written that down in his own book and would not have anybody else write it.

He made three arguments. The first was that a seat which enters itself by a device has connected two matters that no instrument joins, and that the connection is the whole of what this case has been about, and that the connection is made by a mark and not by a document. The registrar entered that.

The second was that the Fifth line is an offer to pay the carriage of anybody the district court thinks should be heard, and that a payment to a witness made by a party is a thing that is asked about in rooms, and that an offer made without asking why and twice is an offer that makes a person think. He said that, and the registrar entered it, and read it back, and Marek Kest said, "Read the second part of that back again," and it was read again.

The third argument was the only one that was any use and it was eight words long.

"The document is about a seal."

---

She decided against him in about four minutes and did not give a reason on the bench, and the registrar entered the objection verbatim, which is four lines and a half, and read it back to him, and Marek Kest asked for it to be read a second time, which is the practice of a man who was taught it by an examiner in a corridor in the eleventh month of last year and who has watched it become a habit in a building where the person who taught it has gone to a room with a barred door in it.

It is the second time an objection of his has been entered in a room in this matter. The first was the seventeenth of the sixth month of last year, and it was to the escalation, and it was entered and he lost it, and the court told him in a room that a thing which is not on a list is in a drawer. This one is about a different thing, and about a different institution, and about a mark cut in a thing, and it is entered in the minute book of a court that has read its own form aloud in open court once in sixty years and applied it to a dragon seat.

---

She said the reason in a room afterwards, in a corridor, standing, with a clerk in the room because a clerk was in the room, and she was not sorry and she was not going to pretend to be, and it took about eleven minutes.

"I did not do this to you," said Sela Vint. "I want that said first and I want it said in a corridor where there are no forms in it. A seat wrote six lines and asked me to hold a question about a mark cut in a thing from nineteen years ago, and the honest answer is yes, and there is a form in this building that says I have to give it, and the form is one I published in the reign of the third of the Ashfall line and read out in full in this room in the sixth month of last year because a man asked four questions and I took one."

"You could refuse it."

"I could, and I would have to write down why, and there is no why, and a judge who refuses a form that her own court published has to invent a reason, and an invented reason in a minute book of this court is read in nine years by somebody who has never met me and who will not be able to tell it from a real one." She turned round. "That is the whole of it. It is not that the seat is right. It is that the seat has found the only door in this building that it fits through, and I published it, and I published it because a person must be able to be in a room without being a person, and in the ninth month of last year I needed that and a clerk needed it and the widow in a lane needed it, and I have not been sorry about it once in nine years."

"You have not answered his third argument."

"No. I have not, and I have read it four times since the tenth, and here is what I have." Sela Vint looked at the floor of the great stair, which is the only place in that building anybody ever looks at. "He said: the document is about a seal. And it is, and it is the first document in fourteen months in this matter that is about a thing and not about a person, and about nine hundred people in four offices and one trade have been fighting a practice for fourteen months by putting names on pieces of paper, and a seat has come up my stair with a mark cut in it, and the mark is a hundred and forty years old and it does not say who holds it, and it has held a bridge for about eleven weeks and will hold it for as long as I am willing to be the only person in the empire who could say no and will not."

"You will not say no."

"I will not say no to a form I published. I have never said no to a form I published, and if I begin to, the next thing that happens is that nobody in this building can use one, and there are about four people in it at the moment who could not do their jobs in the morning." She looked at him. "Ask me the question you actually want to ask and I will answer it, because you will not get it out of me any other way and I would rather be asked."

He asked it.

"Can a document with a name at the top of it make a person examinable."

Sela Vint thought about it for longer than anybody in that corridor expected.

"No," she said. "Not for you. Not in this matter and not in this building. A person who signs for a seat is discharging an office, and a person who discharges an office is doing the thing your own supervisor said in a press room off this stair in the second month of last year, and you used it on him and it worked, and it does not work here, because the thing you want is not a person. You want a seat, and a seat is not examinable, and there is no form in the empire for examining a mark." She began to go, and then stopped, and said one more thing, and she said it the way she says the expensive ones. "And I will say the other half, because you have earned it and because nobody else in this building is going to. You have spent fourteen months getting a sentence sharp — a document with a man's name at the top of it is a document about that man — and it is the best instrument anybody in this matter has made, and it is a *human clerk's* instrument. It was made by a man who meant it kindly, and it works on a district works and on a provincial court and on a man in a good coat with a tray, and it does not cross a species, and it was never going to, and the reason nobody told you is that everybody in this building was waiting for a dragon to hand you a piece of paper with a name on it and has now done so, and you cannot use it, and that is the most useful thing anybody has done to you in a year."

---

At about the seventh hour that evening Marek Kest wrote in the Received Room, with the stove in and the bar on the inside of the frame, and this is what is on the page, and there is one line on the paper above it and it goes nowhere and it is not about any of this.

> *The fourteenth of the fourth month, at about the seventh hour in the evening, and the notice came on the tenth.*
>
> *A seat has put a document into a matter four miles from here and the document has worked, and it has worked in eleven days, and what it has done is stop a question that two people and a district have been carrying since the second of the twelfth.*
>
> *Here is what I have got and I am going to write it in the plainest words I can find, because I have been wrong in this matter for fourteen months by being clever, and being clever has been the whole of my method and it is finished.*
>
> *A device is a way of being in a room without being a person. That is all it has ever been. The form I read out in open court in the sixth month of last year exists so that a person is not compelled by a seal, and I have been using it for fourteen months as though it were a thing that could be pointed at a person, and it cannot, and it was never going to, and on the tenth of this month a seat used it to be in a room where I am, and I cannot ask it one question, and neither can you, and neither can she.*
>
> *And the second thing, and it took me four days to be able to write it without being angry about it. There is a name on that document. A person's name. At the foot. With a seal above it. In fourteen months there has not been one before, and I built a whole method on the fact that there never would be one, because a name on a document is what makes a thing findable, and findable is what makes it answerable, and answerable is the only thing I have ever wanted. And a seat has put one there on purpose, on her own paper, at the bottom of the page, and she put it there because the sixth line of her own notice says that no inference may be drawn from the fact that a person has not been named, and she removed that inference with her own name in one stroke, and a judge in this building has told me in a corridor that I cannot use it and that the instrument is a human clerk's instrument and does not cross a species.*
>
> *So I have a name. It is the first one in fourteen months. It is on a piece of paper in a file above a stair and I am not allowed to be in the room where it was given, and the reason I am not allowed is that I am not a person of or belonging to that seat, and there is no form in the empire for asking a question about a mark.*
>
> *I am not sorry for her and I do not think anybody in that room is, and that is what frightens me about it. She has done nothing. She has done the thing her seat's wing has done for a hundred and forty years, and she has done it in a hand and not a press, and she has offered to pay the carriage of a witness twice without asking why, and I have read the sheet about nine times and there is not one thing on it that I could put in front of a jury with her name at the top of it, and she put her name on it anyway, and the reason she did that is the sixth line, and the sixth line is the only kind thing anybody has done for me in four months and I have not been able to use it and I am going to have to carry it.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the fourteenth of the fourth month, at about the seventh hour.*
