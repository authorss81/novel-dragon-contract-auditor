# Chapter 53: The Press, and What a Clerk Never Looks At

The flue was booked for the fourteenth of the second month and a man from the works was coming on the second of the tenth to take up the corner of the floor where the press stood, and Ivet Sarn found this out the way she found out most things in the nine years of the window, which was by a person standing in front of it being angry about something else.

"The third flagstone," said the man from the works. "Not the fourth. The press is on the third and the fourth is the drain and I am not lifting the fourth for a clerk who wants her winter back."

"I have not asked for my winter back."

"Then somebody has, in a minute, in a hand that is not yours, and I am telling you it is a heating flue and not a restoration, and the man who booked it has put the fourteenth of the second month on it, and I have the paper." He put the paper on the sill, which is where Sarn puts things, and it was a work's order with a date on it and a signature at the foot, and the signature was a foreman's and the foreman was not the man standing in front of her.

Sarn read it twice, and then she went and looked at the wall, which is the thing she does instead of saying a thing.

> *WHAT THIS DISTRICT HAS TO DO ABOUT IT, WHICH IS ALMOST NOTHING.*
>
> *One: the first order of the Cinder Court says that no guarantor duty may attach to any person who has not been heard and has not signed. That is now true here, and it was already true here, because this court said it in a room on the twenty-first of the fifth month, and what changed on the seventeenth of the sixth is that it is true in forty-five other places as well, and this court is glad of it and claims none of it.*
>
> *Two: the second order says the sentence is model wording and is not a decision of any court. It is not withdrawn in this district, because this court is the only body that can withdraw its own practice, and it has not been asked to and has not offered to, and I am not going to write on this wall that a district is bound by a high court it has never been heard by.*
>
> *Three: the process may be run again in this district next season. This district has not decided. It is not decided either way and I have not asked the magistrate and I will not be asked and I will not put my own view on a wall.*
>
> *Four: thirty-four names are on the hearing slate, of which four said the words I do not understand this and are entered as having said them, which is a complete answer and not a refusal. Twenty-two households are on the register of pending duties. And a request that is not granted is on the slate anyway, with a date on it, and that is the whole of what a slate is for.*
>
> *Five: a creditor in Greyfen has a lawyer and has had a paper served on a household in the first month, and the sale of the thing that paper was against is in a book at Coldsfoot and the book is right, and this court has no power over him and did not promise anybody that it had.*
>
> *Six: I have not run a window in eleven weeks and I have not opened one, and there is nobody in this building whose job it is to open one, and if you have a paper with a duty on it and you come to this window and ask me, I will read it to you and I will not tell you what to do about it, and I will enter that you came, because the register above my head is a register of duties and does not do that and never will.*
>
> *Seven: people ask me whether I would rather be a tidier clerk in a building where a decision stops at the building. I would not. I would rather be frightening and indispensable at a window with a nail in it, in a district of four hundred and sixty households where a man in a good coat has to stand and read everything before he reaches my sill. That is the whole of what I have got and it took nine years and a trial that cost nine shillings and fourpence and four people saying the words I do not understand this in a register, and I have nobody to hand it to, and there is a sentence on this wall about it, and the sentence is the only inheritance I have ever been left.*

The seventh paragraph had been on the wall since the third of the seventh month and it was the only thing on it that had ever been read aloud by a man she knew, and the man from the works had read it, and she had watched him read it, and she did not enjoy it, and she would not have given it up.

"That is a strange thing to have on a wall," he said.

"It is nine years of a job."

"Will you take the third flagstone up on the second and let me see what's under it."

"No."

"I have a paper with the fourteenth of the second month on it."

"You have a paper with the fourteenth of the second month on it and I have a minute of the twenty-first of the fifth month that says this court joined about sixty households in this district as guarantors in the sixth year in letters and without a hearing, and that it cannot get their names in time, and that they are not before it and have not asked to be." Sarn put the work's paper back on the sill and squared it with two fingers, which she did with everything. "And I have just worked out where they are."

---

They are not in a register, and the reason they are not in a register is the answer, and it took her a morning and a half to arrive at it standing at a window in a building where she had worked nine years.

A register of them would have been a record of the court doing it. A clerk in the sixth year who entered sixty names in a book headed *households to which the public-necessity sentence has been applied* would have had to write a date, and a decision, and the fact that no one was heard, and there is no column in any register in this building for that, and there never has been one, and she has looked.

The sentence was applied **in letters**. That is what the court itself said in its own minute, in its own words, on the twenty-first of the fifth month, with a magistrate's name at the foot of it: it applied the sentence in letters and without a hearing to about sixty households in this district in the sixth year. Letters. Not filings. Letters go out on a cycle, and a cycle is a thing a clerk does four times a year, and the copies of what went out are filed by the cycle, and the thing a cycle does with what comes back is press it and put it in a bundle and write the bundle on the shelf.

And nobody goes and looks.

She had worked nine years at a window through which people came in and complained about the court, and she had never once, in nine years, gone into the old records room at the end of the embankment corridor, which has a cold floor and a press in it and a window that does not open, and which is where this building's outgoing correspondence goes to be kept for the twenty years after nobody needs it.

The fourth week of the ninth month she asked the young clerk from the intake desk at Auremar — who came down on a rider at his own cost and did not think twice about it, and who wrote the time in the margin in ink and initialled it, for the fifth time, for the same man, four hundred and thirty miles away, and has still never been thanked for that one either — to come for a day, and he came for two.

The press is a press. It is a long shallow drawer of deal with a screw at the side and it goes in about four inches, and a clerk puts the marked returns in it face down and closes it, and a clerk does that four times a year and writes the date on the outside in chalk, and that is the entire technology.

The bundles were there, in order, on a shelf of oak that had not been moved since the seventh year.

*Returns, third cycle, sixth year. Thirty-one marked. One bundle.*

She read them standing up with the bundle on the sill of the window because the floor was cold and the window was the only place with a sill, and it took about four hours, and it was not difficult work, and it is the only four hours of her life she would describe as easy.

A press-mark is a word in the corner of a returned envelope and the clerk's initial beside it. Eleven of the fifty-four carried a press-mark that is not a name. Eleven, out of fifty-four: *left* four times, *gone* three times, *no such* twice, and twice a mark that is only two letters and a blot, and she wrote those down as they came.

Thirty-one carried a name and a door, in the returning officer's own hand, which is a small upright hand and the same hand on all thirty-one because it was one man doing the whole district's walks for eleven years, and each one had a date against it and a word after the date, and the word was *seen* on twenty-two of the thirty-one and *told* on nine.

The twelve that came back with nothing at all on them were returned to the district, which means they came back from the post, and there is a stamp for that and the stamp says *undeliverable* and says nothing about where.

She counted twice and got it the same way both times, and then she counted it a third way, which was to count the bundles, and there were **three** bundles and not four, and there should have been four, and the fourth cycle of the sixth year was not in that room.

The fourth cycle of the sixth year was never made. She went and found the woman who had been a clerk in the sixth year — retired, two streets up the embankment, and about sixty-one — and walked there at about the seventh hour with a sheet of foolscap and a list of eleven marks on it, and stood in the woman's kitchen, and said, "I am going to ask you a question about a bundle and I would like you to tell me if it existed."

"It did."

"Will you tell me who made it."

"It was going to be me." The woman said it without any particular weight, the way a person says a thing they have had eleven years to get used to. "I was the third clerk of the year. I was the third clerk because the first two had gone and they took the bundles up. I did the first, the second and the third and I was going to do the fourth and then I was told my notice, and I went in the seventh year, and there was nobody after me at the end of the sixth because they did not replace me until the docket had closed, and the docket did not close until the following spring."

"Who told you not to make it."

"Nobody told me not to make it." She looked at the list. "I did not make it because the fourth cycle is the one that takes about nine days, and I had eleven weeks of the docket left and I had a child, and I have gone over that eleven years about four hundred times and I have not found a moment in it where anybody did anything to me."

Sarn wrote that down in the woman's kitchen in her own hand and read it back, and the woman said that she would like a copy and could not pay a shilling, and Sarn gave her one and did not put it on any account, and has still not put it on any account.

---

**About sixty.** Three cycles of fifty-four households, and six of the minute's sixty in a fourth cycle that was never copied because a clerk had a child and eleven weeks of docket and no replacement, and that is the whole of the arithmetic and it is worse than a lie and better than a lie, because a lie would have had a number on it.

She entered them in the register of pending duties, which is where they belong, in the form she has used for nine years: a date, a household, a duty, the next review, a shilling a copy. The duty in every one of the thirty-one is the same and it is in the magistrate's own words, and she had to read the sentence twice before she could put it in a column without her hand doing something she did not want it to do.

*The holder of the said property is joined as guarantor until full review of the matter.*

*Until full review.* And the magistrate's third finding had said the court does not know their names and cannot get them in time and they are not before it and have not asked to be, and the fourth had said the district will have to work out in the sixth month what it does in a flood when it cannot use the sentence.

Eighteen months, now, since that finding. And on the wall, in paragraph four, thirty-four names and a register of twenty-two and a request that was not granted and was on the slate anyway with a date on it.

She wrote the thirty-one in on the fourth week of the ninth month, in her own hand, over about two days, and each line has a name and a door, and she did not put a duty against any of them beyond the sentence itself, because there is no other duty and inventing one would be the thing she has been frightened of since the fourth of the tenth month.

And then she took the chalk, which she has not used on that board since the second of the seventh, and wrote a line at the head of the block, above the first name, in letters about four inches high, and a queue at the window read it upside down for a week and had it read out.

> *THIRTY-ONE OF ABOUT SIXTY.*
> *Nineteen returns did not come back. Six copies were never made, by a clerk who was not asked to and did not, and I have her kitchen and the date and her word for it, and she is sixty-one and has a child and is alive.*
> *Nobody has been asked to do anything about this and nobody is going to be until a person with a bar comes to this window and asks.*

And underneath the thirty-one, at the bottom of the block, in her own writing, small, where a person has to come close to read it:

> *Nine years and I have never once gone and looked at an outgoing rack. I asked every person in this district for a name, off a docket, out of a file, off a wall, and I never went and looked at a press in a cold room at the end of the embankment corridor, and the sixty were in it eight feet from where I work, and that is not the district's fault. That is mine, and I have put it on the wall in my own hand with the day on it, and the day is the fourth of the tenth month. — I.S.*

The man from the works came back on the second of the tenth with his paper and stood in front of the wall and read all of it, all of it, standing up, in about eleven minutes, which is how long it takes, and then asked the question Sarn had been waiting nine months for a person to ask her.

"What is a bar."

"A person comes in here with a bar and asks," said Ivet Sarn. "That is the whole of the arrangement and I will put it in writing if you want it. Nobody in this building has to open a window. There is no rule and there is no office that says anybody has to open anything. And a person with a bar has to answer questions in a room, in words, in front of the rest of us, and cannot be instructed, and cannot be managed, and cannot be put in a box on a form in a corridor four hundred and thirty miles away."

He read the wall again at the third hour, and the line about the clerk who was not asked to, and then he said, "Then somebody should come with a bar."

"Somebody should," said Ivet Sarn, "and I have written the number of the ones who will not come back on the same board as the ones who might, because a board that only carries the good news is a notice."
