# Chapter 82: A Hearing That Cannot Fail

The refusal of the third of the fourth month gave the household a date. It said in terms that a further application might be made on a date to be fixed on seven days' notice, and it said the date might be fixed, and it said nothing about how, and in nine years nobody in that court had ever had to work out how, because in nine years nothing had ever happened in that court that a person could not walk into.

A date to be fixed on seven days' notice requires notice. Notice requires a person to serve it on. And the schedule of persons who may be answerable in the matter of the crossing at Lowcross, compiled from the parish list of the second year and from returns received and revised in the third cycle of the sixth year, has eleven lines on it, and ten of them are offices or bodies, and one of the ten is a parish that was dissolved in the sixth year in a district reorganisation of which nobody in Lowcross knew, because the notice went to the parish.

The eleventh line is a maintenance form of the eighth month, and it is the only living person in the file, and she is in it under the heading *persons from whom a return has been requested*, because she answered a form, and a person who has been asked a question is filed under asked and not under liable.

And four of the ten lines read, in the correct column, since the fourth month of the sixth year: *the present holder*.

So the notice went to the holder.

---

He had been a clerk of that court for two years and he had a form for it, and the form is a printed sheet with a box for the person served and a box for the officer and a space on the back for the manner of service, and the back is the part that matters, because the back is the only part anybody ever reads in nine years.

He wrote the notice himself. He wrote it on the twelfth of the fifth month, which gave him two days' margin on a week, which is more margin than a district court ordinarily allows anybody, and he did not tell his magistrate, because a notice to fix a date under a refusal is the clerk's ordinary work and there are about nine of them a month in that court and none of them has ever been anybody's problem.

He read it before he sealed it, which he always does, and it read:

> *To the holder of the crossing at Lowcross, in the lower district of this city.*
>
> *Take notice that the further application mentioned in the order of this court made on the third of the fourth month may be heard on a day to be fixed, and that the day will be fixed on seven days' notice from the day of the service of this notice, and that if you do not attend on that day the court will proceed on the papers before it.*
>
> *Given at the chamber of the district court of the lower district.*

And he read that and thought, for about four seconds, about the word at the top of it, and then he thought: *that is the form of words. The form says the holder. The schedule says the holder. I am not the one who wrote it.* And then he sealed it, and that is the whole of what a person does in this empire that they afterwards wish they had not done, and the wishing is always about four seconds long and never gets written down.

---

It was served at the yard on the fourteenth of the fifth month, at about the eleventh hour, by an officer of the court who had served about four hundred notices in two years and who wrote the manner of service on the back in the ordinary form.

It was served properly. He wants that on the page because everything in the second half of this chapter is about the fact that it was served properly.

The woman took it. She is sixty-one and has signed one instrument in sixty-one years, in a yard, on the ninth of the ninth month of last year, on a box headed *the present holder* that she read at arm's length while a young man in a good coat stood in her yard and did not want to be unkind to her. She has refused a second instrument three times, and the third time she refused it before the form was put in front of her, because her granddaughter read it through first and found the second person's name line empty and said so out loud.

The granddaughter is eleven and she reads.

So the granddaughter read the notice to her grandmother in the yard, standing, with the officer about nine feet away and not moving, and she read all of it including the part about proceeding on the papers, because nobody had told her not to and because she is the person in that household who reads.

"What does it want me to do," said Hester Crane.

"It says you can come on a day they are going to fix," said the granddaughter.

"And if I do not come."

"They will do it anyway without you."

The yard was quiet for about as long as it takes to say that twice.

"Right," said Hester Crane. "Then you can tell him the paper's been read and it is not to be taken as me agreeing to it, because I cannot read it and a paper in a file in nine years would have my words in it and I would not be able to read it."

"You cannot read it," said the granddaughter, not as a question.

"No," said her grandmother. "And I am not going to hand you the next one, and I told you that in the fourth month and I have not changed my mind about it once and I am not going to now."

The officer wrote on the back of the notice: *served, and read aloud in the yard at the request of the person served, who states that she cannot read and that the service is not to be taken as her assent to anything.* He wrote it in full and read it back to the yard before he left, because he is a good officer and there are not many of them.

And the granddaughter, who is eleven, said one more thing, and it is on the page because a girl of eleven has asked two questions in this matter and both of them were the right ones.

She said: "It says the holder. There isn't one. You told me the parish went in the sixth year."

"There isn't one," said Hester Crane.

She said the rest of it in the yard, with the officer nine feet away and not moving, and she said it to a girl of eleven and not to a court, and it is the third of the four questions asked by a person of eleven in this book.

"You come and ask me whose name the word is for," she said, "and I am going to tell you and then you are going to have it in your head, and you are eleven, and in about six years you will be a woman and the word will still be in your head and I will not be able to take it out again. It is for the parish. The parish went in the sixth year. There has been nobody to send anything to since, and when a word has nowhere to go it stops being about a body and starts being about whoever is nearest, and I am the nearest, and I have been the nearest for about four years, and I did not know that is what had happened until a man in a good coat put his finger on a box in the ninth month of last year."

"Are they going to keep sending you things?"

"They are going to keep sending the district things, and the district is going to keep putting that word in, and the word is going to keep arriving at my gate, and I cannot read one word of any of it, and there is nobody I can ask, because the one person I would ask is eleven." She put her hand flat on the table. "So the fourth one is the one that decides it. *Who read this to me, and do they get paid if I sign.* And in this case the answer is a girl of eleven who gets nothing, is owed nothing, and is the only reason I know what is in it. That is the whole of the protection I have got and it is standing in my kitchen with a piece of paper in her hand."

---

The clerk came in on the twenty-first of the fifth month with the notice, and the magistrate was on the bench, and there was a man of sixty-four in the room.

The clerk had a card of his own on the table and had had it for four days and had not opened his mouth about it, and the reason he had not is worth about four seconds of this chapter. He had sent a question to a stranger and had put his own name at the foot of it, and he did not know yet whether that was going to get him into trouble, and a clerk who does not know whether his own name is going to get him into trouble does not raise it in a room in front of a magistrate.

The man of sixty-four was not a party, was not a witness, and had not been asked to come. He had been sent a card by the clerk himself, four days before, on the stationer's own paper with the court's nothing at the head of it, and the card had one question on it: *what does the crossing at the Lowcross cost to keep, per day.* The clerk had asked it because the bill in the file was four months old and unanswerable and he had begun to be frightened of it, and because he is the kind of clerk who asks a question in writing and puts his own name at the top of it, and the card had his name at the bottom.

Emrys Rudd had walked in from the far side, which is four hundred yards, at about the seventh hour, and had come because the card said the court, and because in nineteen years he had been sent four things and had gone to two of them, and both of them had wanted something.

So the room held: the magistrate; the clerk with the notice and a form; the officer at the door; a man of sixty-four in a lean-to's coat; and nobody at all who lived on the far side of that water.

---

"What are we doing with this," said Joane Tranter.

"Entering it served and fixing the day," said the clerk. "That is all it is. It goes in as served, the seven days run from the fourteenth, and the day is fixed for the twenty-eighth or the fourth of the sixth, whichever the list has, and the household can come or not come, and if they do not come we proceed on the papers."

"Have they come?"

"No, ma'am."

"Have they refused?"

He stopped. It had not occurred to him that there was a third thing, and he had been a clerk for two years and had served about four hundred notices.

"They have not refused," he said.

"Then what is it?"

"They have been served." He said it carefully, the way you say a thing you have said before. "The officer wrote on the back that it was read aloud in the yard at the request of the person served and that the service is not to be taken as her assent. Which is generous, ma'am, and it is more than the form asks, and I do not think it carries an absence."

"It carries an absence exactly," said the magistrate. "That is what a person who is not in a room is. Take it up."

She had the back of the notice in her hand, which she had not asked for, and read the officer's four lines twice, and then she read them a third time, which she does.

"Write what I say," she said, "and read it back, and do not improve it."

> *Entered the twenty-first of the fifth month. A notice to fix a date was served on the fourteenth of the fifth month upon the person named in the eleventh line of the schedule of persons who may be answerable in this matter, and that person was not in court on this day and has not been heard. She has not refused. The court cannot distinguish between a person who has refused and a person who has not come, and it does not propose to try, because both of them are an absence and an absence has no date and no initial on it and the court has no instrument that can tell them apart.*
>
> *The notice was served and the service is entered. The notice was not read by the person served. It was read aloud to her by another person in her own yard at her own request, and the reading is entered, and the reading is not a hearing and is not to be treated as one.*
>
> *No date is fixed.*

"That is honest," said the clerk, "and it is useless."

"It is both," said Joane Tranter, "and I have been in this court nine years and I have found out that those two things are the same and that the second one is the one that goes in a minute book."

And then she said, to the room rather than to him: "You. At the back. You have been sitting there for eleven minutes and I have not asked you anything, and you were sent a card with a question on it and you came four hundred yards for it. Say your piece, and then I will say mine, and then we will both have said ours."

---

Emrys Rudd took about a minute and a half to get started and then took about four minutes and a half to finish, and there is a version of this in a minute of that court in about nine sentences, and it is this.

"I have been at the head of that crossing nineteen years and I have been handed a paper about eleven times in nineteen years and I have signed every one of them and I have been right every time, and the reason I have been right is that I have been standing at the end of a bar for nineteen years and I know what the bar is for and nobody who handed me one of them wanted anything else. The bank wants a stone laid. The district wants the figure for a return. That one wanted to know whether a cart would go. I signed all eleven and eleven times I knew exactly what I was doing.

"You have just served that paper on a woman who cannot read it, at a house, with her granddaughter standing next to her, and she has told the officer that the paper is not to be taken as her agreeing to it, and the officer has written that down, and it is the first of those eleven that anybody has written down. Every one of the other ten was me putting a mark on a line because I knew the answer, and this one is a person not knowing the answer and being afraid of it, and you cannot do the first thing with the second thing, and I want that said in a minute book because in four years somebody is going to come to that yard with a tray and say that the holder of that crossing is a person of sixty-one who cannot read and has a granddaughter, and the first thing that will be said about her is a box with a word in it that walks about and stands in for her name.

"That is all. I have been paid a penny a day for nineteen years to sit at the end of a bar and I have been asked two questions in nineteen years and the second one is this afternoon and both of them have been asked by a woman who came herself."

He stopped. There was a longish silence and then he said the last of it, and it is the ninth sentence in the minute and it is the reason this chapter exists.

"And I want to say one more thing and I want it in the room. I would not have said no to a man with a paper in that yard, and I want whoever writes this down to know that, because in four years there is going to be a great many of people saying that people like me and people like her were signing things they did not understand, and about half of that is true and the other half is a man at the end of a bar who could tell you what the bar was for."

---

The minute was read back and it was put in the file and the file was shut.

That is the whole of what happened at the district court of Auremar's lower district on the twenty-first of the fifth month, and it is: a notice served properly, a person who was not in the room and did not refuse, a man of sixty-four who came four hundred yards to answer a card, and a day that was not fixed.

The crossing is open. Nobody is liable for it. The bill is nineteen pounds three and fourpence and there is no line for it in the fund and there has not been one in nineteen years, and about four hundred and eleven people are on the far side of that water, and the district's return for four weeks says three hundred and eighty-eight crossings, and the two figures are different figures and are never the same and have never been the same and are not going to be reconciled by anybody.

The one thing in this matter that has changed on the page today is a form of address. A word that walks about has been put in an envelope and sent by post to a house, by a clerk of a district court who did the ordinary thing correctly, and nobody wrote it, and nobody is going to be charged with it, and there is no instrument in this empire for examining a word in an address, and the finding of this afternoon, in a minute, with a name at the foot of it, is that the word is the only part of the notice that will still be true in nine years.

It is nine o'clock in the evening before he writes his own account of it, and he writes it in a book of his own with no office at the top of it, which is a thing he has kept for about two years and has about nine lines in it, and all nine are questions he has asked a person in writing and never got an answer to.

> *The fourteenth of the fifth month. I served a notice on a person who cannot read it. I did not ask her to come to this court and she did not come and I cannot say that she refused, and the officer wrote on the back that the reading was not to be taken as her assent, and he wrote it because he is a better officer than me and not because anybody told him to.*
>
> *I sent it to the holder because the schedule says the holder. I have written the schedule's own words out twice this afternoon to see whether I would still have written them, and I would have, both times, at about the same speed, and that is what frightens me. I did not have a moment of doubt. I sealed it four seconds after I read it. My whole trade is that I do not have a moment of doubt, and it has cost me one this afternoon, and what it cost is a day of a woman of sixty-one's, and I am not going to be able to give it back.*
>
> *And here is the part I am not going to put in the file. The notice said the holder. The schedule has said the holder in four returns since the fourth month of the sixth year. I looked it up on the thirteenth, in the room, in about nine seconds, and I did not go and ask anybody what a word in a column meant, and I did not write to a window, and I did not take it to my magistrate. There is a man in a district of this city who would have told me in about four minutes what a word that walks about is, and he is on a list, and I do not know his name, and there is a clerk of twenty-five in a building four miles from here who writes down the time a thing arrives and has never once been asked what the figures in his column are a number of. I know about both of them the way a person in a city knows about the weather.*
>
> *That is the whole of what happened. A notice, served properly, at a gate, to a woman of sixty-one, by me, because I am good at my work.*
>
> *— the twenty-first of the fifth month, at about the ninth hour in the evening. My own book. No office at the top of it and I have not put one there.*
