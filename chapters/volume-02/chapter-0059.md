# Chapter 59: A Duty That Nobody Owes

The bar went across the Lowcross end at about the second hour of the twelfth of the twelfth month, by a ladder and a length of chain, and the crossing had been open for nineteen years and four hours, and there is no instrument in the empire that can put that in a column.

The market was the day after. About ninety people came to the Lowcross end, and about sixty of them stood there, and about thirty turned round and went to find out whether the towpath was passable, and it was, in the sense that a towpath is passable, which is a different thing.

Rosamund Crane did not go. She is eleven and she goes to the mill on the far side on market days and there is no school on a market day because there has never been a school on a market day.

Hester Crane went down and looked at the chain and then came back up and made a pot of tea and did not pour it for anybody, and then poured it, and then sat down at the table where a copyist from a shop with a price painted in the window had written eleven lines in a small fast hand about a column.

---

The four miles is not four miles.

It is a mile and three-quarters along the towpath on the Auremar side and then the bank, and the bank is not a road, it is a shoulder of the cut with a rope on it that is there for the barges and not for feet, and in the third month of a year with ice on it you do the last quarter of a mile on a plank and a gatepost and a matter of nerve.

Hester Crane had done it every autumn for nineteen years because a bridge is a thing you do not think about until it is not there, and she had never once had to think about it, and the reason she knew the bank so well is that she has a granddaughter who goes over it twice a week and a son of thirty-four in the district works who could not get to her at four o'clock when the works finish if the bank were bad.

He got there in about an hour and a half on the fourteenth of the month. It was not an emergency. Nobody in the Cranes is ill. It was a woman of eighty-one on the far side who comes over for flour on a market day, and the mill's carts, and a chapel service on the sixteenth, and about ninety market-going.

---

Here is what closing a public bridge does, in eleven days, in a district of the lower city, in a return that a clerk compiled afterwards because nobody had asked for it and because a woman of sixty-one asked for it and paid for it out of a purse that has not had anything in it since the eighth month of the eighth year.

Flour: eleven households on the far side had been carrying their own four-day flour over the bridge and putting it in a dry box. Eleven households had four-day flour, and eleven households had a dry box, and eleven households had a *lift* of a quarter of an inch of sill, and the bridge took the sills out. On the sixteenth they bought flour at the mill price and carried it on their backs. That is a difference of about eleven pounds a month across eleven households and it is in the return because the clerk wrote it down and not because anybody looked for it.

The mill: four carts a day, eleven minutes each way at the crossing. With the bank, ninety minutes each way. The mill sent two carts down the river instead and the road toll is ninepence a cart, so the mill paid the road and lost a day.

The chapel: about fifty people, and about eighteen came, and the eighteen were over sixty-five, and the vicar wrote a line in the return about the fourteen who did not come and did not say why, and a copyist who has copied other people's *why not* for a year read that line three times and did not fill in the column.

The woman of eighty-one: her name is Berta Coll and she is eighty-one and she lives two hundred yards from the far end on the far side, and on the second day she did not come. On the fourth day a neighbour took flour to her. On the ninth day a girl of nineteen from the mill went over on the plank with a sack on her shoulder and came back with an empty, and was gone before anybody on this side knew about it. On the eleventh day somebody in a chapel said so, and the girl of nineteen said that she had done it twice and would do it again, and that her name was Wyn Aldis and that if anybody wanted to write it down they could, and that she would rather they did not because she was not brave, she was nineteen and the water was low.

---

Jonas Cray came up the yard on the nineteenth of the month at about the second hour with a form in a good hand and did not have a good coat on, which Hester Crane noticed and did not think about until the eighth hour of that night.

"I have to write a thing," he said. "And I have never written one, and I have to write it about my own office, and I have to read it to the man who signs it before he signs it, and there is nobody in that building who is going to be glad to read it."

"Write it here," said Hester Crane. "Write it on that and then I will get you somebody to read it."

"You cannot read it."

"I cannot read a paragraph," said Hester Crane, "and I have known that since I was nine years old, and there is a girl in this house of eleven who can, and I am going to tell you something now and I want you to hear it properly. You are going to write a sentence down on that paper about whether your office can do this. And you are going to read it to the man who signs it. And he is going to sign it because it is true and because it is in front of him. And in nine years there is going to be a piece of paper in a file in a building that says that the district works of the lower district could not stand the cost of a public crossing, and it is going to be true, and it is going to have been said by the works, in the works' own hand, on the works' own paper."

"Yes," said Jonas Cray.

"And nobody is going to be thanked for it and it is not going to change anything for about a year."

"That is what the man in the leather case said in the room on the second of the twelfth month," said Jonas Cray. "He said it out loud to a magistrate and I wrote it down at the back of my own book afterwards, which I have never done, and I have been at the works four years."

"Then you are the third one," said Hester Crane, "and I do not know the first two and I have not asked."

He wrote it at her table. It is one sentence and it is on a works form and it is in a young clerk's hand and it has a foreman's name at the foot of it, and it does not say that the district failed and it does not say that the works failed:

> *The district works of the lower district states, on the nineteenth of the twelfth month, that the fund of this district works for the quarter of the first month of next year cannot stand the cost of the bank, abutment and kerb works at the crossing at Lowcross, estimated at nineteen pounds three and fourpence, and that the crossing is a work of the public of this district and is not the work of a holder, and that this office asks the district to stand the cost and does not ask it to be paid by any person.*

He read it out. He read it to Rosamund Crane, who is eleven, and she read it after him, word for word, and he wrote down that she had, with the date, because he is a clerk of that kind and there was nobody to tell him not to be.

And then he read it out to Hester Crane, at arm's length, twice, and she said that the two readings were the same, and that she wanted it noted in her own words in the copyist's hand and not in his, and the copyist wrote it:

> *Read to me twice at my own table and the two were the same, and I cannot read it and the girl read it and he read it and then he asked the girl to read it after him and wrote down that she had, and that is the first time in nineteen years that anybody in this house has been asked whether a paper said what it was supposed to say, and I have run a bridge for nineteen years and I have never once been asked that and I did not know it had never happened.*

And then the young man from the district works stood in a yard and said, out loud, without being asked, that he had been told on the fifteenth of the month by a man in a good coat that he would be spoken to about the two days, and that he had understood at the time that the two days meant the two days early that the bar went up, and that he was now aware that it meant a different thing, and that he had not been spoken to yet and that he did not want to be spoken to before Hester Crane had a copy of it, and that the copy was being written at that moment and that he would like to hand it to her himself, at her gate, and go away.

"You come up here again in the fourth month," said Hester Crane, "and I will be here, and I will not be able to read whatever it is, and you will have to say it to me out loud, and if it is a bad thing to say you will have to stand in this yard and say it twice."

"Yes, Mrs Crane."

"Once a month, and not in a good coat."

---

The arithmetic, which she has done every autumn for nineteen years and which a copyist wrote down on the nineteenth of the twelfth month because nobody had ever asked for it, is this.

The crossing costs the district, if it is mended by the district, about nineteen pounds three and fourpence every nine years, which is about two pounds two and a half a year, which is about a penny a day, which is about nothing.

The fund of the district works holds, on the average of four quarters, about five pounds. The crossing has taken about a pound of that four times in nineteen years and has lost about a pound a year for the other fifteen, and nobody has ever written any of it down in a column, and the reason nobody has ever written it down is that a column wants a number in it and a penny a day is not a number anybody in an office can enter without laughing.

That is the third thing and nobody has said it out loud yet: the crossing is not unaffordable, and the reason it has stood for nineteen years is that the amount is too small to be a line in a fund and too large to be a household's money, and it has lived in that gap for nineteen years, and a woman of sixty-one has been paying it out of a box.

And if the gap is closed — and it will be closed, because a box of that kind closes when a form comes back with a word in it — then somebody has to say out loud, in a room, in writing, that a public bridge is a work of the public, and a district that has never been asked to pay for a crossing has to be asked, and an office that has never written down that it cannot pay has to write it, and a man of fifty with a schedule has to say in a room that the schedule is not the district.

None of those four things is a hearing. All four of them are a hundred times harder than a hearing and about nine years long.

---

Nine names.

A list of nine, in Hester Crane's own hand, on the back of a form, on a table with a lamp on it, read out and written down by a copyist who came on a cart and paid four pence of the carriage and would have paid eleven if it had been eleven, and the list is the only thing in eleven days that nobody asked for and everybody can prove.

> *Carried the crossing at Lowcross between the twelfth and the twenty-fourth of the twelfth month, unpaid, not asked, not permitted in one case, and recorded here because a district clerk asked for it and I am not having it said afterwards that nobody came.*
>
> *Jonas Cray, works, second day, with a ladder and a chain and an ordinary day's order. That is his job and I will not count it twice.*
> *Gideon Harrow, works. The fourth day, the ninth day, and the sixteenth, before and after, with a spade, alone, and he is the man who put the crossing out of use and I am putting it in here and he may read this.*
> *Aloys Fenn, chapel, six days.*
> *Wyn Aldis, mill, nineteen years old, the second day, the fourth day, the ninth day, three times, and there is no road for it and she says she was not brave.*
> *Rennick Quill, mill, the fourth and the ninth, with the cart.*
> *Maud Quill, his wife, the ninth, with a spade and a bad back.*
> *The two men off the Reed Cut bank, the seventh, with a plank of their own, and neither of them would give a name and I have written down that they would not, and that is the fifth time somebody has refused me a name in eleven months and it is the fifth time it has turned out to be the right thing for them to do.*
> *A woman I do not know at all, the eleventh, who came from the far side and went over and came back and would not say anything about it and would not take anything for it, and who is not on this list with a name because she would not give me one and I am not going to invent her.*
> *And my son, who is in the works, and who is not on this list, because he was not permitted, and who went four times, and who has been spoken to by a foreman about it, and who would like it written here that he was not permitted and that he is not sorry and that the foreman is a decent man who is going to be in a difficult position in the fourth month.*

And the bridge came back on the **twenty-fourth of the twelfth month**, which is eleven days early against the fourteen that was in the order, and it came back because nine people carried it, and it is going to come back in a year only if somebody is paid.

---

The copyist asked her the question that the day was going to be about, and she answered it before she was asked, which is what she does.

"You will not sign a second one," said the copyist.

"I will not sign a second one."

"And you have signed one."

"I have signed one and I have got it in a cloth with the note on the reverse and a girl of eleven read it to me at my own table and stopped on the fourth line and asked me whose name it was." Hester Crane put her hands flat on the table, and there was no lamp lit now and it was about the eighth hour in the afternoon in the third week of the twelfth month and the light comes in off the water on that side of the yard at about the fourth hour and is gone by six. "I have read the sheet that is nailed to the Reed Cut gate. I do not read the whole of it. I know the fourth one by the number because people say it out loud on a lane and I have heard it four hundred times and it says that a person who takes on a continuing duty at a place has to sign a new paper for it. The paper says it. It is on a gate four hundred and thirty miles down a river and I have heard a woman of my age say it at a table and I have got it right, I think, and if I have not then somebody will tell me."

"Will you keep the wardenship."

"I will keep the wardenship. I have kept it for nineteen years and I have done the work of it and the work has been done and the bridge has been there and nobody died on it. And I am not going to sign a second instrument, and I am not going to be a woman who has signed two, and I am going to be the woman who did the work and signed one form on a word she read at arm's length because a young man in a good coat was in her yard and did not want to be unkind to her."

"Will you say why, so that it can be written down."

"I will say why, and I want it written down in your hand and not only in mine, and then I am going to have to say it twice, and I am going to say it the second time shorter." She sat up.

"Nineteen years and I never signed nothing. And this year I signed something. So the reason I am not going to sign the second thing is not that I am careful. I have not been careful in my life. The reason is that I am the second woman in four hundred miles that a word that walks about has got hold of, and the first one was a girl of fifteen, and she was in a court, and she had somebody in the room with her, and I am sixty-one and I have a granddaughter who can read."

The copyist wrote it in a small fast hand and read it back, and Hester Crane said it the second time shorter.

"Two women. That's all. I am the second one and I have got my granddaughter and I am not going to hand her the next one."

And then she said the sentence that the whole eleven days and the whole of this case come down to, and she said it to a woman from a shop with a price on the window who had put four pence into a slot for the carriage and would have put in eleven if it had been eleven, and she said it once, and then she made her write it a second time because she knew what it was.

"You have made it true and I am the one it is true about."

She would not have it in a document. She said no to the copyist twice on that and she said why, in her own words, and the reason is worth having because it is the reason a woman who cannot read a paragraph is the right person to have said the sentence this case is about.

"The works have put me in a box and a court has heard about a bridge and a man from the Roll Hall lost a hearing he should have won, and there is a piece of paper in your bag with my name in a column that is not the right column, and if you write that sentence down and put it in a file somewhere and hand it to a man in nine years, then in nine years there will be a document that says the right thing." She put her hands flat on the table again. "And in nine years I will not be able to read it, and it will have my words in it, and that is what the whole of this is and I have known it since the ninth of the ninth month and I did not have the words then."
