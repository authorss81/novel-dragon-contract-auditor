# Chapter 88: The Woman With the Barrow of Hot Water

The woman who brings papers to be stamped stopped coming to the bench about eleven weeks ago, and the plate-cutter did not notice for a fortnight, and then she did notice, and she noticed the way you notice a thing in a trade, which is by counting.

Three days in ten for eleven years. That is somewhere over a thousand times, and she has never once counted and neither has anybody. A woman who has been coming three days in ten for eleven years and then stops is a woman who has found somewhere else, and a woman who has found somewhere else is not a woman who has run away, because a woman who runs away does not come back on the fourth week to say so.

She came back on the fourth week. She came down the lane at about the fourth hour with a bundle under her arm and she stood at the bench for about a minute and a half and she said, in the voice she has used for eleven years, which is a voice that has never once asked for anything:

"There is a woman with a barrow at the end of Weaver's Cut."

"Yes," said the plate-cutter.

"She writes things down for people. She wrote one down for me."

---

So the plate-cutter walked down to Weaver's Cut on the twenty-first of the seventh month, at about the fifth hour, and stood at the end of it for about a quarter of an hour, and did not say anything to anybody, and the reason she did not say anything to anybody is on the page and is the whole of what this chapter is about.

There is a woman of about fifty-five at the end of that cut with a barrow of hot water and a brazier under it, and she is there from the fifth hour to about the ninth, and everybody in that lane goes to her. She is not an office. She has no book with a heading on it and no office at the top of it and no fee and no name over the door, and she cannot read a paragraph either, and she can read a number and she can add up a thing on the side of a slate, and she is the reason about nine families in that lane know each other.

The plate-cutter stood at the end of the cut and watched her do it, and it took about six minutes, and it was exactly what she does.

A man of about seventy had a paper on the barrow. It was a demand from a district water office about a house he had left eleven years ago, and it wanted the sum of two shillings and a farthing by the sixth of the eighth, and it carried a line saying that the person might come to the office and say the house is not his, and the office would then consider it. The sum of two shillings and a farthing is about half a day's water-hauling in that lane and it is not a large sum and it is the fourth one of those and the last one will be bigger.

The old man could not read it. He has had that office's paper twice before in four years and both times he put it in a box and the box has been emptied by somebody else in the meantime.

The woman with the barrow read it aloud, all of it, twice, and then she asked him four things.

Not four questions. Four things, and they were her own and not anybody's, and the plate-cutter heard them from thirty feet away and could not hear the actual words and did not need to, because she has watched about four hundred people ask a person who cannot read a paragraph what a paper is and every single one of them asked the same four things in the same order.

The four things were: is there work in it; whose name is on it; if you stop, whose name answers for it; and who read this to me and do they get paid if I sign.

The old man answered the second one without being asked. He said the name on it is his, and it has been his for eleven years after he left, and that he had told the office so in person once and the man at the counter wrote something down, and that he had not been able to find out what.

The woman with the barrow had a sheet of paper in front of her with two boxes ruled on it, and no heading, and nothing at the top of it at all, and the plate-cutter could see the ruling from thirty feet because the ruling is hers — she rules two boxes by hand at about a minute and forty seconds a sheet and she has ruled about a thousand of them in twenty-two years and she would know her own ruling on a wall in the dark.

The old man said four things. She wrote them down, in his words, badly, in about a quarter of an inch of space.

Then she turned the sheet over and wrote the day on the back, and beside the day three short cuts in a triangle.

Not her own mark. She does not have a mark. She had copied his.

And then she said, and the plate-cutter heard this too, that she was not going to keep it, because she does not know what to do with a piece of paper, and she gave it back to him, which is wrong, and he put it in his coat, which is worse, and both of those are what a person does.

---

And the four questions are the second thing in it that the plate-cutter did not invent and did not know she had.

They were on a stone at the top of a lane in a flood district four hundred and thirty miles down a river, in chalk, in a hand nobody can now name, in the sixth month of last year, and they were not a law and they were not advice and they were four lines, and they have been read out at a trestle table in that lane ever since by anybody who turns up. A copyist of a third gallery of this city holds the forty-one pages of the Cinder Court's record under a four-line form with none in the office box, and about four hundred and forty people in four offices and one trade have read the order on it. The girl of thirteen, who was thirteen in the fifth month, has them. And now a woman with a barrow has them, and has had them for about four months, and has never said where she got them, and would not be able to say, and did not in fact.

"You have not asked her where she got the four," Marek Kest said, when the plate-cutter told him, on the twenty-third, in the Received Room.

"No," said the plate-cutter. "And I am not going to, and I want that written down with the day on it, because you will ask me in about four years why I did not ask a question I could have asked in nine seconds, and the answer is that she got them from a woman who got them from a woman, and if I ask her where, then the next person who thinks of one of those four things has to hide it, and I have spent eleven years being the only person in this lane who knows what a number in the edge of a thing is a number of, and I am not going to spend the next four years being the reason nobody else in this city writes anything down."

---

She went and looked at the sheet on the twenty-second, which took about nine minutes and a quarter of an hour of standing about, and it is in the plate-cutter's own book and it is the first time in about eleven years she has looked at another person's copy of a thing she does, and the finding is four lines.

> *The twenty-second of the seventh month. The sheet is mine. The ruling is mine to the inch. The two boxes are in the right places and the three lines under them are in the right order and the day and initial on the back are in the right place. There is no heading, no series, no seal and nothing at the head of it, which means somebody taught her that as well, and nobody taught anybody anything.*
>
> *The word in the second box is `None` and she does not know what it means. I know that because I asked her, in the lane, in about four seconds, standing at the barrow, and she said: she said the other woman wrote it, and that there was no office, and that is the whole of what she knows about it. She has been putting it in the second box for about four months. She thinks it is a word that means there was no office. **It is not a word about an office. It is a person who cannot be instructed, and that is a different thing, and I am the only person in this city who can say which, and I said about nine words about it on the edge of that barrow and she wrote none of them down, and I did not ask her to.**
>
> *I have let the word out of my mouth and I cannot get it back, and I have been thinking about it since yesterday and I have got it down to two sentences and they are the ones in the next line.*

---

> *One. `None` is not a protection. It is a person. And it only works while the person who writes it knows that is what it is, and does not put an office in the box, and an office in that box is a door and the door is behind the questioner and the whole thing turns round and the copyist is inside it.*
>
> *Two. There is no way to send a correction. There is no form, there is no series, there is no run, no counter to send it to and no office to receive it, and a form with a run on it is a model waiting for a district, and I am not going to be the reason there is a run on it. I can walk to the end of that cut and I can stand at the barrow and I can say it out loud, and if I do it then I am a person in this matter that a document is about, and I am sixty, and I have three cuts in a triangle and no office, and I am not worth a tray, and that is the whole of what I have got and I am not spending it on nine words about a box.*

She wrote that down on the twenty-second and read it back twice and did not cross it out, and then she went back to the bench at about the seventh hour and cut about forty plates, and the stove was out by the eighth and she lit it again.

---

The three of them between them, and it is worth setting out in order because it is the whole of how anything in this world has ever travelled from one person to another:

The plate-cutter did it to the woman at the far end of her own lane once, in the fifth month, in about four minutes, with no form and no heading, because the woman asked and could not read. The woman at the far end of her own lane told the woman with the barrow, because that is what a person does at a barrow at the fifth hour. The woman with the barrow got a sheet of foolscap and had her own granddaughter rule two boxes on it, or a neighbour, or a man who charges a penny for a pen; and she wrote the word `None` in the second box because that is what she had been told there was to write in it. And in about four months there were about nine people in Weaver's Cut who had a piece of paper with a day on the back of it and no name on the front of it, and every one of them got it from a person standing next to them.

Eleven weeks, three women, no form, no sender, and nothing in it that anybody in this building knows about.

And there is nothing wrong with it. That is the finding, and it is the fourth time in a year that the only correct answer to a method has turned out to be that it travels.

And the other thing about the eleven weeks is on the page because it is the cost and nobody else is going to pay attention to it.

In the fifth month the plate-cutter wrote down, in her own hand, with the day on it, that she had about three weeks of being not worth a tray left, by accident, because a man with a tray has to knock on a door to get at her and he does not knock on doors.

The three weeks came and went. It is the twenty-fourth of the seventh month. Nobody has knocked. Nobody has sent anybody. A certifier's clerk came once and asked for a name and was told no, and a district court's officer came twice and asked for a thing and was told no, and about nine people in this lane now know that there is a woman at the bench who will tell you the truth about a number, because a girl of thirteen walked up here with a card and told her mother, and a mother tells a lane.

**She has not become worth a tray. She has become famous, which is a different thing and is worse, and it took eleven weeks and two visits from a court.** A person nobody can find is safe. A person everybody in one lane knows about has a radius, and the radius is about a quarter of a mile, and there is nothing at all in this empire that cannot be got by walking further than a quarter of a mile, and in about four years somebody will come from further than a quarter of a mile, and the reason they will is that she is now true, and a thing that is true and about a person is the most interesting kind of thing there is.

She wrote it out on the twenty-third, and it is the last four lines of her own book for that month.

> *The twenty-third of the seventh month. I have been in this lane twenty-two years and I have never seen anybody do the thing I do except me, and I thought that was because I am the only one who thought of it, and it turns out it is because there is a lane at the end of this one with a barrow on it and I have never once been down it.*
>
> **This is not a vindication of anything and I am not going to let anybody in this city write it down as one.** I did not invent it. She has been putting a number and a mark and a day in the corner of a piece of paper for about eleven years when a person could not find out whether a plate was theirs, and nobody ever asked her why, and I found that out in a lane in the fifth month by accident, and a copyist with a barred door four miles away put a box round it in the fourth month of this year without knowing it existed. That is the whole of its history and it is not an invention and it is not a discovery and it is the shape of a thing that was always there, and a person who claims it for his book in nine years has claimed a thing that was never anybody's, which is the correct outcome and the reason nobody is going to be paid for it.*
>
> *And it is a fact about people and not about the sheet: a thing that is worth a day and a mark and four pence to a person who has nothing is done by people. That is all it is. It is not evidence. It does not prove the sheet works. It does not prove the sheet is any good. It is what a person does when a person has worked out that a piece of paper can come back to them with something on it, and about nine of us in this city have now worked that out independently, and one of us wrote it on the back of a form and one of us wrote it in pencil in a quarter-inch of a girl's card and one of us has a granddaughter who ruled two boxes in a lane off the Kiln road, and between the three of us there is not one line of law.*
>
> *I. It is the twenty-fourth of the seventh month. The eighteenth of the seventh has come and gone. There is a room in this city with a seat's device on the door of it and a notice on a table in it with about a half-inch of white paper at the foot of it where a signature goes, and that room sat or did not sit, and nobody has been told which, and I have not asked, and I am not going to. I have got a bench under a window in a lane and about nine hundred plates and a stove that wants lighting at the eighth hour. I have been in this lane twenty-two years and I have never once been anywhere else, and I am not going to be interested in a room with a seal on the door, and I would like it noted that that is not a principle. It is that I am sixty and it is a long way.*

---

On the twenty-fourth she cut about forty plates and there was a card in the tray with a number in the edge of it and she cut it, because that is the work and the work has not changed and is not going to.

The girl of thirteen came back on the last day of that month. She is fourteen now, or she says she is, and she had a second paper, and she wanted to know whether that one was hers too, and the plate-cutter took the edge of it between her own thumb and the side of her forefinger the way you find a cut in a coin, moved it about an inch, and stopped.

"That is not mine either," she said. "That is out of a different part of the run. Go and ask the woman with the barrow, and tell her it is from me, and she will do the same thing I did, and you will not have to walk up here again, and that is the point of the whole of it."

The girl looked at her.

"Is she allowed to do that?"

"There is no allowed," said the plate-cutter. "That is what I have been trying to tell you since the sixth of the fifth month. Nobody is allowed and nobody is not allowed and there is nobody to allow it. Go and ask her."
