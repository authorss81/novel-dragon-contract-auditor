# Chapter 70: A Date With Nothing Under It

The notice came down on the morning packet of the sixth of the third month, and it is one side of a sheet, and it is printed, because a date for a sitting in room four is printed and sent to the list, and the list is about two hundred and forty names and about nine addresses in this city, and one of the nine addresses is a room with a card in the window at the height of a man's chest.

Nevin Trost read it standing up in the third room off the corridor behind the third gallery, at about the seventh hour, and read it a second time, and then stood there for a while with his thumb on the corner of it, which is what a man does with a notice he is going to have to act on and does not want to touch again.

The notice said:

> *Cinder Court, room four. On the eighteenth of the seventh month, at the second hour, on the motion of the court, with jurisdiction, and over the objection of the applicant entered on the seventeenth of the sixth: the instruction sealed with the device standing upon the file in the matter of the rehearing in the district of Mosswake will be heard at a full sitting. Copies of this notice are on the list.*

And at the head of it, where a court puts its device, is the device, and there is nothing else at the head of it, and at the foot of it — where a court puts a name, and where on the seventeenth of the sixth there is a name, and where a form read out in a room on the seventeenth of the sixth in this same building says a name is to be — at the foot of it there is nothing.

Not a seal. Not a form of words. Nothing at all, and about a half-inch of white paper where a signature goes.

Trost stood in a room with three shelves in it and looked at that for about a minute and a half.

"Well," he said.

He had a cold. He had had it since the second month and he had a wife and a strong instinct for finishing his working day, and those are three facts about Nevin Trost that have been true of him since the eighth of the eleventh of last year and none of them is going to go away in the next six weeks.

He wrote the notice into the day's book of the Examinations office at about a quarter past the seventh, because it was a notice, and a notice is a document, and a document that arrives is entered, and then he read the entry back and found that he had written the word *sitting* twice and had written nothing at all about the half-inch of white paper, so he went back and put a line under it, and the line says:

> *Entered the sixth of the third month. The notice of the eighteenth of the seventh month. **The notice carries the device and no name at the foot.** Entered that, because the answer of the twenty-ninth of the first month from the same court stated, in a form, that a date is not to be fixed for a matter in which no party can be named, and that when a date is fixed for that matter it will be fixed with a name at the foot of it. A date has been fixed. There is no name at the foot of it. I am the examiner who made the record of the refusal of the sealed page and I am writing this down because somebody in nine years will want to know that it was noticed on the day.*

And then he sat down and did the arithmetic, which he had already done standing at the window, and did again because a man should not do an arithmetic like that once.

The eighteenth of the seventh month is four months and about twelve days from the sixth of the third month.

Four months is what it takes the Standing Office of Provincial Continuities to print an amendment sheet to a series and distribute it on the ordinary list to forty-six addresses holding a flood docket. It is what it takes four districts to receive it, file it with their standing instructions, and believe that a court has decided it, which is what four districts did in the last round, and which is why there is a public-necessity sentence in nine hundred and forty copies that a high court has recorded as model wording and not as a decision of any court. It is what it takes a river to come up in a district in the fourth month and a clerk at a window to reach for a form because the form is the only thing in the room.

Everything this matter has been about since the twelfth of the ninth month of last year goes on happening for another four months, and the reason is a date.

Nevin Trost is the examiner of Examinations, thirty-two years old, six years in the job, and the reason he can say out loud what that date costs is not that he is clever. It is that a magistrate of a district court said it in a room in the twelfth month, over a timber bridge, and lost the case she was right about.

*That is the answer of an office that has understood the law and drawn the only conclusion available to it, and I want it on the record that I have read nine files in this district in nine years and this is the first one where an office has told me in writing that it is powerless. The district should know what that costs: nothing, and nothing gained, and the two are not the same thing.*

He wrote it out on the back of the notice, in his own hand, without the magistrate's name on it, and then he stopped and got out the day's book again and wrote the name on it too, because a sentence with a name at the foot of it is a sentence anybody can be asked about and one without is a sentence in a drawer.

---

The ninety words were in a drawer in the same room and they had been in it since the ninth of the eleventh of last year, which is four months, and the drawer is the second drawer of a table in an office with four desks and a window, and nobody in the building has ever opened that drawer, and that is not because the drawer is locked. It is because nobody has any reason to.

It is one sheet of the Notaries' Table's own writing paper, which is a bad cream stock about the size of a long envelope, and it has ninety words on it, and it has no heading, and it has no office at the head of it, and it has no signature, and it is the instruction that the head of that Table put in the corridor outside the fourth room of the Chancery into the hand of an examiner of twenty-nine on the ninth of the eleventh of last year and said *this for the file* and walked away.

Ninety words. Trost has counted them about four hundred times and he can give the number without looking, which is what happens to a person who has had a piece of paper in a drawer for four months.

It says, in substance and in a form of words that somebody else wrote, that the request of the eighteenth of the ninth month and the supplement of the eighth of the eleventh are to be received and consolidated and accepted as a limited administrative inquiry into the filing practice of the district of Mosswake; that the scope is to be fixed on one page; that the name of the person conducting it is to be on that page; and that the acceptance is to be dated, and is to carry a name at the foot of it, and the reason for that is to be written under it in the examiner's own hand.

Every single thing he asked for.

Trost read it that night in a room with a cold in it, on the sixth of the third month, at about the ninth hour, and then he did the thing he has been not doing for four months, which is to write down, in a book of his own, on a page of his own, with the day on it, why he is not doing it, and he had written that page four times in four months and had crossed out all four and this is the fifth.

> *Fifth attempt, sixth of the third month, about the ninth hour, and I am not going to cross this one out.*
>
> *The reason I have not filed ninety words in four months is not that they are dangerous to me, and I have written that reason four times and crossed it out four times, and it is not that, because a decision I signed in the ninth month of last year has four lines under it in my own hand saying that I was told to put my name to it, and those four lines are the only thing in this matter I have ever been proud of, and I have been proud of them in four rooms, and the ninety words would make them a complaint.*
>
> *Filing them makes me a man who reported his own office. A man who reported his own office is a man who cannot examine his office's decisions, and I have six years in this job and I have wanted one thing since the second month, which is a file I can answer for in six years, and I had it in the ninth month of last year and I do not have it now, and I am going to have it in about four years, and there is a bridge in the lower district of this city that is open on unpaid labour.*
>
> *That was the reason until about an hour ago. The reason now is the date. It is four months away and it is on a piece of paper with a half-inch of white space at the foot of it, and I have made four records in this matter with my name at the foot of them and one of them I did not want to write, and every one of the four is worth exactly nothing against a date four months out, because a record is a thing that a person can be shown, and a document nobody has produced is a document nobody can be shown.*
>
> *A review date with nobody standing at it is not a review. I did not write that. A woman wrote it on a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river, in the first person, with the day on it, and I have read it three times, and I am not going to be able to say in six years that I read it and did not act on it.*

He read it back and did not cross it out, and at about the tenth hour he walked the ninety words down a corridor to the office where Examinations keeps its matter files, and filed them in the file of the matter, in his own hand, with the date, and with this line under them:

> *Filed the sixth of the third month, held unfiled in my own drawer since the ninth of the eleventh of last year. Filed now because a date has been fixed for the eighteenth of the seventh month and it is four months away and the half-inch at the foot of it is empty, and a document nobody has produced is a document nobody can be shown. I am aware that filing this makes me a person who has reported his own office. I would like that entered as a cost and not as a finding. — N. Trost, examiner, Examinations.*

And then he did the second thing, which is the cost, and it took a further half hour, and it was the reason he filed them and not the other way round.

He went up the stairs to the copying alcove of the third gallery and put a copy of the ninety words on the counter, and she was not in and the alcove was empty and there was a lamp on, so he waited.

"You will not take a copy of a document out of your own file," she said, when she came back and read it standing up. "I have watched that happen. A copy of a document in your own file, in your own hand, carried out of your own office, is a thing that cannot be un-carried and it is a thing you can be asked for by anybody who knows to ask."

"No," said Nevin Trost. "Which is why I have brought it to you and not to a registrar and not to the Docket of Powers. Three rooms off this corridor there is a register of persons with about fourteen entries a year in it and a man who has kept it for twenty-two years and who will enter this correctly in about nine minutes, and he will then know where it came from, and so will the four hundred and ten people in thirty years of that book, because a man who files a thing about his own office has to have it in a book somewhere, and if it is in that book then the man who keeps it has a copy in his head for the rest of his life, and I would like that to be true and I would like it to be with somebody who cannot be instructed."

He put it on the counter.

"You are asking me to hold a copy of a document out of a file in an office that is not yours, in a book of my own, with no office on it, which is the same shape of thing as a custody form with *None* in the second box, and you have had one of those and you have said it was the best thing that has ever happened to you."

"Yes," said Nevin Trost, "and I have had a cold since the second month and I am going to say the rest of it in one breath so that I do not have to say it twice. I have held a piece of paper for four months because it was the only piece of paper in this matter that had somebody's handwriting on it that was not a dead person or a copyist, and because for four months I was the only man in the empire holding a thing that a court would have had to look at, and that is a very strange four months, and I did not enjoy one hour of it, and every hour of it was a preference."

She read it twice. Then she copied it out — a second copy, in her own hand, in a book of her own — read the second copy back twice, wrote on the back of the first one *held by T. Rook, no office, the sixth of the third month*, and gave it back to him.

And then she said one thing, and she said it in the flat voice, and it is the sentence this chapter turns on:

"There is one more person who could be asked about that sheet and I am not going to ask, and I want it written down that I have worked out who it is and that I have not asked, because the ninety words are on the writing paper of the Notaries' Table and a pad is not a book and nobody keeps a pad, and there is no stub for an instruction handed to a man in a corridor, and the man who wrote it handed it to you and walked away and he is not on a stub and he is not in a book and he is not anywhere in this empire that anybody can get at with a form."

"You could go back to the press room," said Nevin Trost.

"I could go back to the press room in about nine minutes and I could look at the same book on the same shelf and I would find nothing, because what I am describing is the absence of a record and not the presence of one, and if there were a record of it then it would not be ninety words in a drawer in a room where nobody has any reason to look." She squared the copy. "That is the whole of what I have learned in four months and I am not going to unlearn it at the third hour of the night because a man has a cold."

---

The man with the burn-scarred hand came on the eighteenth of the third month, in the third hour of the afternoon, to the third room off the corridor behind the third gallery, and he had not been asked to, and nobody in the building knew him, and he gave his name at the card in the window because the card says what the room is for and he had read it on his way up.

"Oren Tal," he said. "I am a scale notary. I am not one any more. I was trained in this building by a woman called Kest about thirty-one years ago and I do not know what became of her and I have not asked, and you will not get a second sentence about it."

"Sixty-nine?" said Nevin Trost, who was guessing at the scar and was about nine years out.

"Sixty-one or two. It was a fire and it was not mine and I was in the room and I got out of it and the hand says so and I have never once said to anybody what I was doing when I got out of it." He put the notice of the sixth of the third month down on the table and squared it, which is a thing Tamsin Rook does, and which Tamsin Rook noticed and said nothing about. "I came about that."

"That is a date."

"That is a date four months out on a piece of paper with a half-inch of white space at the foot of it," said Oren Tal, "and I have been waiting about forty years for a date out of that room, and I am not going to be alive for a great many more of them, and I want to say one thing in a room with two people in it and then I want to go, and I am going to say it now and I am not going to be asked anything afterwards."

Nobody said anything.

"The old office of First Custodian was supposed to be **temporary**." He said it in the flat voice of a man reading an operative line, and he had the air of a man who has read a great many of them. "That is not an opinion of mine and it is not a tradition and it is not what anybody has been telling you for ninety years. It is a word. It is in the operative words and it was in the operative words before there was a Quiet Reign and before there was a proxy and before there was anything else, and every office that has ever widened that office has done it by not putting the word in the sentence."

"And what is that for," said Tamsin Rook.

"I am not going to say what that is for," said Oren Tal.

"Mr Tal—"

"You asked me a question and I have refused you, and I am going to refuse you in a form, and I want it understood that I have noticed the shape of what I am doing and that I have watched three people in this matter do it and do it better. A refusal with a name at the foot of it is worth more than an answer, and I have got about four years' worth of things I could say to you in a room and I am going to say none of them." He reached inside his coat and put a folded leaf on the table. "That is a copy. I made it about forty years ago. It is a schedule of the term and it is in a copyist's hand and the copyist's name is on it and the name is not one I am going to say in this office."

Nevin Trost did not touch it.

"What do you want me to do with it."

"I want you to do exactly what you have been doing for four months with a hundred and forty other things," said Oren Tal, "which is to hold it, and to decide for yourself what it is for, and to put it where it can be found. And I want you to understand that I have just handed the only thing I have kept in sixty years to a man I met ninety seconds ago, and that I have not asked you for anything, and that I am not going to be thanked, and that if anybody in this building thanks me I will leave."

He stood up.

"There is one more thing and it is for the clerk and not for you." He looked at the copyist. "You went and looked at an outgoing thing. I heard about it from a man who heard it from a man who counted paper at a door in a district court, and the counting is the part I have been thinking about for a week. It is this. For about forty years a temporary office is the only thing in the law that has an end on the face of it. A supervisor's term ends and somebody is chosen. A bench is held for a term. A wardenship is for nineteen years and then it is somebody's else. **A temporary office is the only office in this empire that is honest about when it stops, and it is the only one nobody in this building has ever had to administer.** That is what I have come to say, and I am not going to say what it is for, and if you write it down, write it down as a man talking and not as a notary, and if you cannot do that, then put the leaf in the drawer and do not write the other thing down, and in about nine years somebody will find the leaf and find the other thing themselves."

He was at the door in about eleven seconds.

"Mr Tal," said Tamsin Rook. "The woman called Kest. Is there anything you can tell me about her that is not about a file."

Oren Tal stopped in the doorway and did not turn round, and it took him about four seconds, and what he said was:

"No."

And he went down a stair of the Crown Kiln that smells of fired scale, and has done since anybody can remember, and out into a city in the third month, and neither of them saw him again.

---

Trost did not thank him. He had been told not to and he did not, and it was not obedience; it was that there was no form.

He put the leaf in a file of his own and did not put Oren Tal's other remark in a file of his own, and on the nineteenth of the third month, at about the seventh hour, he wrote it on the back of a sheet in a book he keeps at home, in his own hand, with the day on it, and the sheet says:

> *From the mouth of a man of about sixty-one, in the third room off the corridor, on the eighteenth of the third month, with two other people in the room, and with a document on the table. Written here and not in the office, because he asked for it not to be written in an office, and because a thing said in a room by a man who is not an officer of anything is a thing said in a room and the only honest place for it is a book with no office on it.*
>
> *He says the old office of First Custodian was supposed to be temporary. He says the word is in the operative words. He says every office that has widened that office has done it by not putting the word in the sentence. He says there is one more thing and he would not say what it is for, and when I asked him he refused me in a form, and he said he had watched three people in this matter do it and do it better than he could.*
>
> *He says a temporary office is the only office in this empire that is honest about when it stops, and it is the only one nobody in this building has ever had to administer.*
>
> *I do not know what to do with any of it. I am thirty-two and I have a cold and a file I can no longer answer for in six years and a date on a piece of paper with a half-inch of white space at the foot of it, and the half-inch is the finding, and the finding is four months long.*
>
> *N.T., the nineteenth of the third month, at home, at about the seventh hour.*

And the notice of the sixth of the third month went into a file in the third room off the corridor behind the third gallery, where it is, with the device at the head of it and nothing at the foot, and in the last week of the third month of this year a district in another province will receive a sheet of a series it has received before, and a clerk in a room will believe that a court has decided it, and none of the four hundred and forty people in four offices and one trade is going to be told that the only thing standing in front of that is a **date** and a half-inch of white paper.
