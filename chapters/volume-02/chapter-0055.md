# Chapter 55: The Register That Is Sixty Years Old

The Docket of Powers is three rooms off the corridor behind the third gallery, and the corridor is the same corridor the Forms Received rack is in, which meant that Marek Kest had walked past the door about four times a day for four years without once looking at it, in the manner of a man who has looked at a door and seen a door.

There is no sign on the door. The words are on a card in the window at the height of a man's chest, and the card is the only thing in three rooms that has been re-papered in about thirty years, and the paper on it is a different colour from the wall.

> *Docket of Powers. Register of persons recorded as having performed a scale reading under a recognised authority. Open at the fourth hour. Closed at the sixth and at any hour the Docket is at a hearing. No fee.*

Marek Kest went at the fourth hour of the third week of the eleventh month, with the letter of the seventeenth of the tenth in his coat, which was four lines long and had no threat in it and no request in it and said that the Docket had been informed that a clerk of the Roll Hall had performed two scale readings and that the Docket would be glad to give him an opportunity to explain the position before the register was made up.

He had four weeks to decide whether to explain the position. He had come on the first morning of the last one, on purpose, because he had worked out inside the first week that a man who is given four weeks and uses all of them has made a decision, and a man who is given four weeks and comes on the first morning of the fourth has made one too, and the difference is only in the shape of it.

Oswin Marrow was about sixty and had been master of the Docket for twenty-two years and was not a lawyer and had never been a clerk, and had spent the whole of that time keeping a book.

He offered Marek a chair, which nobody in four years had offered Marek a chair.

"Sit down. Do you want the book open?"

"Yes."

Marrow had it open before he finished the sentence, at a page, which meant he had left it there on purpose the way Sarn leaves a line ruled off on a board, and Marek Kest looked at it for about a minute and a half and understood exactly what was happening and did not say anything about it.

One page to a year. Four lines to an entry: name, date, instrument, authority. And the entries before the twelfth of the ninth month of last year went on for about forty years in a fair upright hand, and after it the hand changed four times, because hands change, and the four lines are the whole mechanism and there is no series and no run and no office at the head of it.

"Four hundred and something in thirty years," said Marrow. "Have a guess."

"About fourteen a year."

"That is very nearly right and it took me eleven years to stop being surprised by it." He turned the page back a little. "Do you know why it is fourteen?"

"Because a scale reading needs a permission and a permission needs somebody to have asked for one."

"Because a scale reading is an expensive thing done by a person who is frightened, and the reason there are fourteen a year in an empire with nine Houses and about four hundred thousand inheritances is that a person only agrees to have one when there is no other way of getting at the record, and when there is another way of getting at the record they use it and the register is empty for that year." Marrow closed the book over his thumb. "That is the whole of what this Docket is. I am the man who finds out who has done one after the fact, and I have about a hundred and forty names a decade to write down and I have never once had to enforce anything."

"I have never been in this book," said Marek Kest.

"You performed two scale readings."

"I performed two scale readings."

"That is the finding." Marrow wrote one line on a sheet of foolscap and did not look up while he did it. "Twelve hundred years of this office's practice is that a reading is an act performed by a person recorded beforehand. Not afterwards. Beforehand. The record is not a list of people who did it. The record is the permission, and the permission is what a court relies on when it relies on the reading, and if the permission does not exist then the thing the court relied on was not there to be relied on."

"How long has that been the practice."

"About twelve hundred years. It is not a rule anybody writes down. It is a practice, and like every practice in this building it is older than the paperwork and the paperwork exists to make it work." Marrow put the pen down. "So the reading is not void. Nobody is going to pretend to you that it is void. It happened, it was entered in a minute book, the minute book is in a court in another province, and a court is not a fool. What is missing is the thing that made it a reading, which is that you were recorded as a person who could do one, and you were not, and the reason you were not is that nobody ever wrote you into this book, in eleven months, and I would like to know why not, and I have asked your supervisor's office twice and been told that there is no procedure for it."

"There is no procedure for it," said Marek Kest. "I asked in the fourth month of last year, in a document, and the answer came back sealed with a seal and no name at the foot of it, and it answered two of five questions and referred three to a district's standing instructions."

Marrow looked at him for about four seconds.

"Then that is a very stupid answer and it is the only one that office had." He turned the foolscap round and pushed it across. "Read that. If it is wrong, tell me what is wrong, because I am about to enter it in a book that people have believed for sixty years and I would rather be corrected by you than by a man in a good coat in nine years."

It was three paragraphs and it was correct.

> *Finding. The applicant performed a reading of a scale instrument on two occasions. On the twelfth of the ninth month of last year at a public rehearing in the district of Mosswake, on a limited audit issued in open court by that court. On the sixth of the fourth month of this year, at about the seventh hour, alone, on a step at Reed Cut, on the same undischarged limited audit. Neither reading is entered in this register. A reading by a person not entered in this register at the time of the reading is an unauthorised scale reading.*

> *This Docket has no power to punish, to fine, to suspend, or to refer a person. It has three powers and it will use all three. First: it enters this finding in the public register at the foot of this page, in the form used, with the name of the person, the dates, the permissions and the disposition. Second: it bars the person from any audit, in any jurisdiction, in any office, until that person has been examined. Third: it refers to the court named in the practice, which is the court sitting in room four at the Crown Kiln, the single question whether a reading so performed was of any legal effect whatever, and refers it, and the answer will come back in its own time and in its own form and probably with a name at the foot of it, because that court has learned that trick from a registrar and I am glad of it.*

> *M. Kest may apply to be examined, in this building, on a date, before two persons named by him, and this Docket will give him that date and will not move it.*

Marek Kest read it three times. It was, in the plainest terms he had ever been handed, an accurate account of the worst thing that had happened to him in a year, written by a stranger who had spent an hour with him and had a book that was sixty years old.

"You have not asked me one question about the readings themselves," he said.

"You did not notice and I am not going to," said Oswin Marrow. "I do not care what your readings said. That is not this Docket's business. That is a court's business and a witness's business and it is the business of the four hundred and forty people who acted on the record your readings were entered in. I care whether you had the right to do it, and you did not, and I have written that down, and I am now going to do three things with it, and I have told you all three in advance, which is more than anybody in this building has ever done with you, and I would like you to notice that as well and not only the rest."

He signed the finding, and then he turned the register round to the page for last year and the page for this year, and there was not a gap in either of them, because Tamsin Rook had been in on the fourteenth of the ninth month and had filled both in with her own hand, and her initials, and five lines underneath saying that she had done it on her own initiative and that she had not asked the person named, and that it was not a finding that either reading was authorised.

Marek Kest read it four times.

"You knew about that."

"I was told about it on the fifteenth of the ninth by a clerk with a card, in four minutes at a counter, along with the fact that the independent copy of the evidence in this matter exists and is not produced and that I have no power." Marrow took the sheet away from him. "Your copyist has entered two acts of yours into a public register in her own hand and has put her own name next to them, and has said in writing on the face of it that she did it without asking you, and that is a thing that will be held against her for about four years and it is the correct thing for her to have done and she did it in about nine minutes, and I would like to say one more thing about it and then I will stop."

"Say it."

"A register with a gap in it is a shape with a hole in it, and a shape with a hole in it is a thing a person does not have to lie about." Marrow shut the book. "She put her name next to yours. That is the whole of what she bought and it cost her a fortnight of her own standing in a building she has no office in, and I have been in this room twenty-two years and I have seen it done twice, and one of the two was me, in the nineteenth year, and it was not a woman and it was not a copyist and I have never told anybody and I am telling a man who is not the person it concerns because he is the person it is nearest to."

The room was quiet for a bit. There is a stove in it too, and it was lit, and it was the second stove Marek Kest had been in that week that somebody else had lit.

"May I say the thing I have to say," he said, "and then you can tell me no."

"You may say it and I will do what I am able to do with it."

"I do not contest the entry." He put his hand flat on the foolscap, which is a thing he has watched Tamsin do four hundred times and had never once done himself. "Two readings happened and you are right that they happened. I do not contest the bar. I will not be examined in a hurry to get it lifted, because the examination would be about whether I was frightened, and I was, and that is not an argument."

"Then what."

"I contest the referral. And I want to contest it in one page, in writing, today, and I want to tell you the reason in a sentence." Marek Kest looked at the sixty years of the book and did not look at the man. "If a court finds that those two readings were of no legal effect whatever, it will not touch me. I am not what they are for. It will touch the only record in this empire in which a girl of fifteen is entered as heard and a household is entered as asked, and a person who is erased out of a record is a person nobody can call, and I have spent eleven months learning that on the page of a wall four hundred and thirty miles down a river, in a clerk's hand, in a sentence about sixty households whose names she did not have."

"There is a difficulty," said Oswin Marrow. "You are not the person who can put that."

"No."

"No. I am. And I am going to tell you what a Docket of Powers is for, and then I am going to refuse you, and then I am going to give you the date anyway, and you may put both halves of that in your own book with today on it."

He squared the foolscap.

"A Docket of Powers keeps a register of persons. It does not keep a register of records. It has no power to say to a court what a reading did or did not do to a file, and if it could, this office would be the second most dangerous room in the Roll Hall instead of the one with the best window." Marrow put the pen down. "So the referral goes, and I am the person who makes it, and I will make it in a form that says what you have just said in one sentence at the top, in my own hand, and the sentence is that a finding that the reading was of no effect would not be a finding about the reader, and a court that wants to make it has to say what it is a finding about. That is not a refusal of the referral. It is a condition on the shape of the question, and it goes in on the same page, and I do not want your thanks, because a man who is being examined for four months does not need the man examining him to be on his side."

Marek Kest wrote the page.

It is one page, in his own hand, and he wrote it standing up in a room with a stove in it, and he wrote it in about eleven minutes, and it is the only page he has ever written with another person in the room.

> *I do not contest the finding, the entry, or the bar. I contest the question as it is framed, and I ask that it be reframed before it is referred, and I ask for nothing else.*
>
> *The question as framed is whether a reading so performed was of any legal effect whatever. The answer to that, if it is no, is a finding about the reader, and I have no standing to object to a finding about myself and I do not object to one.*
>
> *I ask that the question referred be this instead: whether a record in which such a reading was entered, being a record of a court and not of this Docket, is altered in its effect by the finding that the reading was unauthorised. And I want to say, in a clerk's hand, that I do not know the answer, and that if the answer is no then every person in this empire who acted on that record acted on nothing, and the district's own return puts that at three hundred and eighty-eight crossings a week, and a person cannot be held to a record that turns out to have been made by a man with no right to make it.*
>
> *And I ask the second thing the Docket can give. I ask to be examined in this building on a date, before two persons whom I name, and I ask for the date to be given now and not moved, and I will attend it, and I have not asked to be examined quickly, and I have not asked for the bar to be lifted, and I will pay the fee of the room myself and I would like that on the page because a man who is being examined should not be examined in somebody else's room.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the third week of the eleventh month, at about the fifth hour.*

Oswin Marrow read it twice, and then he signed the second line of his own finding, which was the line that said the question goes with a condition on its shape, and wrote the date of the examination at the foot of Marek Kest's page, and it was the **eleventh of the first month**, at the fourth hour, in the Docket's own room, and he named the two persons himself when Marek asked him to name his own, and named **the intake clerk of the Chancery** and **a copyist of the third gallery**, which meant that the examination would be conducted by a man of twenty-five who had never read a book of procedure and a woman of twenty-four who had no office, and Oswin Marrow said that he had chosen them on purpose and would like that entered.

"You have given me a bar," said Marek Kest, standing in the doorway with his coat on. "I would like to tell you that it is the fourth thing that has happened to me in eleven months and the first one I have not been able to argue with."

"That is not a compliment to the Docket."

"It is not," said Marek Kest. "It is a complaint about the four months before it, and the four months before it were mine."

And he went down the corridor and past the Forms Received rack without stopping at it, which was the first time in four years he had walked past that rack and not stopped at it, and he noticed himself not stopping, and he wrote that down in his own book that night with the date on it, because a thing you notice about yourself in a corridor is worth four lines and about nine years later somebody will want to know.
