# Chapter 98: What a Conviction Is For

The original came up on the eighteenth of the tenth month, in a box, with an officer's return at the door and a line in it that said the box was not opened in the corridor, which nobody had asked for and which the officer of the door had written on the back of his own paper because he has been the officer at that door since the second of the second and he counted the sheets going into that chamber on the morning of the trial and has been getting them back out in the same order ever since, and nobody has ever said thank you for it.

It went on a bench in the chamber of the district court of Auremar, which is not the district court of the lower district and is four miles and a great deal of other business away from it, and Silas Sowle was in the room and had not expected to be, and the reason he was in the room is that the direction of the second of the second month said the original was to be produced to that court, and a solicitor whose application is refused with a date is a solicitor who turns up.

The box was opened at about the third hour. There is a book in it and a wrapper and a piece of paper with a clerk's name at the foot of it from the middle of the first month, and the paper is four paragraphs long and is a certification of a copy.

The book has ninety-one leaves in it and the leaves are numbered one to ninety-one with nothing missing.

The wrapper it was copied from is inside the back board, because a gallery keeps the wrapper it made a book from, because the wrapper is where the count is, and on the wrapper, in a copyist's hand of the sixth year, is the figure ninety-two, and under the ninety-two in the same hand is the word *complete* with no figure against it.

"Ninety-one and ninety-two," said Silas Sowle, to nobody. "I have been told about that since the third week of the first month and I did not understand it and I have not been able to ask, because the man who wrote the certificate is not mine and I am the household's solicitor and nobody else's, and a man in the third gallery certified a copy short against his own gallery and told nobody he had done it, and I found out about it from a sheet with four words on the front of it which arrived in a coat."

The clerk of the court read the certification out in the ordinary way and then read the fourth paragraph of it, which is the one that says the certifying clerk has been unable to account for the difference and is not able to say that a leaf was never collated into the book.

"So the book is short by one leaf of the original," said the magistrate, "and the original is in front of me, and it has ninety-one leaves in it, and the figure on the wrapper is the only thing in this city that says it was ever ninety-two."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Was the ninety-two a count of the original or a count of the copy."

"The same hand wrote both," said Silas Sowle. "The ninety-two is what came off the original and the ninety-one is what went into the book, and there is a word under the ninety-one and no figure under the word, and the word is *complete*."

"Then the original was ninety-two and this book is ninety-one," said the magistrate, "and the leaf is not in the book, and nobody knows whether the leaf was never collated or whether it was collated and taken out in six years, and there is no instrument in this city that can tell those two things apart, and I am not going to try, and I am going to enter what I can."

She entered it in about four minutes and read it back, and what she entered is this and it is short and it is the only thing in fourteen months that has come out of a bench and gone into a minute and been a fact about a book and nothing else.

> *The original of the ledger directed to be produced to this court has been produced, and it is of ninety-one leaves, numbered consecutively. The wrapper from which the copy in the Chancery was made is endorsed ninety-two in the hand of the copyist, and against the figure ninety-one in the same hand is the word complete with no figure against it. This court enters that a copy of a book may be asked what it does not contain, and that this copy was asked, and that the answer was given on the face of the certificate by a clerk of the Chancery against his own gallery, and that the only remaining difference between the two figures is one leaf, and that this court makes no finding as to how that difference arose and directs that nobody be asked about it in any proceeding in this court or elsewhere.*

That last clause is the important one and she added it on her own motion, and nobody had asked for it, and it is the fourth thing Joane Tranter has entered on her own motion in nine years and it is the only one this district has got that Joane Tranter is not the author of.

---

Marek Kest was in the corridor outside the chamber with a sheet of paper in his hand and the sheet had four words on the front of it and no office, and he gave it to Silas Sowle at about the fourth hour, and the four words were: *the question, and the date.*

> *Mr Sowle. — I am going to use the rule on the front of the copy your copyist sent you, and I am telling you that I am using it because you told me on the thirteenth of the third month of last year that you read it off a sheet of paper and that it was the single most useful thing anybody did for me in four months, and I have never once used it on anybody but you and I am about to.*
>
> *The question: is there a use to be made in this district of a conviction that does not require a person to stand in a room and say a name out loud, and if there is, what is it, and what is the thing that it is as against.*
>
> *The date: the twenty-first of the tenth month, at about the sixth hour, in a room, in words, and not on a page. You choose the room. I will come to it and I will not come early.*
>
> *I am not asking you to give up the thing you want to do. I know what the thing is and I have known since you told me about the returned form in a coat, and I have read the two findings of the ninth of the eighth and a woman in a yard can say more about a man in a good coat than a magistrate can in a chamber, and I have not been in that yard. There is a conviction in this city with a judge's name at the foot of it and a man in it who was found guilty on the second of the second month, and there are about forty people in four trades who have spent the last six months learning that a name in a file is a price, and the cheapest thing you could do in the next fortnight is read that conviction out in a lane in this district and watch what happens, and it will work, and about nine people will be able to repeat it, and not one of them will be able to tell you afterwards whether they were told something true.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the nineteenth of the tenth month.*

Silas Sowle read it twice on the bench outside the chamber and then said: "You have written me a letter asking me to talk you out of something."

"Yes."

"And you have told me what you think I am going to say."

"Not in the sheet," said Marek Kest. "The sheet has a question and a date. The other thing I said out loud, on a stair, where nobody wrote it down, and I have not put it in writing because if it is in writing then in nine years it is a document with my name at the top of it, and I have had one of those in the last fortnight and I am still finding out what it is."

---

The answer was given on the twenty-first of the tenth month at about the sixth hour, in Silas Sowle's own office, which is two rooms above a chandler's shop and has a bench in it with a lamp on it and about nine ledgers, and the reason it was in that room is that Silas Sowle asked for it, and the reason he asked for it is that he said, and it is in his own book, that he had asked to be told in a room he had to sweep up in the morning.

"There is a use, and it is a shilling a copy, and it is the only one I can find and I have been looking for nine days."

"Go on."

"The conviction is a fact about a man in a room," said Silas Sowle. "That is all it is. It is about a man and a press and a number of things and a mark, and it is in a minute book in this city with the eleven protected people not named in any account of it, and the court entered on its own motion that no person in it had asked him for the name of a client or the name of a house and that it would not.

"That is the whole of what it is. **A conviction is a fact about a person in a room, and it is not a licence to go and find out who his clients were, and the line between those two is the whole of the difference between a court and a mob, and the line is about eleven seconds wide.**"

"Why eleven seconds."

"Because it is the length of a person standing up," said Silas Sowle, "and I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have got a woman of sixty-one who has said no to a word twice and signed nine things she could not read, and if I stand up in a lane in this district and read out what a man was found guilty of, then in about eleven seconds that lane is not hearing about a man. That lane is hearing about a word. And the word did not come from him. The word came out of a printed sheet in a run of nine hundred and forty copies on a list of forty-six addresses, and the man in the district house is not the man who printed it, and if I say his name in that lane in the next fortnight then in about four years that lane will be able to say that the whole of this was done by one man in Candlewright Lane, and it will be able to say it because I gave it to them, and I will have given it to them out of a good heart, in a bad coat, about a bill that was never the bill."

He put his hand flat on the bench, which he does and which is a thing he learned off a man who learned it off somebody.

"So what is the use."

"**A copy of the record of the trial, in plain words, at a shilling, sold at a counter, and nobody reads it out.** Not read out. Sold. It says a person was found guilty on the local counts and what the counts were, and it says a mark is a mark and not a name, and it says the court did not ask for a client and will not, and it says four hundred certificates and thirty-one of them called in on the fourth of the second month by an officer who read a list off a counter and did not ask a question. That is worth a shilling to a woman of thirty-five with a card in her sleeve and a mark on it who wants to know whether the mark is somebody's, and she takes it to a trade assessor, and he reads it, and that is the whole of it, and there is a girl of seventeen at a chandler's counter in Candlewright Lane who copies at a penny a sheet and would do ninety of them in a day and nobody has ever asked her to do anything for nothing twice in a row."

"And what do you tell the assessor if he asks you the next question."

"I tell him the next question is the one nobody can ask, and I tell him why, and I tell him about a man in a trade who is the only person alive who can break a number on a plate into hers and not yours, and I do not tell him which lane, because she is sixty and she has spent eleven years arranging not to be a person in a file, and because the day that becomes a fact in a file she is one, and a person who is findable is a person who can be asked the next question, and the next question is the one I have just spent nineteen years not asking anybody."

He said the last of it, and it is the sentence he has said out loud in about nine rooms in fourteen months and none of them were his own.

"And the man in the district house. Nobody has asked him for a name and nobody is going to, and I want it said in a room in this matter that the movement has decided that, and that it is not because he is owed anything, and that it is the only decision any of us has taken in fourteen months that I have never once wanted to take and would take again tomorrow."

---

Then he read the record out, which nobody had asked him to do and which he did because a man who has just refused to say a sentence ought to be made to say what he is saying instead, and it took him about nine minutes and the room listened.

The record says, in the words of the court that made it, that a plea was put and accepted and read back, and that it admitted the counts in a schedule and nothing else; that it neither admitted nor denied any matter touching any person or office not named in the schedule; and that the defendant's appearance to be tried was not an admission touching any such person or office. It says the defendant was convicted on the counts in the schedule and on no other count. It says the mark was entered as a mark. It says the court has no power to restore the certificates and nobody asked it to. It says eleven people are protected and none of them is named in any account of the trial. And it says, in a line that is the fourth of five and that a registrar of a district court entered on his own motion and asked for by nobody:

*That no person in this court has asked the defendant for the name of a client or the name of a house, and that it will not.*

"Nineteen years," said Silas Sowle, when he had finished. "Nineteen years of this district's poor business and I have never once had a document I could hand to a woman. I have had a hundred things I could hand to a woman, and every one of them wanted a pound and a name in it, and this is the first piece of paper I have ever read out of a file that a person can carry home and put on a table and **nobody can be sent to because of it.**"

---

The clerk of that court asked one question in the doorway on his way out and it was a fair one and it is in the minute of the twentieth as having been asked.

"May the movement use the fact that he was convicted to find out who sent him the work?"

"No," said Silas Sowle. "And the reason is not kindness and it is not the case and it is not the protection order. **A person who will not write a name cannot be compelled to write one, and that is the whole of the protection, and it is the only protection there has ever been in this matter.** A person who will write one can only be got at by a process, and a process that gets at names is a process that gets at anybody's, and a thing that gets at anybody's does not stop at the man in the district house. It goes to the next man, and then to the woman who sends her paper to be stamped, and then to the girl of seventeen on the bench, and then it is a system, and about four or five certifiers in this city worked that out in about three weeks by doing nothing at all."

"So the conviction does nothing at all."

"The conviction is worth a shilling a copy," said Silas Sowle, "and it is sold at a counter and nobody reads it out, and I am not going to count them and I am not going to be told by anybody how many there are, and the reason I am not counting is that a man who counts his copies is a man building a list, and I have spent a year watching four people in four hands build the only list that has ever worked in this matter and I have watched the four pages of it come back into a drawer this month with a name on one of them that the other three have never heard of, and I have not tidied it, and I am not going to start a fifth."

---

Marek Kest wrote it out in his own book that night with the stove in, and read it back, and did not cross anything out, and this is the last of it.

> *The twenty-first of the tenth month, at about the ninth hour. A conviction is a fact about a person in a room. Everything else anybody wants to do with a conviction is done in about eleven seconds and is done by a person who is not in the room, and the thing that happens in the eleven seconds is that the listener stops hearing about the man and starts hearing about a word, and the word was in a printed sheet in nine hundred and forty copies before the man was born, and the man is in a district house for three years and will be somewhere else for the rest of his life, and the word does not have a district house.*
>
> *And the gap in the copy. A book of ninety-one leaves and a wrapper that says ninety-two, and a clerk of nine years in the third gallery certified the copy short on the face of it against his own gallery and said in a certificate that he could not account for the difference and could not say that a leaf had never been collated, and that certificate is the only reason anybody in this city knows the original was ever ninety-two leaves at all. **A gap in a copy is a fact about the copying and not about the book, and a clerk who finds a filing without a reason writes reason not known, and I have now watched that happen twice in a year in two buildings and both times it was the honest thing and both times it was in a clerk's own hand.***
>
> *And nobody is going to look for the ninety-second leaf. I have written that down here, in my own hand, with the day, and the reason is in Chapter 87 and it is the same reason and I am not going to write it out again, and the reason is that a person who goes and finds a leaf has started a case, and I have found four things in fourteen months by starting cases and I have got a bar, a watch, a sheet nobody came to read and a woman in a lane who is famous instead of safe, and I am not starting a fifth one with a hook ledger and a steel die in it, because the man who carries the papers is right: the eleven pence is bought for not knowing, and the day a man in a good coat can read a thing in a bag, the carrier will say he only looked, and he will be right, and every one of us will wish he had been something worse.*
>
> *M. Kest, clerk, Received Room, the twenty-first of the tenth month, at about the ninth hour.*
