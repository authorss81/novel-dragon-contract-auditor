# Chapter 316: A Day-Book Four Miles Off, A Correct Sum In A Third Hand, And A Column Headed Next Review With Nothing In It

The lodging house is four miles off on a street that goes down to the river road and it is a counter and a shelf and a stair behind the counter, and a woman of about thirty-five has kept the day-book on the shelf for about nine years, and it is the only book in the building anybody opens.

He went in at about the fourth hour on a day when he had no reason to be there that he has ever said out loud, and there were about nine people in that room and none of them looked at him.

This is the second day of the second week of the fourth month of the year after the year after the year after next, and the lamp in his own room four miles off is not lit and is not going to be until about the seventh hour, and the lamp in that room is not lit in the middle of an afternoon either.

---

He rules lines for other men's sums so that they come out even, and that is the whole of what he is for, and he has been doing it for eleven years.

He ruled a line in this day-book about a year ago, in a column of somebody else's, and he did not put a figure under it, and what came back under it in about nine minutes' time was a correct sum in a third hand.

He has been ruling lines for other men's sums for eleven years, and the day-book on the shelf in front of him is ruled in four divisions on a page, three of them headed in one hand unaltered since before he was born.

**It is correct.** He has gone over it four times and it comes out, and it comes out the same way twice, and the hand that wrote it is not his and is not the hand of either of the two people in his practice, and nobody is going to tell him whose it is, and he is not going to go and look for the hand.

That is the whole of what a ruled line does. It waits. A person comes along eventually and puts a correct answer under it and the line has done its work and there is no way at all of knowing afterwards whether it was the line or the person.

---

**The day-book has four columns ruled across the face of a page, and three of them have headings over them,** and the headings are in a hand that has not changed since about the year he was born.

The fourth of the four is headed *next review*.

It has never had anything in it. Not in nine years, not once, not a day and not a name, and the woman who keeps the day-book has never put a mark under that heading, and there is nothing to mark, and there has been nothing to mark since the day the heading went on.

He stood at the shelf and looked at it for about four minutes and worked out something and then had to stand there a while longer with the rest of it.

The column was empty and the heading over it was one he could never fill, and nobody in the building came in and found him at it.

**A heading over an empty column is the most complete machine there is,** and it is better than a full one, and there is no form in this empire that will let anybody take a heading off a column that has had one over it since before she could read. A full column gets argued about and copied and carried forward, and somebody eventually wants the next line. An empty one with a heading over it gets looked at by the person who keeps it every single day for about nine years, and **it never once gets asked anything,** and it will be there after both of them.

Nobody has ever asked that column a question, because a column cannot be asked one, and because a heading is the thing that makes a column a machine.

He had it the other way round until this week, and he did not know that until he was standing in a lodging house four miles off with his hand on a shelf, and there is nothing in this empire that will tell him he had it wrong now.

---

The woman of about thirty-five came from behind the counter and said the day had gone by, and it had, and there was nothing to be done about it, and he was not charged for it.

"Do you want it struck out?"

"It is struck out and it has been struck out every week since the first of them, and the striking out is a line like any other line, and the line does not go anywhere and does not come back either."

She wrote it and she did not look up. **There is no form in this empire that would let her keep a day-book in a lodging house and be believed about a sum,** and there has never been one, and she is not going to be thanked for keeping it and has not been.

She wrote the day out as struck and did not look up. Fourpence went onto the counter for one page copied, and not one word passed between them about a third hand or about what a line under a heading is for, and about nine minutes is all the two of them gave to the whole of it.

He did not ask her one thing. Not the third hand, and not what the line had been for, and not why the fourth column had gone nine years with a heading over it and nothing under it, and there is no form in this empire that would let him ask her what the third hand is, and there never is going to be one, and the asking would have made her a person with something to refuse, and she has enough of that.

---

She came about nine miles twice. Once to a building off the old river road and once to a door that was not the door, and she was not let in either time, and she wrote none of it down then and she has not written any of it down since.

He has that at second hand and it is a fact about a woman and not a thing he is going to act on, and he is not going to ask her about it, and **No form in this empire makes a day four miles off into a place a person can be got at,** and it is also not a place a person can be got out of, and a woman who has been twice is a woman who has been twice.

**He put fourpence on the counter for a copy of a page, and she did not ask him what for and he did not tell her,** and that is the whole of what either of them said about a third hand in about nine minutes.

---

**He went back the four miles on his own two feet,** and he did not go north and he did not go and look at the hand, and he did not stop on the way to work out whether a third hand was worth a day of somebody else's wage, and a day of that is about eleven pence and he has never spent one of those on asking anybody anything.

The four miles took him back on his own two feet. He turned the third hand over in his head for the length of a street, and a third hand is worth about eleven pence a day to whoever it belongs to, and none of them has ever gone on putting a question to anybody.

**The bill for his being findable is not his and stands on somebody else's account,** and he worked that out in about four minutes on a step in another street a long while back, and there is no instrument in this empire that stops it being so, and there is none that would stop him either, and he has never once used either of them.

The third hand is somebody's and he is not going to find out whose, and the empty column is going to sit under that heading until one of them is dead, and he did not ask anybody anything this week and is not going to.
