# Chapter 331: A Lane Off The Slade With A Bay At The Top Of It, A Door Standing Open Because Of The Weather, About A Hundred Yards Off, And Four Sentences Said Out Loud To An Empty Lane

The lane runs off the Slade at its north end and there is a bay at the top of it, and the bay is about nine foot by eleven, and there is a bench in it that is longer than the bay is wide, which is the first thing a person would notice about the place.

He is thirty-four or thereabouts, and the hand is a fair one, and the trade is to copy out other men's figures until the totals agree. Four days a season he stands on somebody's list and this is not one of the four.

This is the second day of the first week of the eighth month of the year after the year after the year after next, and it is about the fourth hour, and the weather is on, and the door of that bay is standing open because of the weather.

---

He had told himself he would come to the end of the lane and no further, and he got to about a hundred yards of the end of it and stopped there.

Rain off a roof goes sideways before it goes down and the flags of the lane take it in about four seconds. He got to within a hundred yards of it, stopped, and the stopping took no longer than the deciding did.

The gate had been shut behind him at about the fourth hour by a man of about forty who does not argue, and the shut gate was not what stopped him. Nothing that happened at that gate stopped him. **He stopped on his own account, the way a man stops reading a page he has read twice.**

From where he stood he could see into the bay, because the door was open. He could see the length of the bench and the lamp hanging above the middle of it and the far wall of the place, and he could see the light on the flags going in and out with the weather, and he did not go the hundred yards.

From where he stood the front of the bay was open to him and about four minutes of it went past standing in the rain. A bench, a lamp, a cross in chalk the colour of the wall it is on, and about nine men somewhere inside out of the weather. He read nothing and counted nothing and none of it was any use.

---

Four hundred yards further up that same lane there is a counting room with a roof on it, and on a day with any light in the weather a man at one end of the lane can see the roof of the other end of it. He has been in that room and he was not going up it this morning and he did not go up it.

What is at the far end of that lane is a foreman of fifty-one who went into a box about four years ago without anybody putting a question to her about it. The half of a sentence of hers that anybody holds is held by the man who keeps the books four hundred yards further up that same lane, and he has wanted the rest of it since the third month of the year after and has not asked and is not going to. He has never had any of it at all. The asking is not a thing that can be done from a hundred yards by a man with no office in a building that is not hers, and it is not a thing that can be done from four hundred yards either, and that is the whole of the difference between the two of them.

**Nothing in that lane would have stopped a man walking up it.** He could have done it in about seven minutes and there is not one gate between here and the door.

---

At the back of the bay there are two of seventeen, and they are on a written engagement at the rate a list is set at, and they have been at the back of it for weeks.

One of them is a girl and one of them is a reader. The reader is paid forty-five pence a day and four days a week, and what a day of that comes to depends entirely on what is set in front of her, anywhere from fourpence to a shilling.

**Neither of them was spoken to that morning, and neither of them was thanked, and neither of them was sent for, and neither of them is going to start.**

He did not go in and he did not stand at the door. He looked at the bay for about four minutes and then he went back down the lane at the speed he had come up it. He did not look at the two of them at the back of it either, because from a hundred yards he could not have told which of two people was which, and because there is no form for letting a man who has never met anybody into a bay to find out who is in it.

He kept his hands in his coat, looked for about four minutes, and went back down the way he had come. He kept his eyes off the pair at the back as well, and not out of modesty; a hundred yards off he could not have told the two of them apart.

---

On the end wall of that bay, at about the height of the third link of a chain, somebody put a mark in chalk a long time ago.

The cross means nothing. It is nobody's and it is about four years off being anybody's evidence, and the chain it stands level with is on a man, and nothing in that lane is going to turn either one of them into anything at all. He could see the mark from where he stood and he did not go up and put a hand on it, and the not going up is the same as the not going in and neither of them took any longer than the other.

---

Then he said the reason out loud, in the middle of the lane, and there was nobody in the lane to hear it.

"A person who cannot be read cannot be asked in writing. **A person who cannot be asked cannot be told anything**, and the two of those are the same thing and not two."

"The arrangement that keeps about four men in a bay out of every book in this empire is the arrangement that loses about nine sheets a year nobody can account for. **They are one arrangement and not two**, and it has been running for nineteen years and it is going to keep running after everybody in it is dead."

"**It is not a right, and it is not a method, and nobody is going to be using it.** Not me and not the four and not the man at the foot of the sheets, and I have gone at it two years to find a way to use it and there is not one."

That is the whole of it, and it took him about as long to say as a thing of that size takes. He did not say it to anybody. Nobody asked him why he was standing in a lane looking at a bay, and he did not tell anybody, and the count of things anybody has asked out loud in this matter did not move, and it is not going to.

**There is no form for carrying a question to the back of that bay**, where four men cannot make sense of a paragraph, and there is not going to be one, and the not going to be is not a decision anybody took. It is what a lane is.

It is worth setting out what the arrangement is, because what he had just said was three sentences and not a mechanism, and it took him about four minutes on the walk down the lane to put the pieces of it in a row.

Four men in that bay cannot make sense of a paragraph. Nobody has put a single question about a number to any of them in the whole of this, and that is not kindness and it is not a rule anybody made, and he has looked for the rule for two years and there is not one. **It is the other half of the same thing.** The arrangement that will not let a question reach those four is the arrangement that has kept about nine hundred sheets a year correct for nineteen years, and it has done that by not letting a question reach him either.

**He is the only person in this matter that anybody has ever been able to put a question to, and he is the one nobody has asked.** About nine of those a year go out not as the person above them wrote. The man who knows which nine cannot write one of them down, and he has now said the whole of it out loud twice at a rail at the end of a cold passage. About nine people were in that room the second time and a clerk wrote none of it, and she said out loud that writing it down would make it an order.

Nobody has ever asked the four what they would like to be asked. Nobody has ever asked the man at the foot of the sheets what he would like to be asked. **Those are the same not-asking, and the reason is that a person in both cases would have to be a person in a record, and a person in a record is a party, and being a party is how you are served.**

**Nobody has ever put a name to any of the four, and a name is the first thing a question needs.** He has thought about those four more in the last two years than about anything else in his life. There is no arrangement anywhere that would let him ask them and be refused, and being refused is the whole of what he would need, and he would take it in an afternoon.

---

He thought about going in three separate times over about four minutes and he did it none of the three times, and that is the whole of what he did on that lane, and it took less time than a sum.

Three times in about four minutes he thought about going in and he did none of them. About seven minutes of walking would have done the rest, and not one gate stands anywhere between where he stood and the door of it, and he knows the length of it to a pace because he has counted it.

Then he stood in the rain at the bottom end of it for a while, and then he went home, and he wrote out one sum before the light went for a man who cannot pay him until the spring, and it came to eleven shillings and it took about nine minutes, and that man will get it in the spring and will not know it was done in a room where nothing was lit.

Nothing was resolved and nobody was sent for and nobody was thanked. The two of seventeen at the back of that bay are not going to be asked anything this week either, and the mark in the chalk is a mark, and the hundred yards is a hundred yards.

The door of the bay would be shut by about the seventh hour by somebody who was not asked to shut it and would not be thanked for shutting it.

"Is that door open?" he said it out loud at about the sixth hour, to the wall of his own room, about the door of the bay, and the wall did not take it, and nothing in this empire lets a door be said to a person, and about four hundred yards of lane is not a short way to walk and he walked none of it twice.
