# Chapter 302: A Counting Room About Eleven Feet By Fourteen, Sixteen Books, And Three Women She Has Not Told

The counting room is at the back of a dye end in a lane in a furnace town about four hundred and thirty miles up this river, and it is about eleven feet by fourteen, and there is a bench along one wall and a window with a broken light in it and about forty books on shelves that were made for taller books.

There is a stove in the corner of the room behind the bench that has not been lit since the summer and gets lit every day of the winter by whoever gets in first.

Eleven houses off a dyers' lane keep their numbers there and none of the eleven has ever asked to see the number.

She is twenty and her name is Nell Kest and she keeps sixteen books, and fifteen of the sixteen belong to somebody else and not one of the fifteen has an office on the cover of it.

The sixteenth has her own name at the head of it and she has never once been asked what it is for.

This is the fourth day of the fourth week of the twelfth month of the year after the year after next, and the dye end has been running since about the second hour, and the broken light in that window has let the same weather in for about four years and nobody has had it out.

---

The money for nineteen days is in a weigh-house ledger about half a mile from this room and her wage is in it and her name is not under hers.

The stove behind the bench has been out since she came in and the room takes a while to admit it. Through the summer the two of them sat at one temperature all day and she never noticed, and she has noticed every morning since the first frost and has not done anything about it, because nothing in the room would let her and the lighting of it belongs to whoever comes through the door first.

Her wage has been held since the second week of the third month of the year after, and the man who holds it has come up two stairs four times and said the same three things each time, and the three things are that the ledger is closed, that the entry is not his, and that he would look at it in the spring.

She has written none of them down because there is nowhere in this building to put a thing like that and because **there is no form in this empire that pays a woman for the two days a week she spends telling another woman she is owed money, and there is not going to be one.**

A foreman off a dye end is a different man and he is about forty, and he is the only person in that city who can pick up or put down the whole of a woman's money, and he has said out loud to men who were working that he cannot say what a wage is held against, and he has said it four times and once to a boy of about nine with a slate.

He came up the two stairs this week and put a notice in a coat on the bench and did not send it, and told her to write it and keep it and not send it, and she said out loud what he ought to do instead and gave him a reason she made up in about four seconds. **A wage-day notice in a coat on a bench in a counting room says a woman is owed money, and there is nothing in this empire that can hold a notice against anybody at all**, and that is the whole of what she got out of the four visits, and she is not thanked for it and there is no form anywhere that would thank her.

She is not sorry for the four seconds and would give the same four seconds again tomorrow. She has given them about three times and the man has carried the coat away each time without a word, and a coat with a paper in it gets no more return anywhere than a coat on a hook.

---

She has decided to go and tell four women that she is not paid, and she has told one.

That was in the fourth week of the ninth month of the year after next, and it took about nine minutes on a step, and the woman she told has not said one word about it since and has not said it to anybody else, and that is not a promise and it is the best the arrangement has got.

There are three left and the going is on her own time and there is no fund for the telling and no half-day and nobody to cover the bench.

Somebody has to stand at the bench while she is at a door and there is nobody to stand at it. She has done the sum on more evenings than there are women left to do it for, and it comes out the same way every time, with a half-day in it that nobody has the standing to give.

A woman of about thirty came to her in the third month of the year after next with money in her hand and offered some of it, and she refused it, and it was the first time in about three years that she had refused anything from anybody. She gave the reason out loud and it was one sentence:

She is not going up this river this season. She has not said she is never going to, and she is the only person in this matter who knows that there is a difference between the two, and nobody has ever been in a position to establish which of the two it is.

---

**And this is the other end of something, and it is the other end of a thing that has two ends and about four hundred and thirty miles of road between them, and nobody in this building knows that the other end exists.**

He is a man of thirty-four with a fair hand and he is in a room over a chandler's shop taken by the week, and he goes through the rest of it about once in four weeks, and this week he did it too.

He has never written her name down and she has never written his, and neither of them has been told that the other one is doing it, and no form in this empire could be filled in that would join two people in two rooms in two cities who have not asked to be joined.

A practice is a count of things done and not of people, and that is the second time in about two years she has had the thought in her own head and has not put it anywhere.

There are two of it and one is a woman telling a woman on a step that she is owed nineteen days, and the other is a man writing out another man's figures so that they come out even. **Nobody in this empire keeps a column headed by anything for the two of them, and a column with a heading over it is a machine, and a machine at that scale is the only thing this empire has.** She worked that out in about four minutes at about the fourth hour and has not said it out loud to anybody, and there is a foreman in a coat who could have been told and was not.

She is not on a list. There is no list. There is no heading over either of them and there is no money behind either of them and the whole of what is between them is that both of them have said one true thing out loud in a room with nobody in authority in it.

---

The four days this week went the way four days go.

The eleven houses came in their numbers and eleven numbers went out, and a man came at about the third hour about a figure that was wrong in a ledger about half a mile off and it was wrong in the ledger and not in the room, and nobody could be told so by anybody without a form, and no form exists, and the man went away with what he came with and has not come back about it and is not going to.

A girl of about fifteen came at about the fifth hour to ask whether a number she had copied out of a book for her mother was right, and it was, and she said it was and would not be thanked and said it before she was thanked, which is a thing a person of twenty has learned to do in about three years.

After the girl had gone she thought about her for a while, and there is not much to think about and she thought about it anyway. A figure turning out right is the easiest thing in a week in the counting room, and it costs whoever says so one sentence beforehand and about fourpence of nothing.

She has been in that counting room about three years and has never once put a figure of her own into any of the sixteen.

The figures in the sixteen are all somebody else's, and the sixteenth has her name at the head of it and nothing in it but the day and the weather and what the eleven houses sent, and if that book were ever asked for it would be a book about the weather.

Nobody was thanked. Nobody was sent. Nothing was written down that was not a figure about somebody else's money.

She shut the sixteen at about the seventh hour. The fourth of them went into her bag with the other three she has no answer for, and the stove had gone out behind the bench, and the broken light in the window had gone from weather to dark without anything happening in between. **The three women she has not told are three women in eleven houses off a dyers' lane who do not know on this day that there are three of them, and no form in this empire will ever put that in front of them.** She is not going to be thanked for any of it and has not been and is not going to be, and there is not one form anywhere that calls for a person to be thanked for saying a thing out loud in a room, and there never is going to be one.
