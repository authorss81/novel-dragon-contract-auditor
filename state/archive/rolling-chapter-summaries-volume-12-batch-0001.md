**0551, the shed at the end of the lane off the Slade, and a bundle at the end of a bench. Point of view: the foreman of fifty-one.** She has been leaving the ordinary work of that shed at the end of the bench where the two of seventeen sit, about nine mornings a month, and **a girl of seventeen pushed it back three feet into the middle of it, twice, without saying a word.** The reason is a sentence she said out loud in that bay about a year ago and has not repeated, and it is hers from `chapter-0493.md`, said out loud to the flags of that shed about a girl of seventeen at the back of a bench: a girl of seventeen has not been asked one thing in about a year, is not being given the chance to turn anything down, and an offer of a second of a second would find nowhere in this empire to go with the fact that she did not want it. **The sentence is a recall and it is carried in this chapter's own words, not in the words it was first said in, and the fix pass revoiced it for that reason: the batch had returned thirty-two consecutive words of `chapter-0493.md` verbatim and the longest run of identical words between the two chapters is now five.** **She does not ask. There is no paper in this empire that asks a person of seventeen whether she would rather not, and she is not going to invent one, and she has not got a heading.** What she does is put the work where the girl can reach it. **She works out that a girl who puts a thing back has made a decision, and that a decision is a party, and that the whole of what she wanted was a place where the girl could have said no and been a girl who had said no and gone on sitting there.** On the third morning she puts the bundle back at the end, and the girl picks it up at about the next hour, and nobody is told who put it where. She comes out of that bay at the sixth hour with her hands in a worse state and does not look at them. The chain goes along under a hand that has been on it eleven years; the reader of seventeen turns a page; the girl has been asked nothing and refused nothing.

**0552, a building off the old river road, and a door left open, and a shelf. Point of view: the woman of about nineteen.** She had stopped going into the room at the back of that floor about a fortnight ago and went in on this morning to write out a week of minutes in her own hand, because the second copy had not gone in for a fortnight and it was hers to do. **She had worked out that a person who stands where things are said cannot afterwards be shown to have been somewhere they were not, and she gave up the only thing she had to get it, which was that nobody in that building had ever wanted one single thing out of her.** The door of that room has been standing open about nine mornings out of twelve for a fortnight and about one person has come through it. **The window over the shelf has had no pane in it for about four years; the paper has gone the colour of the stone; there is one thing on it with no full stop at the end of it and her employer's own name at the foot of it, and it has been there about eleven years.** She stands four foot off the woman of fifty-three and looks at it for about as long as it takes to write a day down, and does not touch it, and is not asked about it, and would not answer it if she were. She works out that touching it makes her a person who has read it and not touching it makes her a person who stood next to it, and that neither can be put anywhere and that they are the same absence. **Her span in that building is about a month, and the page now says so: the chapter previously gave the same judgement as one worked out "in nineteen years", which is her age and not a span, and the two cannot both be true of a girl who has been on that floor a month.** **She puts the bundle of the day's sheets on the sill and not on the shelf, and afterwards stands with her hand flat on the wood working out why, and finds it: she will not be a person who stood in the way of it.** The man of about sixty-one is referred to by his other hand, his pot, and the fact that he reads the top of every sheet before he signs the foot of it; the girl of nineteen is not asked one question about a number.

**0553, the ground floor of a rented house, entered from the lane above it, and a book left open on a table. Point of view: the woman of about thirty-five.** She has been taking the day-book down that lane **open** instead of shut and leaving it on the table in that passage, and it has been four days and nobody has turned a page. Four headings over four columns in her own hand and a fifth that has not been ruled in nine years, and a room in her own house that has stood empty since before the spring that she has not let. **Those two are the same kind of thing, and there is no form anywhere in this empire that will take them under one heading, and a heading is the only thing in this matter that can put two things together.** The woman at the bottom of that stair has been there about twenty years and has never gone above the second of those stairs. The exchange is four flat lines about whether the book is open and where it is, and neither of them asks the other anything. **The movement's smallest thing is said once here, in a woman's mouth, to the table: a woman who sets a thing down where another person goes has done the only part of it anybody can see, and the only part anybody can see is the putting down.** On the first day the other woman came through with a rag and looked at the table the way everybody looks at a table. On the third she stopped on the far side of it for about as long as it takes to put a coin in an apron, and did not touch it, and said nothing. **On the fourth day she shut it, going past, with the flat of her hand on the front board, without lifting it off the table.** A thing that is shut on a table by somebody else is a thing that has been answered. She came down the lane at the fourth hour and picked it up and did not open it and went back up the lane with it shut, and did not stop on the step.

**0554, the room taken by the week, and a second plate. Point of view: the protagonist, in person, and his want is visible.** There has been one plate in that room for about two years and a second one has been in the press the whole of that time with the dust on the face of it. **He has been putting the second plate on the other side of that table for about a fortnight and this morning he washed it, and putting it out washed is a different thing from putting it out.** The chair is still in the middle of that floor facing about four feet of plaster; the stool is against the wall; the coat is on the crooked nail and nothing has been out of it. The catch on that window is turned and the window is shut, and at about the first hour of every working morning there is somebody in the back of the house behind sweeping a floor. **He took the whole of that in about two minutes standing up in the last week of the last month: whatever he says in that room goes out of the window whether the window is open or shut, and he has been saying things out loud to that room for about ten years, and he has spent two years shutting the cheapest second hearer in this empire out of a room nobody else holds a key to.** **What he wanted was a room with a second person in it who had not come for him, and the cheapest one going in this city has been a turned catch and a gap about as deep as a well, at one hour of every working morning, for the whole of two years.** So he has got a plate. He washes it about four days out of seven. It was clean at the third hour and it was not clean at the fifth, and a man who washes a thing every morning on purpose acquires a second morning and a fifth and a ninth, and by the fourth week there are more mornings than there are reasons. **He did not put it back in the press and he is not going to.**

**0555, the shed at the end of the lane off the Slade, and the top of a sheet. Point of view: the reader of seventeen.** He has been at the end of that bench since he came out of somewhere else and nobody in that lane has ever wanted one single thing out of him and nobody has ever said a word to him about any part of it, and he has not broken either half of that. **He can make sense of a paragraph and is not one of the four who cannot.** He began reading the top of the sheets that came to that end of the bench — the head, the date at the head, the line at the top — about a fortnight ago, and **he has worked out that there is no form in which he is entered as the one who read the top of a sheet and none in which he is entered as the one who did not, and that a person who reads the top of one sheet cannot afterwards be put back as the one who never read the top of anything.** He read about one a day for a fortnight and found nothing at all. On the fourteenth morning he squared a sheet on the bench with the heel of his hand and stopped, and nobody asked him to. He is the only other person in that shed who has seen the girl push a bundle back three feet twice, and he has worked out that the woman who puts work at that end wants the girl to be able to put it back, and that he would rather she did not find out that she can, **and there is not one thing in this empire he could have said to anybody about any part of that.** He carried the next armful down at the same speed as the last one. He has not been spoken to this week and is not going to be thanked and is not going to be sent for.

**0556, the room taken by the week, and two mornings with no brush. Point of view: the protagonist, in person.** **The back room at the other end of that air was empty at the first hour on two mornings running, and it had not been empty at the first hour for about two years, and he did not open the window about it.** He goes through the four things a person can do about a thing they cannot account for: go to the window, which he is not going to do and has a reason he has not said to anybody; go and knock on a door at the back of that house, which means a street and a name and a question and he holds nothing and goes nowhere; wait and find out on the third morning; **and do the fourth thing, which is nothing at all, and nothing is the only one of the four there is no form for.** The brush came back at about the first hour on the third morning, at the same rate. He stood in the middle of that floor with his hand not on the catch and listened to the whole of it and worked out that he had been waiting for it since the first hour and had not known that, **and that a man waiting for a brush on boards through a shut window is a man standing in a room with a second person in it, which is the thing he wanted, and which has been happening on the other side of four foot of air every working morning for two years while he kept the catch turned.** The second plate was on the other side of the table on all three of those mornings and he washed it on two of them. He has decided that a plate is a thing that can be washed and put down and left, that nobody is coming for it, that he is not waiting for anybody, **and that those are two different things and the first one is his.**

**0557, the ground floor of a rented house, and a lid. Point of view: the woman at the bottom of a stranger's stair.** She has been at the bottom of that stair about twenty years and has never gone above the second of them. **She has been shutting the lid on that tin straight for about a fortnight, and the lid had been coming down crooked for about a month before that, and about nine years of one man's rent is in it and has not been counted in six years and she has never counted it and there is no reason on earth to count it.** A tin with a crooked lid is a tin somebody has been in a hurry with. A tin with the lid shut on it is a tin a person has done something to, **and there is no form anywhere in this empire in which a woman at the foot of her own stair is entered as the one who shut a lid.** The woman of about thirty-five comes down that lane and says four flat things about the lid and gets four flat things back, and neither of them asks the other anything. **At about the sixth hour the stair does not go up.** It has gone up and come down every working day for about two years and she knows the sound of it the way she knows the sound of her own coin on a stair. **She stands at the bottom of it, not at the foot of it — at the bottom of it, which is the thing a person stands on — and she does not go up.** The coin came under that door at the seventh hour in the ordinary way. **Nobody in that house is ever going to be told that the stair did not go up on that morning, and there is a key to the top room somewhere in that building and she is the only person who knows it is not on the board and not in that tin.**

**0558, a building off the old river road, and a mark in the dust. Point of view: the woman of about fifty-three.** She is the point of view of this chapter, which she has not been in any closed chapter of this matter. **She has been dusting the shelf under the window with no pane in it every morning for about a fortnight and going round a mark in the dust about the size of the question, and she has not wiped the mark out and has not once put her hand on the question and is not going to.** Nineteen years of minutes and she is the only person who has ever been in that book, and nothing has been put to that room in about four years. The question carries no full stop and her own name is at the foot of it in her own hand, and she put it there on purpose so that she would be a door in that room somebody could walk through, **and it is not a door, it is a name, and she has known that for eleven years.** A mark left in dust is the only thing in this empire that will keep a question exactly where it was for eleven years by accident rather than on purpose, and going round it with a cloth has made the mark sharper, and there is not one person in nine hundred buildings who could be shown having made it. **A girl of nineteen comes in with an armful at about the fourth hour and finds her standing at that shelf with a cloth, and neither of them says one word.** The girl is not going to ask her what she was doing, and she is not going to say, because saying it is asking, and she has waited eleven years for somebody to ask her.

**0559, the shed at the end of the lane off the Slade, and a second bundle. Point of view: the foreman of fifty-one.** She has begun leaving **two** bundles at that end instead of one, and the girl has taken both of them, and nothing has been said in that bay for nine mornings, and this morning she takes one of them back and puts it in the middle and the girl does not put it back. She has not come down the length of her own floor for about a fortnight. **The reason she gave herself was that one bundle is a thing that can be declined without saying anything and two bundles is not, and it took her until the fourth morning to find out that a girl of seventeen does not work that way.** A girl who pushes a bundle back has refused something and a girl who takes two bundles has agreed to something, **and the only instrument a foreman of fifty-one has got is a bundle, and it works, and it works in the wrong direction, and a bundle of work is the commonest form of not asking anybody anything there is.** She has given a girl of seventeen more to do instead of a choice and there is not one column in nine hundred buildings that a bundle at the end of a bench could be entered in. The reader at that end of the bench turned a page. The bracket was not on and would not be on until about the seventh hour, and a mark in chalk on that end wall stands at the height of the third link of a chain, and nobody in that lane has ever said what it is a mark of.

**0560, the ground floor of a rented house, entered from the lane, and a room that has not been let. Point of view: the woman of about thirty-five.** Two things she is not going to do and they are the same kind of thing, and it took her about nine years to establish that there is not a heading she is allowed to put them under. **She has begun standing in the middle of the room she has not let, nine times in a fortnight, and there are two things in it, and she has not ruled the fifth column and is not going to.** She comes down the lane with the book shut under her arm, the way it has been shut since the fourth day somebody else shut it on her table, and the four flat lines about the lid are said a fourth week running, and neither of them asks the other anything. **Standing in the middle of that room she works out that a heading over two refusals is a heading over every refusal in this empire that has not been made yet, and that she has no say in any of those, and that this is where a refusal goes when it is not a column.** She puts the second thing down where the first one was and comes out and shuts the door. The fifth column is not ruled and is not going to be ruled, and the whole of what happened on that lane that morning was about a lid.
