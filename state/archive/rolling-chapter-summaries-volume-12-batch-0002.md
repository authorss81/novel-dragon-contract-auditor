**0561, the ground floor of a rented house, and the top step at the turn. Point of view: the woman of about thirty-five.** She has stopped saying the four flat things about the lid and has begun carrying a sheet of her own down that lane, because the boards of that top step are wet and there is water coming up through them from the wall. **She puts the sheet on the boards and lifts the tin about an inch and pushes it under and squares the corners with the heel of her hand, in about as long as it takes to put a coin in an apron, and then stands on the step below the turn with her arms down and waits, which she has not done on that lane before.** The woman at the bottom of that stair comes down two steps, works the tin about a quarter of an inch, takes hold of the near corner of the sheet and pulls. **The tin does not move. The sheet comes out in a long scrape with two inches of its fold gone over wet, and is folded the other way and put back on the top step face down and squared with two fingers.** Four flat lines, and the last of them is *that is not the same as it being wanted on them*. The tin stays where it is. **The woman of thirty-five goes up the lane with nothing and has left a thing of her own in a passage she does not live in, and she has worked out that she is not going to put a sheet under anything else in that house.** Nobody is thanked and nobody asks where the sheet came from.

**0562, the landing outside a door, and a mat. Point of view: the protagonist, in person, and his want is visible.** He has carried the mat up that stair and put it by his own door and taken it up again eleven times in about a fortnight, and on the twelfth morning he leaves it where it was. It came off a cart at the end of a lane with four other things in it and he gave what he had for it. **A mat is a thing for feet. A plate is a thing for a person to eat off and there is nobody to eat off it and he has made his peace with that.** At about the first hour a coin comes under his door, put down flat, turned over with the flats of two fingers and pushed. **The mat is square and flat and where a mat goes, and the hand has about two inches to come through.** She steps on it, lifts the near corner about four inches, pushes the coin under and puts the corner back down. **He does not open the door, with his hand on it, and she says *it is on the wet boards by the stair*, *it is on my boards*, *it is on the boards, that is all*, and he says *it is on the boards. That is all it is on*, and she goes down and her boot comes off the mat last, on the step.** Cost: a mat on a landing is not his by the fourth morning. It is the landing's.

**0563, the shed on the Slade, and four mornings with nothing at the end of the bench. Point of view: the reader of seventeen.** The armful that used to stop at the end of his bench every working morning has stopped, because the woman who puts work at that end has left nothing there for four mornings. **He has worked out that a person with nothing to do at the end of a bench is a person who can be shown having done nothing.** He did everything there is: forearms on the boards, counting the links in the chain that goes along at the front, the bracket that is not on until about the seventh hour, the chalk on that end wall. That took about an hour. He had stopped reading the top of the sheets a month ago because there was nothing in the top of about a fortnight of them. **On the fifth morning the girl comes down that bench with an armful and does not stop, and sets it down nine foot further on where the top of every sheet is under about a foot of another sheet, walking, without looking at him and without a word.** He does not move any of it and reads nothing. **At the sixth hour the woman who puts work at that end comes the whole way down her own floor for the first time in about a fortnight and puts her hand flat on the wood at the front of that bench, and neither of them looks.** He is not one of the four who cannot make sense of a paragraph and he is never going to be asked which of anything he is.

**0564, the ground floor of a rented house, and two steps and a turn. Point of view: the woman of about thirty-five.** She has decided on the way down the lane that she will say it in a sentence and not a word, and she gets as far as the second step. **She says *I will take it up.* The other one, with her back half turned and a cloth in her hand, says *it goes up two steps and round a turn. It does not go up any further than that*, and when the sentence is said again she picks the cloth down, comes up the two steps, takes the tin off the top step with both hands, carries it round the turn and sets it down in the same place, on the same boards, with the same corner nearest the wall, in the same gap between the edge of the step and the skirting that has been the gap for twenty years.** She puts the lid down flat and takes a finger off it, and goes back down the two steps and picks the cloth up and goes along the passage. **She did not say no, and a thing carried up two steps and put down where it was is not a no either. It is a thing done.** She put her hand where the other one's hand had been and the tin was warm on that side and not the other. **She stands on the step below the turn, gets nothing, and works out that the problem she came down with was about her.**

**0565, the ground floor of a rented house, and the turn. Point of view: the woman of about thirty-five. THIS IS THE CHAPTER THE TURN IS MADE IN AND THE TURN IS MADE ONCE.** She comes down for the eighth week to take her own wet paper back off the top step, because the boards have been dry for a fortnight. **The other one is going the other way with a coin in her hand, picks the tin up off the top step with both hands, and stands with it against her chest, and the coin goes in over the rim without the lid coming off, because a lid shut straight does not have to be opened to get at what is inside it. She has her head bent over it and she is doing her own work.** The woman of about thirty-five is on the step below the turn with her paper crushed in her hand, looking at the boards. **And she says it out loud, in the ordinary way, at the ordinary volume, in the middle of an ordinary morning, to a woman who is counting a week into a tin: *I have not ruled that column in nine years because I do not want to rule it. That is the whole of it and I have never said it out loud to anybody in nine years. I am saying it now, and you are carrying a tin, and that is all right.*** The woman at the bottom of that stair does not turn round, does not stop, finishes at the rate she does everything, sets the tin down with the lid flat, takes her finger off the lid, and says *that is the week. That is a week of it and not the whole of it*, and goes along the passage without looking along it. **Two people in this empire were in that passage and one of them said a thing out loud and the other one was working, and nobody in this city can be shown having heard it, and there is no form in this empire that will take either of them as the one who said it or as the one who was there or as the one who stayed quiet.** She does the sum on the way up the lane: there is one room in this empire she is now in, and there is exactly one other person alive who was in it, and that person cannot be shown to have been in it either. **It cost her the only thing she had, which was that there was no room anywhere she could be put in as having said a thing.**

**0566, the landing outside a door, and a coin under it. Point of view: the protagonist, in person, and his want is visible.** He has been putting the corner of that mat flat every morning for a week and on the seventh morning the corner is down again and trodden over rather than scuffed. **A mat on a landing takes a week to become a landing's mat and it takes a corner to say so.** She comes up at the first hour, steps on the corner, lifts it, pushes the coin under. She says *it is the second week*. He says *I have it*. **She says *I have not come up for the mat*, and she does not go down, and he does not open the door and does not let go of it, and she says it again — *I have not come up for the mat, because if I come up for the mat then this is a thing about the mat* — and then *it is on a mat and it is the second week of it and I come up here at the first hour and there is a coin, and that is the whole of what I do on this landing*, and she goes down.** He wanted a second person in that room who had not come for him for about ten years and has had one on a landing twice this week who will not give him the reason he wanted, and she is right not to. **Then he works out that a brush on boards through a shut window is a person who is not coming in and a coin under a door is a person who has come and gone and is not coming in either, and that he has been treating the two as the same thing for about two years, and that a plate and a mat are about as far apart as that.** He does not take it up.

**0567, the shed on the Slade, and the front of that bench. Point of view: the foreman of fifty-one.** She has not come down the length of her own floor for about a fortnight, and this morning she does, because the girl is working at the wrong end of that bench — the end by the door, where the count is made. She is fifty-one and eleven years at the back of that bay and a class does not have to do anything to a person, it only has to leave her where it found her. **She goes ninety foot down that floor and puts the flat of her hand on the wood at the front of that bench and keeps it there, and she has worked out that the only reason she went down that floor was to be able to say that she had gone down it, and there is nothing at the front of that bench to say to.** The girl works at the end by the door with her back to forty foot of that bay and does not stop and does not turn round. **On the eighth morning she leaves a bundle at the end of that bench, having told herself on the way down the lane that a thing at the end of a bench is the ordinaryest invitation in the world and a fortnight of standing at the back of that bay has got her down to the whole of it, which was neither the work nor the girl but a thing put where a person could get at it and turn it over — and the girl comes down that bench, does not stop, does not look down, carries it on to the end by the door, and squares it with the heel of her hand.** A girl who pushes a thing back has refused something; a girl who carries it on to where she works has made no decision anybody could enter. **She is not going to ask, there is nothing to ask with, and the girl is not going to be thanked.**

**0568, a landing, and a mat on it that is not hers. Point of view: the woman at the bottom of a stranger's stair.** She has begun lifting the tin with her knee instead of with both hands and has decided not to work out why. Twenty years at the foot of that stair and not one morning on the third tread. A rent comes once a week out of a hand and has never been written down; she has never given anybody a receipt for anything; a number cut into the back of a key is the whole of what that building has ever had to say about anybody sleeping in it. **A coin comes under the door on the floor above the second stair at about the first hour and goes under a door and not into a hand, and she has been putting it there for about two years, and there is a mat on that landing.** She steps over it for a fortnight without looking down at it, and on this morning she looks down at it. **Nobody put that mat there. He is a door with a coin behind it and about two years of nothing, and he has not come down that stair in all that time, and she has not been up past the second of those stairs in all that time.** She does not go and stand on that landing. **When the woman of about thirty-five comes down that lane and says *there is a mat on your landing*, the answer is *that is not a thing you put on a landing*, and *that is a thing a man puts on a landing*, and the woman at the bottom of that stair says *it is the landing's now and it was the landing's by the fourth morning and you did not have to be told that by anybody.* The tin has not moved in a week. She will not be asked about the mat or the tin or the wet boards or any part of it.**

**0569, the shed on the Slade, and the end of that bench at a quarter of an hour before the lane wakes. Point of view: the girl of seventeen, and it is the first time in this matter that she has been the point of view of anything.** She has been coming in a quarter of an hour early for four mornings and standing at the end of that bench, and there has been nothing at the end of that bench for all four of them. **Nobody has asked her one thing in about a year and nobody has ever wanted one single thing out of her, and she has worked out what that costs: a person who has never been given a thing cannot afterwards be shown to have refused one.** She wanted the bundle, and she carried it past anyway four mornings running, and she has worked out that wanting it and carrying it past are both true and that the second is the one anybody could see. On the fifth morning **the reader of seventeen comes in behind her with an armful and does not stop and does not put it down at the end where she is standing and carries it on past her to the end by the door, because he is the only other person in that shed who knows she can put a thing back and he would rather the woman never found out.** Neither of them says one word and neither is going to, and there is no form in this city in which a boy of seventeen is entered as the one who kept a bundle off the end of a bench, nor a girl of seventeen as the one who wanted it put there. **Nobody is going to be told that she had come in early.**

**0570, the ground floor of a rented house, and a top step at the turn. Point of view: the woman of about thirty-five.** She has been saying true things out loud to an empty room for about a fortnight — standing in the middle of the room at the back of her own house that she has not let since before the spring — **and it has cost her the only protection she had and has bought nothing whatever, because a room with nobody in it is a room with nobody in it and the whole of the instrument is the second person and the second person has to be working.** She comes down that lane at the third hour on the tenth week of it. The tin is on the top step. Four flat lines: *it has been nine days*, *it has been nine days*, *you have not said anything different*, *I have not said anything different*. **She cannot work out whether the tin went over the rim on that morning because the other one was counting out a week's money or because somebody nine years of not saying anything was talking in her own passage, and both of those are inside about half an hour of her having said it out loud, and there is no form in this city that separates one of them from the other, and the only way either could come to an end is if the woman at the bottom of that stair told her.** She is not going to ask her. **And she is going to do it again, and not in that passage and not in an empty room, and in any room in this city where a person is working and nobody wants one single thing out of her, and she will not know whether it took and will never know, and she is not going to stop to find out.** The cost is small and it is hers: from now on there is exactly one room in this empire she is in, and she is going into it on a working morning to be in it, and nobody in that house is ever going to be told that.
