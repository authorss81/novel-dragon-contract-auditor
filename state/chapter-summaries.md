# Chapter Summaries

Per-chapter detail for the rolling summary. Two to five sentences each. Newest batch last.

## Volume 01, Batch 0001 (Chapters 1–10) — The First Correction

### Chapter 0001 — The Summary

The seventh of the month, late-afternoon inheritance docket in the Auremar Roll Hall. Marek finds a third witness notch on the Venn clerk's summary that does not exist on the witness copy, and a filed correction with no authority attached that substitutes "the present holder" for Aldren Venn's name. A witness line hung off the oldest docket entry names Mara Venn as the household witness, which is the entry nobody reads. He asks Tamsin Rook to make a private comparison copy, then carries the slip withholding the summary to the Table: Pell refuses to countersign it on an evening docket, on the grounds that the next clerk to open the file will find his name on it. Marek keeps the summary out of the packet himself, sends three things instead of four to the courier, and stands in the cold yard with an unsigned slip in his coat and a night to decide whether to be on the river at all. Stage 0 only; he gains a preserved copy and a question, not authority.

### Chapter 0002 — The Work That Is Named

The eighth, at the Reed Cut channel and the Venn farm. Tavi Venn works the gate linkage and refuses to discuss inheritance in the lane. Mara Venn, from the step and not the kitchen, dates the original to the second month of the year four years back, gives her account — she witnessed it, she never signed a continuation, and a young clerk brought a paper to the field in the sixth month that she refused — and keeps four years of household figures in a wax tablet. Sivra Oris states publicly at the gate that the field-side upkeep was Aldren's from the day he signed until the day he died and the gate and navigation duty is hers, refusing both readings. Tamsin goes ahead to the court; the deadline is fixed: gates inspected on the eleventh, winter-seed notices issued on the fourteenth.

### Chapter 0003 — The Clerk's Window

The eighth, Tamsin's POV at the Mosswake Roll Court. Ivet Sarn refuses to reopen settled precedent and gives her real reason, recorded by Tamsin with date, time, witnesses, and the two inches of hand, pinned up publicly. Told to write the reason down, Sarn adds one thing and says it once: it took her four days to open a file in the fifth year, and on the fourth day the relief rolls could not be certified. Tamsin writes *four days* under the date and does not ask again. Tamsin then indexes the received tray and finds an unfiled fourth-month Dunn summary carrying the same four lines, with Nessa Dunn, fifteen, in the surety line. Nessa has signed nothing and has been told at the gate that she is going to owe it. The Venn packet goes back down the river that evening with the summary left out of it, by the morning courier, because a packet left in a district tray over a flood weekend is a packet the Table certifies on Monday out of habit; the unsigned slip is in Marek's coat.

### Chapter 0004 — The Cabinet

The ninth, at the Notaries' Table in Auremar. Waiting to be seen, Marek turns over the back of the correction and finds a flat, even certificate impression and a broker's fee entry naming Candlewright Lane, authorised by a district office with no business doing so; he writes the address down twice and understands he has a name and no use for it. Pell concedes the correction is probably bad and argues that reopening a flood-season file costs the district eleven hundred households a late winter; he offers to certify this week and compare later, and explains that a comparison cannot be had by a man who is not in the room when the sleeve comes out. Tamsin puts the Dunn copy on the table openly rather than in a drawer, and Pell reads it three times. Marek leaves a written comparison request on the desk; a board notice then records a sealed administrative hold on the Venn file on a reference that carries no office name, not to be enforced, removed, or assigned.

### Chapter 0005 — The Next Household Worker

The ninth, evening, Nell's POV in the Greyfen dye-house. Ostyn Vare reads the continuation clause aloud, honestly, and the clause makes a departing worker's tool debt answerable by "the next household worker." Marek arrives intending to remove the document and is refused. Nell finds the four-line Venn summary in his satchel — the third line of it, the one carrying the substituted guarantor language, being the sentence she recognizes from the wage schedule six inches from her elbow — and forces him to state what he knows, then demands the draft he has been hiding: an invalid proposed exception that recites a comparison he never made. Tamsin admits she found it in the wastebasket and refuses to work beside him until he discloses everything. Nell's terms: he shows her anything he writes before he writes it, and she establishes that the concealment is three days old, not three weeks.

### Chapter 0006 — The Emergency Stair

The tenth, with the closing beat on the eleventh. Marek learns what the hold costs: it saves the field from seizure and forbids the Venns from assigning the crop, so the family cannot feed itself, and the field-side duty is being done unpaid by Tavi because no one else will touch it. Magistrate Leda Rell offers a closed order that would preserve the grain and release seed tonight, founded on the corrected file and without the family in the room. Marek asks for a public rehearing on the twelfth instead and accepts the cost. He hands Tamsin the whole file and asks Mara and Tavi to decide whether to testify; Tavi testifies about the linkage and Mara about the paper offered on the field, and Tavi then names the thing she actually wants — the gate reset and the west bank work put on the water office in writing, with the household expressly not required to do the field-side, because she has cut that ditch unpaid since the fourth month and knows the lane will read the work as acceptance. At the clerk's window after hours, Sarn sends her own rider to Auremar on the court's custody slip and says she will hold whatever comes back until the twelfth and not before the hour. On the eleventh the witness order goes up on the board, and Tamsin finds it dated the ninth and drawn at eleven.

### Chapter 0007 — The Order on the Board

The twelfth, Tamsin's POV on the morning of the hearing. She stops the court before the applicant opens by showing that the posted witness order is a third issue the register does not have, with the widow's name struck through in raking lamplight and the broker's certificate put in front of her. Pell objects that a copyist's memory is not evidence, and withdraws his objection about the provincial hold while keeping the rest. Rell reads the face of the hold aloud and the First Seat's fire-and-water device is named in open court for the first time. Marek asks the court rather than the Table to direct that the leaf be produced; Sarn produces it at ten out of her own strong box, in a Table sleeve with the Table's seal on the wax, enters it in the minute book as received into the custody of the court, and demands the applicant's written application as protection for herself. Garren Pyle's certificate is shown to be a cold press mark, not a true seal, and to carry the copied third notch again. Rell issues a limited audit of the Venn file, named and witnessed, and warns that whatever she says this afternoon will be copied by clerks who do not know Marek's name.

### Chapter 0008 — The Sentence the Court Wrote

The twelfth, afternoon. Marek argues from the leaf that the instrument names a person and requires a signature from anyone assuming a continuing duty. Rell refuses to narrow the public-necessity sentence, because a signature asked for in February is not a signature. At the recess she reads the sheet Tavi asked for on the step of the Venn house on the evening of the tenth and says *that is a small thing and it is the whole case*, and tells Marek not to be thanked for it in front of the lane. She rules at twenty past three in five terms: Tavi's personal debt void; the field preserved until the second month, when seven years from the signing are up; Sivra Oris's gate and navigation duty unaffected; a new signature required of any successor; and the bank works and gate reset on the district water office, with the household expressly not required to perform the field-side upkeep. Exercising the audit, Marek receives the first Roll-Answer, which is accurate about the record the court has just made and names the present holder of the field rather than the girl whose debt was voided; he pays in ash taste, counterpressure, a headache that persists, and a temporary inability to lie about one named obligation. The public-necessity sentence is read at two o'clock and in the same minute the registrar files the long-unfiled Dunn summary on the authority of it, which is before the terms are ruled; and Nessa Dunn asks the bench whether she has just been made a debtor by another name.

### Chapter 0009 — What the Lane Made of It

Tamsin's POV over the four days after the twelfth, at a trestle table. The ruling mutates in the lane into "the holder pays," which is the opposite of the fourth term, and Tamsin corrects it in public and tells the lane to keep the copies in drawers rather than wait for her. Mara asks about the spring and gets an honest answer: until the second month, not security; and when she then asks whether anything left in the file can come back for them, Marek cannot give her the reassuring sentence, Tamsin answers in his place, and she writes the unanswered question in her own book. Orra Dunn is afraid a hearing will expose the whole household, and receives the date of the twenty-third and a separate sitting requested in the register. Nell reads her own clause aloud, signs a new wage schedule with a visible exit clause that the guild has not yet accepted, and sits on the third stool. Odile Fenn refuses to examine Tavi while a guarantor claim is open. Tamsin collates the twenty-six copies — nine her own, six from a Greyfen copyist, four from the hooded woman, three from Ivo Harker's tin, and four from two unnamed men off the bank who argued their way to the right reading — and finds a faded red running stitch with a sequence of small non-letter marks in the margin of one of them, on a leaf that was otherwise blank, and states plainly that she does not know what it is. A court runner summons them: the exhibit's chain is being opened, and a sealed First Seat page with a thread in its fold has been slipped in behind the memorandum.

### Chapter 0010 — The Form

The seventeenth and eighteenth, in Auremar. Marek arrives on the seventeenth with the headache still in his skull and the ash taste still in his mouth, and finds that he can no longer lie about the guarantor, which he proves twice on a quay. He stopped at Mosswake on the way down and Perrin Ashe, the penny doctor at the river stair, put two fingers on his eyes and told him the reading had not let go of him, and to come to a person before a book. Under a joint order, Ruhl Dunnet states that he may take a person but not a shop, and only once the paper is safe, so Tamsin inventories Corvin Dray's office over two days with the eleven waiting clients sent out to the yard; on the second morning a man who gives his name as a firm offers to have three documents of the sealed exhibit released before it is opened, free and unsigned, and says a fourth can be taken from the woman holding a copy in the Venn lane; Marek and Dunnet refuse together and have the offer, including the attempt to reach the woman named in the twelfth, written into the book with the hour. The ledger is a price list: sixty-one copied certificates and ninety-eight sold witness sequences at four shillings, across Venn, Dunn, and two provincial files at Glasswater and Emberwatch. Corvin admits selling a mark and a date and refuses to name the four clients who write the sentences. The chain reveals a four-paragraph printed form with a series number and a print run requiring flood-season continuity filings to be entered in the routine column and not itemised. Dunnet arrests Corvin only after the ledger is copied, at the sixth hour. Marek writes the nine-paragraph audit request by hand, argues with Tamsin over whether to name Glasswater and Emberwatch, and gives in to her; Examinations takes custody of the request before the lamps go out, without deciding scope, and the intake clerk writes the file number on Tamsin's copy in ink and initials it.

## Volume 01, Batch 0002 (Chapters 11–20) — What a Ruling Costs

### Chapter 0011 — The Schedule

The nineteenth and twentieth, at Reed Cut. Hallis Dren needs the fifth term's works actually done under the water office's own flood authority, and he has written the sheet himself and will not have it posted from an applicant's word, because his certification and his mother's canal house are both on it and because the reserve is nineteen sacks short and the rate falls in the fourth month hardest on the smallest holdings. Marek gets it nailed to the gate rail with the memorandum number, the date, and Dren's name, and holds one end of the cord for twenty minutes in silence. The crew arrives on the twentieth under a foreman who reads the sheet three times and calls it a good sheet; Tavi Venn has been in the ditch since first light, is made to stand clear, watches her own ditch empty, and hands her bar back at noon. The fifth term works. The lane decides she took the ditch, out loud, kindly, from two women bringing soup, and Marek cannot say the one sentence that would correct it because the sentence about the guarantor does not come, and thirty people count the silence. He tells Tavi the true reason — that he has no standing to advise her either — and she says it is the worst sentence anyone has said to her in a year and the first one that was true. He writes to Tamsin and adds that the lane's version is going to spread and that he has not stopped it, because stopping it is how it gets into a record as his opinion.

### Chapter 0012 — Until Full Review

The twenty-third, the separate sitting in the Dunn matter, in the river courtroom with the fixed benches roped out. Orra Dunn says in open court that she does not want her household named anywhere and then asks for the opposite, because a clerk in the second row has been writing since they came in and she wants one piece of paper in the matter written by somebody who is not the applicant. Nessa, fifteen, says she signed a school paper, a hoe, and a seed-share paper, wants to be examined for the seed trade, and will not sign anything. The registrar admits the summary was his own hand and that he entered it at two o'clock under a sentence read at two o'clock. Marek states in open court that he has no offer and asks for three things, expecting one. Rell postpones the guarantor duty with a date and refuses the bar on enforcement, and says out loud that a postponement is worse for that household than a refusal would have been and that she cannot give them a refusal because the sentence is hers; she then directs, on the record and dated the twenty-third, that this court will not again apply that sentence on its own motion without a separate hearing and the signature of a named person who has been heard. In the corridor, Hallow Parrow of the Seeders' Aid Fund asks whether it is the end of it, and Marek cannot say the comfortable sentence and instead tells him the true one, which Parrow values. Orra tells Rell she was right about the cost and came anyway, and says she did it because a thing in her own mouth can be looked at and a thing only in a creditor's book cannot. The date is now a number travelling to a factor at the speed of a river boat.

### Chapter 0013 — The Register

Orra Dunn's POV, the twenty-fourth onward. She does the sum on a board that can be wiped: four creditors, a floor worth four hundred and eighty clean and two hundred and ten with a pending duty on it, and two hundred and seventy that is not a market but a court saying a number out loud on a Friday. She goes to all four. Grice will hold to the first week of the fourth month if Nessa is taken off the paper in front of him; Kell's mother will sign that the floor is not security; and Emrys Tale has already sold his paper on the floor to Grice for a hundred and six and says so in his own door, having decided the certainty was worth more than the ninety-two, and tells her Grice will serve it on the forty quarters of rye in the second month. A neighbour of forty-one years' standing walks half a mile in the rain to give her four pounds of tilling seed she does not need and cannot refuse, and that, not the court, is the day. On the third day Orra asks the court window for a public register of pending duties, against everything she said on the twenty-third, because a duty that exists only in one creditor's hand cannot be contested by anybody but that creditor. Sarn starts it, charges a shilling, refuses to waive it, and writes Nessa's refusal into it. Nessa asks for the entry to go on the wall, not in the board, because the seed office sends a boy round every Monday. A woman of about thirty-five in a river court's grey coat gets off the packet boat on the fourth day with a bundle of papers that are not hers and will not give her name.

### Chapter 0014 — Four Days

Ivet Sarn's POV, the thirtieth of the ninth month into the fourth of the tenth. The unnamed woman in the bundle coat comes to the window again and Sarn refuses to say whose file it is at a window, promises to look it up, writes *offered and declined* with the hour, and finds a Mosswake file from the fourth month of the fifth year carrying the same four lines. She asks for twenty minutes with the magistrate for something four years old that is not her business, and asks Marek to sit down and not speak, which is a request he recognises as her asking him to watch her not protect herself. She produces a fourth-month relief tally from the fifth year with her own nineteen-year-old hand on it: *Seed allocation, upper district, held on the fourth day, docket open. File: Sallow, R., barn term, upper. Reopened by me. Held.* Four days, the same four lines, a minor in the surety line, and a docket three weeks from a relief certification, so that going in meant the whole upper district ate in the wrong weeks. She would do it again and says so. She dictates that the record carry the reason and the consequence together, that the Sallows be told first and by her in her own words, and that Marek be sent to the bottom of the lane, where he stands in the rain for five hours and is not allowed up. Rennie Sallow's answer is that he had thought about nothing else for a fortnight and can now stop, and that he wants the eleven pounds recorded as his and not the court's, and that he wants a place where it says a clerk put a thing in a drawer, and that he wants that place to be the window he has been coming to for forty years. On the fourth of the month, with the magistrate in the chair, the record takes both together, and Marek says out loud the thing nobody else will: the wording in the Sallow file is the wording in the Venn correction, four *years* older, made in this building, so this is a practice and not a fraud, and there is no wrong party in the room for a minute book to rule on.

### Chapter 0015 — The Second Box

Nell's POV, the ninth of the tenth month onward, in the Greyfen dye-house workroom and Marl Crenn's assessor room. Crenn is frightened of the exemption form's second box: a form has been read with another form, the box is full, and a dyers' house where people can be named loses its certification — thirty-one years, four houses, about forty-one apprentices — and the thing on the bench in that building is a court ruling in twenty-six copies, which is not a ruling about dye-houses. She will not tell Nell that taking it off the bench empties the box, because the bench is swept every fourth day and a ruling comes back. Vare says out loud that if Nell signs nothing he is the man who had a document in his hand for three weeks and used it against her, and would rather lose the house's exemption trying. The amendment names the departing worker, sets out the sum, gives eight weeks, and carries the balance and the cost of recovery by a **Greyfen Dyers' Tool Fund** and by no household and no worker; it has to be a body with a name, a keeper, a book, and accounts that survive the four of them. Crenn signs, dates, and says plainly that she is not signing because she believes it but because a form that has been read will be read again and she would rather it be read the second time with her name on it. She then warns Nell that the clause is good and is a *form*, will be printed with a number, and travels. The four houses on the lane raise nine pounds a quarter between them; the man on the rack goes round asking foreman's signatures; two sign because they are frightened and the Caudle house has said nothing, and the levy comes out hardest of a woman on four and six with four children and no other trade. Nell takes the number on the card and goes herself, because a foreman walking round a lane is the guild speaking.

### Chapter 0016 — The Permission

Tamsin's POV, Auremar, into the eleventh of the tenth month. The four paragraphs of the First Seat form, recopied large with the paragraphs numbered, undo her: paragraph four says it is not a direction as to validity and gives no authority and takes none away. It is not a crime, it is a permission, and the correct thing to do with it is administrative. Its four paragraphs are each ordinary in a flood season with four hundred and sixty rolls to certify, and what they do is remove the last person in the chain who might have looked: the answer to *I have heard something about my own barn* stops being an argument and becomes *the court is not obliged to examine this individually*. Nine days of two wrong options. Publishing it is an attack on a specific court, and the court is the one that gave a widow her field; not publishing it means families in three districts find out from a district office on a quarterly return. Pell Vey asks, in the Chancery yard, how many of thirteen named people he has, and is refused; he then says one true thing, that he did not know about the form, found out three weeks ago from a boy with a tray, and would not have acted on it in the eighth month, and explains his own worry — a form with a run on it is a form about a business, and somebody in it will eventually need to know which thirteen names are with a copyist. Tamsin writes it down in full and underlines that he says he did not know and did not act, and does not tell Marek. On the eighth day she opens the chain in front of Dunnet and the boy, sees the second sealed page, does not open it, and gets the fact of it entered in the inventory book with the hour and three sets of initials. On the ninth she sends copies of the form to all thirteen named people with a plain warning and a date, and does not publish, and does not ask Marek, and tells him afterwards in one sentence. He opens his mouth to argue, nothing comes, he says "No," and asks for the whole of it including the parts where he would have been right. In the last section, on the eleventh of the tenth month, Hallis Dren receives an ordinary quarterly return, answers the fourth and fifth questions honestly — one, and yes — and sends it four hundred miles without ever learning what it was.

### Chapter 0017 — The Mark

The eighteenth and nineteenth of the tenth month, Candlewright Lane and the district house. A month after the arrest the room is empty, the chandler wants his stair back and asks for his trade loss to be written down, and four of the eleven clients have shut: one shuttered a counting office against investigators who were not constables, one sold his plates, one left the district, one stopped answering letters. Three cannot work, and the order that protects paper, attendance, and hearing does not touch a person's competence, so Dunnet minutes that it does not. Bryn Mawer, seventeen, Dray's copying girl and not among the thirteen, asks to be examined in the copying; Marek puts her in a supplementary sheet, read by her before he sealed it, on the ground that a name and a date on her side of the paper is worth a fortnight of a tired man's patience, and she asks him not to record that she was frightened. Brammel gets the green box back in a leather case with a receipt, his own half of the copy, and the district's inventory stays in the district store; he cannot get his name out of item nine, and reads the return before signing it. Dunnet carries the district's offer: Dray's plea on the local counts, the Venn correction struck and the file restored complete, the request narrowed to Auremar and the form series dropped. Marek refuses, gives three reasons on the record, and separately has the plea refused and entered with a trial set; Dunnet tells him he must tell Mara Venn himself. Dray's statement, taken in a cell with a magistrate, a clerk and Tamsin Rook, is eleven pages with no person's name in it: scale matrices are bought from a furnace-side counting house on a row by the Kiln sheds, the owner appears in the ledger as a mark — a filled square, a bar, a nick — against thirty-eight lines in the *carry* column in three districts, and Dray never went up the stairs because looking would have cost him four years of trade. He makes his own not-looking into evidence, in his own hand, and Tamsin writes it down unkindly. A statement that accuses no one cannot be received against a distant district, so Marek signs a narrowing confining the request to Auremar with the two provincial entries reserved and not abandoned, tells Tamsin in the same hour, and has the time written beside his signature. He ends on the fact that the arrest changed nothing about the form and that Emberwatch is four hundred and forty miles away and unwritten.

### Chapter 0018 — The Rota

Nell's POV, the twenty-third to the thirtieth of the tenth month, between the Greyfen vats and the Venn lane. Three sums and a fourth on the flat of a dye vat: nine pounds a quarter across four houses, and where it lands, and the Caudle share, and the winter — the reserve is spent, the provincial hold is still on, and a household that is fighting has no hours, so Orra Dunn's forty quarters went into the upper barn two days late and eleven days late in the fourth month is the difference between seed in the ground and seed in a sack. On the twenty-third Marek stands in the workroom doorway and is made to take the broken stool at the end and copy, on the condition that he says the same eleven sentences to anybody who comes in; he copies four hours and is for the first time in eleven days not interrupted. Tamsin's four-line letter gives the table to the lane; Nell refuses it twice in public and then, on the twenty-seventh, nails a rota to the table leg naming Ivo Harker, the seed-barn copyist entered as a role because a rota can hold a person who has no name in a book, Coll Fenner, who has finally given his name, and Wenna Sallow, Rennie's daughter, who kept the copy of the tally. On the twenty-eighth a child of seven is sent by Bettany Skail to ask that a paper be read out loud before anybody signs, and the fourth term is used in the lane by nobody anybody trained, and Nell has it entered without the child's name so that she can point at the list in four years' time. Mara Venn brings out the wax tablet and makes it the household accounts. On the twenty-ninth Nery Caudle signs the amendment in her own yard with two pounds and five a quarter of her own wage in it, having had the terms written on the back in her own words. On the thirtieth Wenna Sallow asks, at the table, whether a duty stands against the Sallows, and Marek says the true thing — that he does not know and will not tell her mother there is not — and the sentence comes out of him without difficulty for the first time in nine weeks. In the workroom that evening he tells Nell about the reading: it stopped, it is not a cure, he will not pay a penny to Perrin Ashe to be told the word *well*, the headache went on the twenty-sixth, and he minds losing the shield more than losing the headache. He tells her about their mother — the Chancery roll with a blank where the name should be and a word meaning *struck*, a copy in his father's tin since he was seventeen, a disputed file with their names on it that is neither enforceable nor free, and a sealed page he will not open in a workroom on a Thursday. Nell refuses the ninth month without forgiving it, tells him what it cost her to lie to a vicar about the levy, and gives him the sentence: someday he will be standing in a room and she will be behind him and will need to have been told first. The two letters arrive at the table at the fourth hour, addressed to nobody in particular, and the second is not signed.

### Chapter 0019 — Itemised

Tamsin's POV, the third of the eleventh, in the copying alcove. The left letter came off the Mosswake landing on the thirtieth of the tenth on river-court copying paper with the counterfoil edge still on it, signed *P. Ostrow*; the right one is a single sheet of good laid stock, a true copy of an endorsement, addressed to *whoever is doing this*, with no letter and no signature. Both carry the same four lines, filled in, and beneath them the public-necessity sentence in the exact words of the twelfth of the ninth — seven weeks and three days — and neither is written the way a court writes a thing: no semicolons, no *save as aforesaid*, the words in the order a person sets down what is supposed to go in a box. Both filings were **itemised** — in the individual column, with a number of their own — so the First Seat form's rule failed in at least one, and the reason is either that the form never reached that court or that somebody read it and refused it, and nobody can tell which from the paper. The Emberwatch instrument is a **dyers' house**: the four lines are on a craft term, a second instrument in the house names a person, and the exemption rule means the first must be read with the second, four hundred miles away, and Tamsin can only see it because a woman in Greyfen read the same form to her in a workroom. Perry Ostrow, thirty-five, Glasswater copying room, is two days in the alcove upstairs: she entered the endorsement into the filing book on the third of the tenth, told nobody at home, posted the sheets to the lane on the twenty-ninth, came up the river herself on the first, and gave her name in four minutes on the strength of a promise that Mosswake would enter her letter in the record with her name on it and write back; her husband is blind since the spring, when he read the summary at the top of the delta landing, and she said the same sentence to Sarn at a Mosswake window a month earlier. A Glasswater miller has asked whether the cut is security and has written to Osric Lave in Greyfen, who wrote to the Mosswake court about the Dunn barn; Tamsin puts that in ink in a minute book rather than in a lane. Then the trick: a clerk of a district court may admit an exhibit on her own authority, exhibits are copied to the parties on the day of admission, and a copy that has gone to a party cannot be un-copied. Tamsin wrote to Sarn on the second, told Marek on the third, let him strike a sentence out of the application and sign it, sent it, and told him she had enclosed the Glasswater file without asking — wrongly, on purpose, in one thing, and asked for the time on it. Then she said out loud that she is frightened, because the thing is bigger than a widow and she is a copyist with a stool.

### Chapter 0020 — Nine Hundred and Forty

Marek's POV, the eighth and ninth of the eleventh, in an eleven-foot passage behind the third gallery and the second-floor Received Room. The Forms Received rack is not locked, because a locked rack says *this is worth stealing*, and it is indexed by the office that sent each thing rather than by what the things are, which is why the answer is in the building and was never found. Tamsin has been on the floor of the passage for three days with thirty-one sheets fanned out. Sarn's note reports the two provincial exhibits admitted on the fourth on her own authority, copies issued the same day, and underneath the stamp a note that she put them in before she told the magistrate and has left a paper on the bench about it. The sheet is **Model Endorsements for Continuity Filings (Flood Seasons), series MC/FS**, sealed by the **Standing Office of Provincial Continuities**, distributed to all district courts and landing offices holding a flood docket and to be filed with the standing instructions and not with the papers; base issue of the second month of this year, run five hundred and sixty; amendment sheet dated the twenty-second of the ninth month, run **nine hundred and forty**, carrying a form of words in which the third line is the Mosswake sentence verbatim under a note reading *Adopted from a provincial court, the ninth month. A court may adopt this wording or any other.* Ten days after a ruling, from a public copy. The First Seat form fits nowhere: it is a quarterly *return*, it does not say what the answers are, and it works by making the honest answer small, not by making it a lie — which is why Dren could answer one and yes and tell nobody. Two documents, two offices, four hundred miles of river, and no document anywhere saying the seat issued the form because the office printed the circular. The four lines predate the Sallow file by four years and the first issue of the series is not in this building. Marek writes to Rell and does not send her, and Tamsin says the real reason out loud for him; then, at the eighth hour, he files a two-page supplementary request naming the series, the base issue, the amendment sheet, both runs, the distribution line, and asking who signed the sheet. Nevin Trost of Examinations, tired and not an enemy, wants the provincial exhibits back because the request was narrowed to Auremar and two provinces are stapled into a file that does not mention them; Marek makes him take a thirty-day extension and write the reason in the box. The seventeen-year-old's supplementary line is left in on purpose. The unproduced second sealed page is asked for and refused, and the refusal goes in the form with his name against it. The last thing is an unsigned offer from a man who is not the head of the office: certify the Mosswake practice, empire-wide, for six months with a review in the fourth month, and the request, its preservation demand, and the pursuit of the form series and the circular all close. Marek does not answer, and understands that the thing he wanted at twenty-four and the thing he wants now are the same offer in two coats.
