# VOLUME 10, CHAPTERS 0461 TO 0470, BATCH 0002, MOVEMENT TWO — TEN CHAPTER SUMMARIES

**0461. The Counter At The Top Of Four Flights, The Fifth Time Up It, And A Sentence Said To The Boards Instead Of To Him.** Marek Kest comes up the four flights for the fifth time in his life, at the fifth hour of the fourth day of the third week of the fifth month, and puts nothing on the boards. He came up wanting two things and the chapter names both: a heading over his own name, which no column in that building has had since a bar came off him on an application of one line he wrote himself; and the fourth item on the wall board, which is in Marn Ottery's own hand, went up about four years ago, and none of the three people who work that floor can account for. **Marn Ottery refuses both and then says the sentence this volume exists to say, out loud, to the boards and not to him** — a second holder is a person who can be shown holding a thing and is not a party to it, fourpence is nobody's to put a hand over, the words stop being hers the moment they are in type, and nothing handed out in that room has ever been called back. She separates it in the same breath from the three times she has said the shape of a thing in that room, so the count of three is untouched and the new sentence is not a fourth of it. **The clerk of about twenty-nine then gives the price of saying a thing out loud: a person who has stood in a room while a sentence like that was said is a person who can be shown having stood in it, and she is not writing it down.** About four hundred go out of the case through that morning, none of them is asked what it is for, a courier is not told what is in the satchel. He goes down with neither of the two things he came for and with a third thing that was not in the room when he came into it, and the last line is that from the fourth hour of that morning there is a woman at that counter who could be shown saying the sentence. Nobody is thanked, nobody is sent for, and the fourth item is still in her own hand.

**0462. Two Joined Tables To Herself, A True Thing Said Aloud To The Room, And Four Words At The Top Of A Page.** The point of view is the clerk of about twenty-nine at the far end of two joined tables, about four years, not a lawyer, who said once in a month that is gone that she would not say a second thing and has not. This week the two tables are not joined to a third one and the far end is hers alone from the second hour. **Tamsin Rook says the true half out loud and then the other half, standing at the near end with her hand flat on the case, and the other half is the cost: a person who has said a thing out loud in a room is a person who can be shown having said it, no form anywhere in this empire has a line for the person who was in the room and none has a line for the person who was not, and that is why nobody in that building is ever going to be shown anything or told anything, and that is the arrangement and not a courtesy.** The clerk does not answer it, does not agree, does not disagree, and writes none of it down; the not-writing goes on at exactly the rate it has always gone on, and the reason the rate did not change is that she has never once written down a thing anybody said in front of her. A man of about thirty-four puts fourpence on the boards and is handed a blank; a courier leaves a folded thing and is told nothing; a man of about twenty-six stands at the near end for an hour and buys nothing and is told he does not have to. She shuts the second table at ten minutes to the sixth and there is no form in this empire in which a clerk is entered as the one who said nothing, wrote nothing, agreed with nothing and disagreed with nothing in one morning. The closing line is that the four words she writes every day at the top of that page were the only thing on it at the end of the day.

**0463. The Sixth Time Up Those Four Flights, Two People Left On That Floor, And A Thing With One Holder In It.** The point of view is Marek Kest, in person, at about twenty minutes to the sixth hour, and the other two clerks went down the stair an hour ago and the case is in and the boards are down to a foot and a half. **Marn Ottery is putting the four items back up in the eleven-year places and stops with the fourth in her hand while he is looking at the wall, and the want is in the chapter: he wanted two things and one of them was the fourth of those four and the last six times he came up that stair he came up it for that.** She puts it in its place without turning round and says the other half of the sentence, and the other half is about holders: a thing with one holder can be stopped by stopping the holder, and stopping a holder is done by finding the person and not by finding the paper; a thing with two holders can only be stopped by stopping both, and neither of the two can be shown holding it; a copy is not the thing and a holder of a copy is not a party to anything, and this is not a safety, it is not a rescue, and it does not put one person anywhere safer than they were that morning. **The cost lands on him and on nobody else: she does not know what is in that coat, and what she does know is that he has not opened it in front of her on any of the six visits, and a man carrying nothing does not do that, and there are two of them in there. A leaf with a line struck out of it has one holder and the one holder is him, and it is the only one of the two things on him that anybody could ever get at, and getting at it would not be done with paper.** He puts his hand on the pocket and does not open it and nothing comes out of it. He goes down the four flights with both things in the coat and nobody counted them, and nobody was stopped, and the fourth item is still the one none of the three of them can account for.

**0464. A Form On A Board By A Door, A Man With A Chain Across The Front Bench, And Half A Sentence In A Doorway.** The point of view is Halla Wray, fifty-one, a foreman standing inside a box that a class put her in. The lamp on its bracket is not lit and will not be before the seventh hour and no day of the week will get it lit earlier. She takes the form off the board in the doorway and says the first half of a sentence she has never finished and it stops where it always stops. **The girl is not given the chance to say no about anything this week and nobody in that shed put an offer or a condition to her, and the one time something could have been said to her that she would have had to answer, the man at the front bench said it to the floor. The reader of seventeen turns a page and nobody says one word to him and he is not going to start.** The man of about fifty-five with the chain reads her from about four foot away and tells her the flat thing: a holder who can be put back into a class is a holder who is already a party to it, the class is the box and the form is the lid, the lid does not have to be shut today, the form does not have to be filled in this week and the form only has to exist, and a person who can be re-entered cannot be given a copy of anything without the copy being evidence — **and the new specific is that a second of a form is not an exit, because the second carries the same heading and the heading is the class, and he could make one in about four seconds and does not, and it would be worth nothing to her and a great deal to whoever got hold of it.** She puts the form back on the board and squares it with an unsteady hand and does not touch it again. He says he can read a paragraph, has always been able to read a paragraph, is not going to read one out for anybody, and is not one of the four and never was. A class can be re-entered and nobody re-entered anybody, nobody has ever asked anything to be made void, the chain is where it was, and the second half of the sentence she begins in a doorway has still not arrived.

**0465. The Lock End Of Four Hundred Yards, Eight Journeys With Nothing On The Rack, And A Woman Saying Out Loud That She Is The One.** The point of view is the woman of about twenty-six, four years at the lock, who is on no roll in that building, has never been one of its clerks, has never been handed a sheet to take that stone with and has never been thanked. **Her figure is on no paper, has gone up, and does not get a caption. The man in the felt apron stops on the third pass, which he has not done in four years, and sets out the arrangement once: nothing with a heading on it has come down that passage in a week, a sheet came down it once in four years and went back up with the space on it clean, a copy is the only paper in this empire with no heading printed over it, there is no rule on that passage about one because nobody has ever needed one, and a person standing at that end for four years has therefore never once had to make up their mind about what to do when one turns up.** Then she says the thing this chapter is for, out loud, to the flags, knowing the passage gives it back: a man at the top of a stair four flights long can have a thing put in front of him and the putting is not evidence against anybody, she said that in a week that is gone, that is right and it is not what she has come back to — **a copy is not a thing, and a copy will come down that stone the same as anything else and it will not stick to her the way a thing would not stick to him, and she is the person in that building nobody can be shown anything about.** He comes down and refuses her: she has made herself the second holder of a thing that does not exist in the one room where a thing said out loud stays said, and he has been the hardest person in the building to show anything about for forty years and she has just put him in a room. She does not take it back and does not hand over the figure. Nothing comes down that passage in the week, and a week in which nothing arrives is not a week in which something has been held back, and she lost one in the spring.

**0466. About Two Hundred Sheets Under One Hand Before The Sixth Hour, A True Sentence Said Out Loud In A Room, And A Man Who Is Not Going To Be Told.** The point of view is the man of about sixty-one, second hand at that sill since he was forty-two, who cannot make sense of a paragraph and who is not one of the four and never was. About nine hundred sheets a year leave that building and his mark goes at the foot of every one, and in about nine of them a year the page is not what came down out of the room where it was written, and he is the only man alive who knows which nine and there is nowhere to put one down. **The woman of about fifty-three who keeps the minutes of that room and has not asked it a thing in about four years puts a true sentence into it: a second holder is not a person who can be shown holding a thing, because a second holder is a person holding a copy and a copy is not the thing, and therefore a man at the foot of about nine hundred sheets a year is in the safest place there is in this matter, and he does not know that and is not going to be told, and she has not told him and is not going to.** She also says a man who could read a paragraph could read a second of any of them, and that there is one at the end of a lane about nine miles off who can read a paragraph and is nothing to do with the four, and that he has not been asked and is not going to be. Nobody answers her. **The woman of about nineteen comes out with an armful, says the number, and does not say the true thing she has in her mouth, because a person who tells is a person who has to go on telling and the woman behind her has just put one into a room in the same month, and the not being asked is still the only thing she has.** He signs, about nine hundred go out, the ninth of the nine is on nothing, and nobody in that corridor can point at the one sheet in about two years with the same mark on it twice.

**0467. A Fair Hand At The End Of Those Boards, A Man Named Who Is Not In The Room, And A Bill That Stands On Another Account.** The point of view is the man of about thirty-four with a fair hand, who has come up those four flights twice, has a rolled sheet inside his coat that he does not put on the counter, and has a number in his head he has had two years and has never written and never said. **The number is not printed and is not going to be printed, and the prohibition is named in his own point of view in the chapter: nobody is going to confirm it and nobody is going to deny it, and a number handed over is a roll of names and a roll of names is a body and a body is a caption over a man's head.** Marn Ottery says the finding to the boards: a man who cannot be found is a man whose bill for it is somebody else's, a man who can be found cannot be a holder of anything, a holder of a copy is a person holding a stand-in, a stand-in is not the original, and a man holding a stand-in cannot be shown the original and cannot be asked about the original. **Then she names Marek Kest out loud in that room — six times up four flights, nothing on the boards one time out of the six, thirty-eight, the only person in this matter who cannot be shown holding anything — and the naming is the price of the sentence and not a gift to him.** The fair-hand man is not thanked and is not asked anything. Tamsin Rook, early again, hears all of it and is told in the narration why she has not been told. The four hundred and thirty miles is not walked and a man about four miles off was in a room that week and was not sent for.

**0468. A Box At The End Of That Floor That Has Not Moved, Four Sheets Going Up Four Flights, And Two Men Who Have Just Made It Chargeable.** The point of view is the young man from the shipping floor, two years on it, about four sheets a week up four flights. **The sentence about the box has been said twice out loud and both times to one woman: once in a yard a good many years before he came to that floor and once in a room at the top of four flights about six months ago, and there is no form in this empire in which a man is entered as the one who asked her about either.** The man of about forty-five with the loading end comes down the floor, puts his hand on the lid of the box for two seconds, and gives the finding: a second holder is a person a thing can be shown in the hands of, and a box that has not moved is the only object at that end of that floor nobody can charge to anybody, because it has not moved, while everything else on that floor is handled and has a name on it by the second time. Then the harder half: the young man has said that sentence twice to one woman, and one woman is a person a thing can be attached to and also a person who could be made to forget it, two people would have been better for him, and **this week two men have stood at the end of that floor and talked about it, and a thing nobody can be charged with is a thing nobody has spoken about in front of anybody, so the two of them have just made that box chargeable.** He does not say the sentence a third time, he does not take it back, and he stops walking to that end of the floor for the rest of the week. A courier takes the four sheets up and cannot be told anything and asks for nothing. The box is not opened, not moved and not made a mystery.

**0469. A Door At The Bottom Of A Stair, A Man With A Fair Hand Saying Out Loud What That House Is For, And A Rent Taken In Coin.** The point of view is the woman who keeps the ground floor of a rented house, about twenty years at the bottom of a stranger's stairs, who has not been above the second step this week and is not going to be, and the three rooms above that stair are three rooms and not one of them is hers. She takes a rent and it has never been written down anywhere. **The man of about thirty-four with a fair hand comes up that street in the rain and puts his hand on the frame of her door without knocking, and he has not given her his name and she has never asked him for it and neither of them is going to start.** He says one true thing at the door: a second holder is a person who can be shown holding a thing, and nobody can be shown holding a thing in that house, and it is not because she is careful, it is arithmetic — there is no name on anything she has, the rent is coin, the tin behind the boiler is coin, the number on the back of the key says which room and not who — **and a person who can be shown holding nothing can be made to take a thing in about nine seconds, and that is what the house is for and it has been for twenty years and she has known it for about nine years.** He tells her he had it in his head to leave his rolled sheet on the shelf in the passage, for whoever came in after him, and does not, because the second person to hold a thing in that house is the person who cannot be shown holding anything, and that person is her, and he has a bill of his own on somebody else's account and will not put a second on hers. She sends him down the street. The man behind the coal pays a week of rent in coin and it was never a debt and there is nothing in this empire that can be laid against a woman for a room she has let.

**0470. Twenty Minutes Before The Hour, A Flat Thing Said As An Arrangement And Not A Favour, And A Hand Left On A Case And Then Taken Off.** The point of view is Tamsin Rook, twenty-four, early again, and the early has not been a decision for two years and nothing in this chapter turns it into one. **Marn Ottery says one flat thing at about the third hour, to the boards and not to her, as an arrangement and not a favour: what a second holder is, is a person who can be shown holding a thing without being a party to it, and there is one reason for that and the reason is that a copy is not the thing it was made from — and she is not going to put it in that room again.** Then the rest of it, which is hers and is not an asking: a woman who is early for two years for a reason she has not been told is not going to be told this month either, the not being told is the part that has not been put back, being told is the part that could be, and she could be told on one morning and un-told on the next and forgetting is available and nobody has needed it, and that is what two years of nobody saying one word has been for. **And then the cost of the saying: the reason nobody in that room has told her is that telling her would make her a person somebody can be shown knowing about a copy, and Marn Ottery has just said it out loud with her in the room, and so she is one now, and nobody is going to thank her for it and nobody is going to ask her about it.** Tamsin Rook does not answer, does not agree, does not disagree, does not exercise the undertaking and does not retire it, and its date is said out loud in no mouth in the chapter. About four hundred go out of the case that year, one at a time, and not one of them is asked what it is for; the clerk of about twenty-nine writes her four words and nothing else. The drawer is still shut with four things in it and two of them never looked at, the box is where it was, the four go up on the wall, and the fourth of the four is the one none of the three of them can account for. She goes down the four flights and the last thing she does is put her hand flat on the lid of the case and take it off.

# VOLUME 10 BATCH 0004, CHAPTERS 0481 TO 0490, MOVEMENT FOUR — TEN CHAPTER SUMMARIES

**0481 — A Woman Pays For One Cut.** The counter at the top of four flights, referred to and not described. Tamsin Rook's point of view. A woman of about thirty pays fourpence for a line cut into the strip along the top of one of two sheets she cannot tell apart. **Marek Kest comes off the end of the boards, takes the two sheets out of her hands, squares them, and tells her the cut will not do what she paid for it to do — and then Tamsin cuts it anyway, because fourpence is not hers to withhold.** The clerk of about twenty-nine, the one person in that building who could have read a line out of a printed strip and said out loud what was on it, says nothing at all. Marn Ottery says the one new flat thing: a mark with nothing printed over it cannot be read out, cannot be asked about, and cannot be attributed, and the person who cut this one is a person who could be shown having cut it, and that is the first thing in this matter that has made somebody worse off by being made twice. **The cut, the coat and the leaf are three things and are not merged anywhere in the batch.**

**0482 — A Foreman Who Goes To A Door And Does Not Start It, A Man Who Crosses The Shed, And A Sentence Left Where It Stops.** The shed at the end of the lane off the Slade. The man of about fifty-five with the chain. Halla Wray tells him, flatly and not to him, that a class is entered by a form and the heading is the class and a heading is the only thing that makes a mark into a thing you can be shown; then she tells him the other half: a line in the strip is not a signature, it is a fourth item with no name on it, and nobody in the empire can say whose line any of the four hundred is, including the person who cut it. **He says nothing whatever about a line in a strip, and does not agree, and does not thank her.** The change in the chapter is physical and it is the movement's: he gets up off the front bench and crosses that shed **to the board by the door**, and stands with his back to the form on it about nine foot off her — and neither of them begins the sentence. **Nobody will afterwards be able to say which of the two of them left it where it was. He does not stand in the doorway; the first draft had him in it, and that was the moment in a doorway this volume forbids, and the reading took it out without moving the chapter's change.** The reader of seventeen is unspoken to; the girl is not given the chance to say no; the form on the board by the door is not filled in and the form that put her in that class is not asked to be void.

**0483 — A Thumb That Has Known The Top Of Every Sheet For Nineteen Years, A Woman Of Fifty-Three Who Comes Out Of Her Room, And A Hand That Stops Going There.** The building off the old river road. The man of about sixty-one, second hand since he was forty-two, cannot read a paragraph. **The new thing about him is a thumb: he squares every sheet along the top edge, and about four hundred of the sheets that come down to that sill in a year have been cut into, and his thumb has gone to every one of them for nineteen years, and he has never told one person in that building.** The mark is not described — this volume's hard lock forbids its shape and its size and its comparison to anything — and the mechanism is a fact about his hand and not about the paper. **The first draft measured the cut and the reading took the measurement out.** The woman of about fifty-three comes out of her room onto that floor for the first time in four years and says the one flat thing: a mark at the foot of a sheet and a mark cut in the strip at the top of the same sheet are on one piece of paper and about half a year apart in the life of it, and neither is a party to the other, and a sheet carrying both has no column anywhere on it at all. The woman of about nineteen says the number at the end of the floor and says no second true thing, and he hears the not saying. **From the fourth hour he squares the sheets with the heel of his hand instead, and gives up the only way he had of knowing a thing that was not written down, for a reason he cannot read and would not have said if he could.** Nobody asked him one question about a number.

**0484 — The Same Four Lines For The Eleventh Time.** Four hundred yards up the lane off the Slade, a counting room. Rennick Adley, thirty-eight, the Company's book-keeper, writes the same four lines out for the eleventh time and they come out the same. A woman of about thirty-five with a day-book has come up the four hundred yards with it under her arm, which is the once, and sets it on the table and does not open it. She says the flat thing: a heading is what makes a column, and a line in a strip is a mark with no heading, and that is the one thing a book like hers can neither hold nor refuse. **He answers with the thing about his own hand, and then refuses her the only true thing in that building that is not about a book: nine words of a sentence a woman of fifty-one began in a doorway in that lane, which are not hers, and which he is not going to put in her hands — and the cost is that she is now a person who could be shown knowing he has nine words of something.** The day-book is shut and put back, no fifth column is ruled, it is not turned round, and the four hundred yards is not walked.

**0485 — Two Figures That Do Not Come Out Even.** The counter at the bottom of that stair, where the day-book lives on the shelf behind it. The man of about thirty-four with a fair hand. **The number in his head is not printed, not confirmed and not denied, and the prohibition is named in the chapter in a paragraph of its own.** She says one flat thing about a heading and a column that is not about him and he does not take it up. **She says the name Marek Kest out loud at that counter, and he is not in the room, and the absence has its cost named in the room he is not in: one of her four rooms has stood empty since before the spring, she has not let it, and she is not going to stand there and tell him the two of those are connected, because there is no form in this empire for entering a landlord's empty room and a man's silence under one heading.** Then he does the eight hours and **for the first time in about a fortnight he does not make two figures agree, and the whole of the movement's work on him is that the one mark in this empire that could be made against a man is a fair hand.** A man who is paid to make figures agree and does not is a man a second of a thing can be made out of, and there is no column for a refused job.

**0486 — A Coat On A Nail That Has Never Had Anything Hung On It.** The room taken by the week, referred to and not described.
