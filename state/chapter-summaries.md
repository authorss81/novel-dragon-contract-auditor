## Chapter 351 (1,528) — the room over a chandler's shop taken by the week, four steps and a landing, about four feet of bare wall with a nail in it, and a table with a sheet on it this morning

The volume's first page, and it opens on the only person in this matter anybody could put a question to, and he does not ask one. **He has a number in his head that he cannot give away, and the reason is not modesty: handing it over would be a list, a list of persons is a company, a company is a heading, and a heading is what a man turns into the moment the thing outgrows one room.** He paid fourpence for a copy in each of two other rooms and is in neither of them, and the bill for being findable is not his and stands on somebody else's account. Three other things are owed to him: the thing nobody has thanked him for, a form for asking a person whether they will mind being told a thing before they are told it, and a woman at the foot of about four steps who is not thanked either. **The count of things anybody has asked out loud in this matter is seven, it was seven ten weeks ago, he is not going to be the eighth, and the next asking is not his to make.** The lamp is not lit and is not going to be. The chapter opens on the week after the last image of Volume 07, which was a hand flat on a table with nothing on it, and there is a sheet on the table this week and a column that does not agree and a man of forty who does not know his name. **REPAIRED:** A man of forty comes up the four steps on the first morning with the column unrolled on the table, says the fifth line is the one he is afraid of, is told it is a figure he has written larger than the rest of him and does not come to the same thing twice, tells the clerk he will be told which line is wrong, and is told he will get what he pays for. He rolls the stick and goes down the four steps without saying thank you, and the afternoon is the clerk alone with a line he is not the man to say is wrong.

## Chapter 352 (1,630) — a counting room at the back of a dye end about eleven feet by fourteen, a stove somebody lit and let go out, sixteen books, and a wage she is not going to ask the reason for

Nell Kest, twenty, alone in the room from the second hour. Fifteen of the sixteen books are other people's and none has an office on the cover, and if anybody came up those stairs with a sheet and a seal she could not show a soul the fourth of them. **The second week of the third month of the year after is the week her wage stopped arriving, and she is not going to ask what it is held against, and that decision was made before this week: a question and an answer would make the two of them parties, and a party is the only kind of person a thing can be told what it is going to be used for.** She told one woman of four that she is not paid and said the whole of a rule to a second in about four minutes; the two she cannot reach are the same two. A foreman's notice in a coat says a sum and not what it is against, and the day it arrives is not one of the days he has named. The eleven houses off the dyers' lane came in on the first of the two days and eleven numbers went out on the second. Nobody came for the sixteenth book. She is not going up this river this season, and that is not the same as never, and nobody has asked her either thing. **REPAIRED:** The second of the four women comes in to set the day's numbers and is told, in four minutes, that the rule went nowhere: she has four of her own, two of them have stopped, and she is not going to be the person who says it to a third one, because a person who says things is on a list. She asks for nothing and is asked nothing, and she goes out to a shift, and the third of the four is still not in and the fourth is still at the far end of a lane.

## Chapter 353 (1,406) — the fourth counter of a Table in Auremar, a fee board of four items and not one of them time, and a box of about four hundred blanks under the counter

Marn Ottery, thirty-four, eleven years at that counter and not a lawyer, and **she is the reason she is still standing at it and has never said so out loud to anybody and nobody has thanked her for it.** The border law in her own mouth, the shape before the thing: a column with a heading over it is a machine and a machine can be asked things all day; a column with no heading over it is a person, and a person is only ever asked one way. A thing put to a border household at no charge has to be answered and the answer has to be entered as a form and not a letter, and it got an answer in about three weeks where a House's own table took about eleven and then said no and cost a hundred and forty years, **and the reason is that the law does not need a person, because a border household is not a person either.** The box of about four hundred bought and unfilled blanks has been under that counter eleven years, no form was ever drawn to account for it, and the box is not a post. The fourth item on the fee board is the one three clerks cannot account for and it is hers and the two other clerks' and it is not the book-keeper's, and nobody at that counter has put one question to another about it out loud in about four years. **REPAIRED:** The one man who comes up the stair in the whole morning is answered out loud in two sentences and told that if he has not brought a column he is to have his money back, and he has brought one, and he reads it at a distance, and he goes down the stair without saying thank you, and a man with a column and a bad week is not a villain. The monologue that follows is untouched by this repair, which is the strongest material in the batch and the only chapter in it that was already a room with people in.

## Chapter 354 (1,674) — the same counter two weeks later, the other end of it, the last hour before it shuts, and a woman nobody has asked anything in about two years

Tamsin Rook, twenty-four. **Nobody has asked her anything in about two years, and that is a choice and not a fault**, and the whole of what that costs is wanting to be asked every working day since about the seventh month of next year. The inherited phrase stands once in its own words: *a person who is not asked has not been given a duty*. The man who came up that stair once, in the second week of the tenth month, put one question into the air and did not wait, and she gave him nothing; that is the last time anybody has put a question to her, and she has not decided since whether she wishes she had answered it. **The undertaking of the fourth of the first month has not been retired, has not been amended and has never been exercised, and the reason she gives this time is not one of the three she gave before: nobody asked her, and there is no form anywhere for asking a person whether they will mind being told a thing before they are told it, and she has looked in about nine places for one and found a column with a heading over it in every one of them.** The line in her own book about the woman at the other end of the counter is a window and not a wall, and the four things in the drawer are unopened and the box of blanks is not hers, and the decision about whether she trusts a man's judgment is unmade and is not made in a dead hour at the end of a counter. **REPAIRED:** The two women at one counter, four feet apart, who have not exchanged a word in two years, do: a thing said to a fee board is heard as a thing said to a person, a woman who has not been asked what she had for dinner is compared with a woman who has been asked nothing for eleven years, and the woman of thirty-four says out loud on her way past that the fourth item on that board is the only one she could read in a hurry, and that she wrote it herself four years ago, and that the woman at the other end is not a fool and is not being said so now. Neither of them is thanked. The drawer with four things in it is shut at the end of it and the fourth is still shut.

## Chapter 355 (1,642) — a rented room two streets off a street that goes down to the river road, about four feet of a wall he does not use, and a morning of two refusals and two non-uses

Marek Kest, thirty-eight. **He does not attempt the reading and he gave the reason out loud in the room, to himself and to about four feet of wall: it has been done to him twice, each time it took a whole day out of his mother's own history, he was not in the room for it, and he is thirty-eight and cannot tell whether he came out of that house or stood in the doorway of it.** The third one is not a thing he is going to spend on a man at a rail. **He does not do the other thing either, the one he is able to do, and a correct non-use is entered as one; the reason is nine words long and it is on a page in another year and he is not going to say it again.** The reason he is not at a stage is not said anywhere in this chapter. The mark is about three inches long on the inside of his left wrist and it reads nothing and it decides nothing and it does not know where the ford is; **the wage is the price and the mark is what he has instead of a receipt, and there is no form anywhere that gives a man one.** A bar of his own came off him on the twelfth of the ninth month of last year on an application of one line which he wrote himself. He holds nothing: no paper, no office, no fund, no list. A woman about four miles off is not told that he said the reason out loud to about nine men in a bay. **REPAIRED:** A man of about forty at a gate who is not a villain and does not argue tells him he has two days out of five, that the rate is not why, and that if he wants to know why he is not getting a third he is not getting that either, and lifts the bar about as far as it always comes up. He is not stood at the gate on the second day or the fourth. Everything else the chapter was, it still is: two refusals, two correct non-uses, a mark on a wrist that reads nothing, and tea made too strong, which is what he does when he is afraid.

## Chapter 356 (1,678) — a bay at the top of a lane off the Slade about nine foot by eleven, a bench longer than the bay is wide, and a cross in chalk at the height of the third link of a chain

Halla Wray, fifty-one, foreman by nine families' agreement in a shed behind the second bay about nine years ago, and inside a box since the first week of the seventh month of the year after, entered by a class, and nobody has asked her about it. **Being in a box and not being asked about it are the same arrangement, and the arrangement was made in that shed by nine families and not by her** — said out loud in her own mouth, and she has been given no way of saying it as though it were two things. The form did not ask her whether she would represent anybody, it asked who she represents, and there is a column for the answer and no column for a refusal, and she said that once and is not going to say it again. **At the back of that bay the two of seventeen are unspoken to, unthanked, unsent for, and not going to start, and nobody in that bay has an answer about why.** About four men there cannot make sense of a paragraph and not one question about a number has ever been put to any of them. The man of about fifty-five with the chain is not one of the four, says so out loud to nobody in particular, has known for about four years that findability does not work on him and has never used it. The book-keeper four hundred yards up the lane has wanted the second half of a sentence of hers since the third month of the year after and has not asked, and the day he asks is the day she stops saying the half she has got. The cross in the chalk is nobody's and is about four years off being anybody's evidence. **REPAIRED:** The Company's book-keeper comes four hundred yards down the lane to the doorway of the south end of the hired store and is given half a sentence, and says out loud that he has thought about asking for the rest of it and has not and is not going to, and that he has not copied her half out and has not shown it to the man at the other end of that counter and there is no form that would let him if he wanted to. She tells him to put that on his own side of the doorway. He has half a sentence, and a half is not a debt and not a confession and not an answer, and he would have it anyhow.

## Chapter 357 (1,413) — a room on the ground floor off the old river road, a shelf of eleven books, a pane out of the window for about four years, and about nine hundred sheets a year

The man of about sixty-one, the second hand since he was forty-two, and **he cannot read a paragraph: he can read a figure and a name and most of the short words that hold a sheet together, and everything else is shape.** He reads what he can and signs the rest. About nine of about nine hundred sheets a year are not what the person above him wrote, and he signed every one of them because he was not asked and was given nothing to sign against, and he is the only person alive who knows which ones they were. **There is nowhere in this empire for a man to put his mark beside a sum and call it a disagreement, and it is not an oversight in the making of the page.** What he wants is about four minutes: no to about half of them and yes to the rest, and the yeses matter as much. On every sheet there are three things that can be asked and two of them are a clerk and an office, and that leaves him. He is not in a bay and he is not one of the four who cannot make sense of a paragraph, and a list with him in it alongside those four would be a list of five. Nobody has ever thanked him, he has never asked to be, and he knows both halves of that and has never put them in one sentence. The figure he carries about how many times he has come to that room in nineteen years is not written down and not confirmed by anything. **REPAIRED:** A carrier puts four sheets on the shelf and says four, and he says four, and she tells him she is paid ninepence at the lock and is on a list and is not paid to remember things, and that being on a list is not the same as being asked, and that she worked that out herself in about six years. She says the fifth of the four is the one he would not read out loud, and he says he has never read any of them out loud, and she says that if he were to start she would have to remember it.

## Chapter 358 (1,336) — about four hundred yards of cold flags off a flood terrace, racks coming back light by one, and a room at the far end with a rail that is not on the passage's work

The woman of about twenty-six, four years in that building and not one of the three clerks and not in this. **She keeps the count in her head and has never once put a hand on a rack, and her figure has gone up since the seventh month of the year after and she has not said by how much and is not going to.** She worked out in about four minutes that the figure is the only one this empire keeps on how many people are sitting inside a thing nobody can be asked about, and that a number in a book is a column and a column wants a heading, and a heading over that figure would be a heading over about nine hundred rooms at once, and a person cannot be under a heading like that and stay a person. **Nothing that comes down those flags goes into that room and nothing that comes out of it comes up them, and there is no list that reaches it and no rack that stops at it and no person whose work takes her to that door, and that is the whole of the reason a thing said out loud in that room stays in that room, and it is not because anybody is careful.** She has three questions for the man of about sixty-one and has asked none of them, and the fact that he came at all gave her no fourth. She is the only other person in this matter who knows what was said in that room, and there is no form anywhere that would let anybody put to her the question of what she heard. **REPAIRED:** A man in a felt apron takes a rack down and brings it back light by one, eleven times a day, and says one, and she says one is usual, and he says it has been one since he had that end of it and that he could tell the hour of the day it happened on if he had a book for it and has not got a book for it. He says he is telling a lock, and a lock is not a person, and that is the only reason this passage is the easiest hundred yards of his day and not the hardest.

## Chapter 359 (1,556) — a house with a door on it about four miles off, four steps, a fire gone out at about the fourth hour, and two people with an arrangement and not an understanding

The household, and the price, and neither arrangement chosen by anybody. Six weeks before he did the last thing of that kind he told her about it, out loud, on purpose, without asking her first, and gave the reason: **if he had asked her she would have said no, or said yes and been frightened, and either of those would have been him standing in a room arranging it.** This time he is not telling her at all, and the not telling is the same absence as the not asking and is not a better way of doing anything. She is not thanked and has not been thanked and said out loud that she is not going to ask how it went, and saying it out loud did not make it a rule and did not make it a wall. **There is no form for agreeing in advance to something that has not been proposed, and there is no form for settling it afterwards either, and what there is instead is a person deciding and a person having the deciding done to her.** The bill for being findable is not his and he found out at second hand and did not go and look, and he is not going to find out whose it is by asking. A woman of about thirty-five next door came out on her step twice and nothing was said. No romance begins in this chapter and nothing in it decides whether anybody trusts anybody's judgment. **REPAIRED:** The two of them, who have an arrangement and not an understanding, talk: she says the hour is not a thing she has standing to have an opinion about, he says she has never had standing for anything and that this is not an insult and that the day he says she is right about it is a day she has something from him he cannot put back. She brings his coat out and does not put it on him and does not leave it where he can refuse it, and puts it on the one chair in that room he does not use. Neither of them says where he has been.

## Chapter 360 (1,643) — the same ground-floor room, the same shelf, the pane still out, about four thousand entries and not one name, and an afternoon in which nothing is asked of anybody

The clerk of about fifty-five, nineteen years in that room, writes the minutes, advises nobody and asks nothing. **The arrangement that has kept about nine hundred sheets a year out of every book in this empire for nineteen years is the arrangement that has kept anybody from asking the man at the foot of them one question, and those are one fact and not two.** A question put in that building is a question put into a record, and the record is what the sheet goes in, and the last time a thing of that shape happened to about four hundred people their sheet went back and nobody was punished. **A minute with an answer in it is an order, and an instruction has to be carried out by somebody, and the somebody would have a name on it, and a name on it is a party; so she has not entered one in nineteen years and not entering one is the price of the only thing anybody has ever asked her to do, which is to be right.** Her own finding of about six years of her own motion is on the shelf under the window and is still a question, has not been turned into a sentence, and has no full stop on it; she is the only person in this matter anybody could serve a question on in writing and she is paid nothing for standing ready to have it served. A quarter of it was answered out loud in a room at the far end of her own passage in the second month by a man who walked nine miles to get there on his own two feet, and she entered none of that either, and nobody is going to ask her about it a second time. **She is not told anything about what was said in that room and she wrote none of it down, and there is nothing in this empire that would let a clerk of an office be told that a person is coming or that a person is not, and that is the price of standing ready and nobody laid it out.** The count is the same as it was at the fourth hour, the next asking is nobody's, and the ten weeks hand on a price and not a plan. **REPAIRED:** The man of about sixty-one comes up the passage with four sheets and puts them on her table without being asked, the way he has done twice in nineteen years, and she enters the four in a hand that does not vary, and he says the one thing he says in that building, which is that the flags are cold at that hour, and she says that they are. Neither of them says the other thing, which is nine miles and nineteen years and about four thousand entries with not one name in any of them, and he takes the four up off her table and goes back down, and the flags are loud for a minute and then are not.


## Chapter 361

The fourth counter of a Table in Auremar, at the top of four flights of a stair with no door at the foot of it, in a wet week. The man of about thirty-four with the fair hand comes up the four flights with his hand in his coat and nothing in the other one, and he is in the room for the week and he buys nothing. Marn Ottery says out loud that she said the shape of a thing twice in that room and has been asked one thing back and will not say it a third time, and then tells him the half of the count she means to tell him: about nine people come up that stair on an ordinary day, about four come up in this weather, and the ones who do not come up are the ones who have furthest to come, and the rail is where they decide, and she has watched four people decide and said nothing to any of them. A woman of fifty pays fourpence for a copy of a form about a week of wages on the third day and reads that it is right and goes down. The counter shuts an hour early on the fifth day because of the state of the stairs. He goes down the four flights and stands at the bottom of a stair with no door at the foot of it, where there is nowhere to stand that is not in the way of somebody going up. Nobody asked him anything, nobody thanked him, the count did not move, and the next asking is nobody's.

## Chapter 362

The room at the far end of about four hundred yards of cold flags off a flood terrace at the back of a building off the old river road, with a rail standing about four feet off the floor and a door at the top end with the tread worn through. A woman of about forty with a scuttle lights the lamp, which is the whole of what she does there twice a week, and she is not one of the three clerks of that building. About four people stood in that room before a man of thirty-four did, and he talked for nine minutes with his coat open, and she said nothing at the rail then and has said nothing since. A clerk of about thirty resists her: if she said what she heard he would have to write it down or refuse to, there is no line on that paper for either, and the not-writing would be a thing he did to her. He tells her to come on the other day from this week; she tells him that is not the rota and that he keeps the rota. Then she says the true thing, which is about herself and not about the room: she has got so good at not hearing that she would not know now whether anything had been said in there at all. She goes back down four hundred yards with the lamp lit behind her.

## Chapter 363

A lodging house with a counter, a shelf, a stair behind the counter, about four rooms let by the week, and a day-book on the shelf that is the only book in the building anybody opens. A woman of about thirty-five has kept it nine years in her own hand with a heading over every column: room, week, name, what was paid. She came about nine miles and was not let in and has written none of it down, and there is no form for a lodging house to record a coming that was refused, and the form for lodging a person by the week has nothing in it for a person who stood on a step. A lodger of twenty-two, nine weeks in the back room, resists her: the day-book is the only thing anybody could hand anybody, and there is nothing in it but a name and a week and a penny, and that is because of her. She tells her that being findable is being usable, and she has worked out which end of it she is standing at. She takes the day-book down, rules nothing, and puts it back with the spine square to the edge of the shelf.

## Chapter 364

The bay at the top of a lane off the Slade, nine foot by eleven, a bench longer than the bay is wide, a lamp not lit until about the seventh hour, a chalk mark on the end wall level with the third link of a chain, and two slates on the bench, one of them face up with a question in an office hand and no name under it. The man of about fifty-five with the chain cannot read a paragraph, is not one of the four in that bay who cannot, says so out loud to nobody, and has known for about four years that the one thing he can do will not reach him and has never used it. He used it once, at a shut gate, and told nobody. A man of about twenty-eight resists him: the slate has something on it and neither of them can read it, and he has the one thing that would find out what is on it and it would not find out what is on it. He says the shape of it out loud in four sentences to about nine men — a question with no name under it cannot be shown to have been got wrong — and nothing happens, and he does not use the thing, and the slate is still on the bench face up.

## Chapter 365

The received room at the far end of the fourth counter's floor, four shelves and three of them full, a lamp lit in it, and about four hundred bought and unfilled blanks that have been under that counter for eleven years. Marn Ottery, thirty-four, eleven years at that counter and not a lawyer. Marek Kest, thirty-eight, comes up the four flights for a copy that costs fourpence and does not get to the counter, and carries the box up four steps with her because the two of them have nothing else to do with their hands. She says out loud that about four people have seen a thing happen and there is no form with a line on it for that; he says the line would be for a clerk's name and that a heading over it is a way of finding out that a clerk kept paper nobody asked about for eleven years. He says the rest of it, that the name he is after is not on the page he wants copied and never was. She sets her hands on the box and takes them off it. He goes down the four flights without his copy, paid nothing, not served, asked nothing. The box is on the floor with the lid where it was and no form records the stair.

## Chapter 366

The other end of the same dye end and the same counting room, eleven feet by fourteen, about forty volumes on shelving built for taller books, and the stove out because whoever got in first lit it and went down the lane again. Nell Kest, twenty, sixteen volumes, fifteen on loan, the sixteenth with her own name at the head of it. The second of the four women she told a rule comes in and says she has been carrying four of her own figures since that morning. Nell refuses to say the rule a second time, and says why: a rule said twice to the same person is a person somebody can be served with. The second woman refuses that, and it is said again in about four minutes, and the rule is in two heads in one lane instead of one. The eleven houses send their numbers in on the first of the two days and get eleven back on the second. Her pay stopped in the second week of the third month of the year after, is nineteen days of a crew's money in a coat four miles off, and she is not going to ask what it is against. Nobody has come up those stairs for the sixteenth book.

## Chapter 367

The same bay at the top of a lane off the Slade with the gate at the bottom of it open, which nobody in that lane decided. Halla Wray, fifty-one, whose foreman's job was settled for her by nine families in a shed in an hour and a half and who was not in the shed. With the gate open the bay is louder and nothing changes, and a man stood at the gate for four minutes in which nobody said anything. She says out loud that a foreman who is findable is a foreman who can be served. A man of about twenty-eight resists her: the sentence is true, and a shut gate does the work of nine men for nothing, and he would rather they had nine men. The gate is shut again on the second day. The class she is in is still a heading and what a heading does is turn one person into a firm. The man who keeps the books four hundred yards up the lane has not asked and she has not sent for him, and the second half of that sentence did not arrive. The two of seventeen at the back were not spoken to, and the girl has never been offered a way of saying no.

## Chapter 368

The ground-floor room off the old river road with a shelf of eleven books under a window about two feet across that has had no pane for about four years, and about four thousand entries with no name in any of them. The man of about sixty-one, second hand since he was forty-two, about nine hundred sheets a year, and a paragraph is a shape to him. There is a sheet in front of him this week that is not what the man above him wrote, and he signs it because he was not asked. A young man of about twenty-six comes with a basket at the fourth hour and initialled the corner of each sheet and has never read one. He is told out loud that that one is wrong, and says he is paid for a corner and not for a page, that talking to the only person in the room is not the same as talking to the person, and that the sheet goes down the corridor and does not come back up. He says one sentence of the whole thing this week to a young man paid for a corner. Nobody has thanked him. A person who is not asked has not been given a duty, and he has known that for nineteen years, and the pane is still out of the window.

## Chapter 369

A house with a door on it four miles off, four steps up off the pavement, a door with two coats of paint on it that were both done by somebody else, and the next house about four feet off with a step of its own. The man of about thirty-four with the fair hand comes up the lane; his wife is at the foot of the four steps and the woman of about thirty-five next door is a different person. He has not told her where he has been and is not going to; she has not asked and is not going to, and says out loud that not asking is not the same as not minding and that he has known that difference longer than she has. Nobody in this empire has a form that lets a man put to a woman the single question of whether she would rather know first, and he has gone at it for about two years. The woman next door comes out on her own step and the two women talk across four feet of lane about a delivery that has been late four days running, and neither of them says one word about the man, and there is no form anywhere for two women saying a thing out loud about a man. Nothing is decided, nothing is asked, nothing is thanked, and no understanding is arrived at.

## Chapter 370

The same ground-floor room, the same shelf of eleven books, the window about two feet across with still no pane in it. The clerk of about fifty-five keeps the minutes of that room and in nineteen years has never advised a person in it and never put a question to one, and there is a woman of about nineteen at the far end of it six weeks into the job. A minute with an answer in it is an order and in nineteen years she has entered none, and no form lets her. She is the only person in this matter anybody could serve a question on in writing and nobody has ever paid her for standing ready to be served. The young woman says a question with no full stop on it is not a question but a note, and a note can be thrown out, and she is not going to be the one who keeps it.
