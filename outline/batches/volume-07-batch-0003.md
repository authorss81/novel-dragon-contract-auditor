## THE COUNT OF ASKINGS, SETTLED BEFORE A CARD, AND IT DOES NOT MOVE

**The count is seven at the start of this batch and it is seven at the end of it, and not one of these ten chapters contains an asking, which is a count of things done out loud in a room, one person putting a question to another.** The standing precedent is Chapter 0292 and both of this volume's first two movements. **The rule this batch writes into its own cards, inherited from Batch 0002, is stronger than the standing one: not one question mark in this batch's ten files is put to a person about a person.** Every question mark these ten files carry is about a sheet, a figure, a day, an hour, a letter, an entry or a light. That is the cure and it costs the page nothing.

**One thing in this batch is close enough to the count to be worth naming, and it does not touch it.** Chapter 0323 puts a man of about thirty-four into a room with a question ready in his mouth and he does not have it out. **It is printed without a question mark, in the house form this matter uses for an asking that is not an asking, and the reason he gives out loud is the volume's third rule and not a speech, and a chapter that printed it as a question would have written Chapter 0297 again.**

**Nothing is printed as a figure for the count of askings anywhere in these ten files.**

### 321 — the room over the chandler's shop, the lamp not lit, and a finding that turns out to be a room

1. **Day.** Fourth day of the third week of the fifth month of the year after the year after the year after next, about the fourth hour.
2. **Room.** The room over the chandler's shop taken by the week: four steps and a landing, a table, a chair stood against the wall the bed is against, a window about a foot wide looking at another house's back, about four feet of bare wall with a nail in it and nothing on the nail, and a lamp that is not lit until about the seventh hour. Tar and rope and lamp oil under the boards all day.
3. **Point of view.** The man of about thirty-four with a fair hand, eleven years of writing out other men's sums so that they come out even, four days a season on somebody's list, a house with a door on it four miles off with his wife at the bottom of the steps. Named in the third paragraph and not by name.
4. **What happens.** He does the ordinary once-in-four-weeks walk through the list. The change is this: everything he has carried for about two years has been carried as a document, and it is not a document. **A document goes to about nine hundred rooms and a question has to be said in one of them, and the finding he has is a room, and a room is the only kind of thing a person can be said in.** Which room: the one at the end of a passage about four hundred yards long off a flood terrace, with a rail about four feet high at the far end of it, in a building off the old river road. **He has not been in it. One man has been in it twice in nineteen years and both times in a season, and a woman of about twenty-six is at the other end of that passage and cannot be asked, and there is no form in this empire that would let anybody in that room say that a person was expected.**
5. **What the reader has to hold.** That the address is not findable and never was going to be, that a room is the answer to a question about how a question is asked, and that the only person in that room who can be asked is the man of about sixty-one, and that this time he is nine miles off in another building and nobody has told him and there is no form that would let anybody tell him.
6. **What it may not do.** Not ask anybody anything, not go and look, not walk nine miles or four hundred and thirty, not light the lamp before about the seventh hour, not thank anybody, not be thanked, not print the figure of seven, not name the who, and not convert a room into a document.
7. **The `no form` wording it carries.** *There is no form in this empire that lets a person be said in anything but a room they came into themselves.* And in his own mouth: *no form in this empire has ever carried a question out of one building and put it in front of a person in another.*
8. **Where the emphasis goes.** Four runs, one clause each, three in the narrator and one in his own voice, none holding two sentences: the finding being a room and not a document; a room being the only kind of thing a person can be said in; the two visits and the season; and the absence of any form for saying a person was expected.
9. **`about nine` budget.** Six raw, of which three are the page figure *about nine hundred* in its forms and are excluded, leaving three counted. The bound is about eight.
10. **Question marks.** Two, both in his own mouth in bare short lines, one about a sheet and one about a figure, and neither about a person.
11. **The lock it most nearly breaks.** The seven. It does not move, and the chapter has no asking in it, and the finding is a statement about a shape and not a question put to anybody.
12. **Traps.** The season is not named by a month; the two earlier visits carry no day; no seat, no House, no notary, no document, no bundle, no red thing, no counting-house mark, no ninety-second leaf; the fifth year name written out in full and once; no `may`; no `schedule`; no `register`; no `the book`; the four who cannot read a paragraph are not in this chapter at all.

### 322 — the ground-floor room, the pane still out, and what a question put in that building is put into

1. **Day.** Fourth day of the fourth week of the fifth month of the year after the year after the year after next, about the fifth hour.
2. **Room.** The ground-floor room of a building off the old river road: a shelf with eleven books on it, a table, a chair, a window about two feet wide with a pane out of it for about four years, and about four thousand entries in the eleven books with not one name in any of them. The passage runs about four hundred yards off a flood terrace at the back and there is a rail about four feet high at the far end of it.
3. **Point of view.** The clerk of about fifty-five, nineteen years in that one room, writes the minutes, advises nobody, asks nothing. Named in the second paragraph.
4. **What happens.** She says out loud, to a page, that she is not going to ask him anything, and gives the reason. **A question put in that building is a question put into a record, and the record is nineteen years of a woman of fifty-five copying a hand into eleven books with no name in any of them, and the sheet goes in there, and a person who is asked in that building is a person who has been served at, and the last time a thing of that shape was done to about four hundred people their sheet went back.** A minute with an answer in it is an order and a clerk of this office has not entered an order in nineteen years. **The arrangement that has kept about nine hundred sheets a year correct for nineteen years is the arrangement that has kept anybody from asking him one question, and those are one fact and not two.**
5. **What the reader has to hold.** That the third rule has a floor under it, that the reason is not caution and not kindness but nineteen years of a room that works, and that she is the only person in this matter answerable in writing for a question and is paid nothing for it.
6. **What it may not do.** Not ask him anything, not ask anybody anything, not be asked a second time, not state an elapsed period from the question in the second of the eleven books, not turn the question into an answer, not thank anybody, not send for anybody, and not make the finding sound like a discovery — she has known it for nineteen years.
7. **The `no form` wording it carries.** *There is no form in this empire that would let a clerk of an office tell a person that he was in the room on a day, and the not telling is the same absence as the not asking.* And in her mouth: *no form in this empire has ever kept a person who said a thing in a room out of the book that the thing belongs in.*
8. **Where the emphasis goes.** Five runs, one clause each, four in the narrator and one in her own mouth, none holding two sentences: a question in that building being a question into a record; the nineteen years and the no names; the about four hundred and the sheet going back; a minute with an answer being an order; and the two facts being one fact.
9. **`about nine` budget.** Six raw, of which two are *about nine hundred* and one is *about nine of about nine hundred* counted as one page figure, excluded, leaving three counted.
10. **Question marks.** One, in her own mouth, about a book, and not about a person.
11. **The lock it most nearly breaks.** The question in the second of the eleven books. It stays a question, it is not asked a second time, it carries no period, and it is not turned into an answer by anything in the chapter.
12. **Traps.** No elapsed period, and the reason is recorded in the state layer and not here; the eleventh books and the about four thousand entries are not in one sentence as one thing; no carrier is sent and no lane is named; the season is on and nobody is told; no `may`; the year name once, in full.

### 323 — the hinge: a room with a rail at the end of a passage, nine minutes, and nobody asked

1. **Day.** Second day of the first week of the sixth month of the year after the year after the year after next, about the second hour, and about nine minutes of it.
2. **Room.** The room at the end of the cold passage, with a rail about four feet high standing in it, a step worn through in front of the door at the top of the passage and nobody at the door at any hour of the day. About nine people in it. Three of them are the clerks of the building and about six of them are not. A woman of about forty who carries a scuttle is one of the six.
3. **Point of view.** The man of about thirty-four with a fair hand, who is in the room and has not been in it before. Named in the third paragraph and not by name.
4. **What happens.** **He comes about nine miles on his own two feet, in a season, on his own time, on a day he is not paid for, and nobody sent him and nobody told him, because there is no form in this empire that would let anybody in that room say that a person was expected.** He stands on the wrong side of the rail for about four minutes and then puts a sheet down on the rail itself, which is a thing he has done twice now and which is not a thing any form in this empire records a person doing. He says about nine minutes' worth of it standing up: sixty-one, the second hand since he was forty-two, cannot read a paragraph, about nine of about nine hundred a year not as the person above them wrote, he signed those, there is no column on any of them for a man to write no in, he would want about four minutes, and there is nobody to ask. He picks the sheet up and gives it back and she enters it correctly in a hand that does not vary. **The man of about thirty-four has a question ready and does not have it out, and gives the reason out loud, and the reason is the third rule and not a speech.**
5. **What the reader has to hold.** That the whole of it has now been said out loud twice in nineteen years, that about nine people were in the room, that the clerk of about fifty-five wrote none of it down and said out loud that writing it down would make it an order, that nobody thanked anybody, and that the count of askings is seven and did not move.
6. **What it may not do.** Not ask him anything, not ask the man of thirty-four anything, not thank anybody, not be thanked, not have a man of thirty-four be thanked for not asking, not write any of it down, not name a day of the week, not assert that this is the day of the week he came the last two times, not fill a column, and not have the woman of about forty be asked what she heard.
7. **The `no form` wording it carries.** *There is no form in this empire in which the act of a man standing at a rail is a thing anybody is entitled to have written down afterwards.* And in the clerk's mouth: *no form in this empire would let me enter that a man said a thing at a rail, and an entry with a thing in it is an order, and I have not entered an order in nineteen years.*
8. **Where the emphasis goes.** Six runs, one clause each, none holding two sentences, and no more than one in any one speech turn: the four minutes on the wrong side of the rail; the sheet on the rail itself; the whole of it said twice in nineteen years; the absence of any column for a man to write no in; the question that does not come out; and the price of a man who goes.
9. **`about nine` budget.** Eight raw, of which four are *about nine hundred* and are excluded, leaving four counted. The bound is about eight and the raw column is the one that is governed.
10. **Question marks.** One, in the man of thirty-four's mouth, about a column, and not about a person.
11. **The lock it most nearly breaks.** The seven. The chapter is the one place in this movement where the asking was available and was not taken, and it is printed without a question mark and with the reason given out loud.
12. **Traps.** About nine people in the room is a page figure and is not the count of askings; the woman of about forty is not one of the three clerks and is not in this and cannot be asked; the man of about sixty-one is not one of the four men in a bay; the four hundred and thirty miles is not walked; no season is named by a month; no `may`; the year name once, in full.

### 324 — the same room, the season still on, and what the third movement cost

1. **Day.** Second day of the second week of the sixth month of the year after the year after the year after next, about the fifth hour.
