# Volume 01 Batch 0005 Outline: The Second Dunn Hearing and the Question With a Route

Batch number: 5
Chapter range: 41–50

Batch goal: **Close Volume 01.** This batch contains the volume climax in Chapters 41–46 and the aftermath in Chapters 47–50, and it is the last ten chapters of the volume, so everything the volume promised must be either paid or explicitly carried with a named holder. The volume's local promise — Tavi Venn keeps the field, Mara gets a winter, Nessa is heard before anything attaches to her, Corvin's certificates stop, Nell's exit clause survives, and the Kest file is formally reopened without being cleared — is discharged here. The volume's larger promise — why the same language appears in three provinces, in a broker's records, and in Ilyra Kest's private file, and who stands behind the sealed instruction — is **not** discharged, is made harder, and is given a route. The last image of the volume is not a victory. It is a red thread continuing across a burned archive wrapper.

Starting condition: The **second Dunn hearing is set for the twenty-first of the fifth month**, at the second hour, in the Mosswake Roll Court's own room, with the household to be heard and the girl to be heard. Tavi Venn's canal examination is arranged for the fifth month and not given; the fee of two shillings and sixpence is paid out of the Reed Cut district works fund under the head *labour, examinations, engaged*, by Wendt Sayer, a foreman, as a recruitment cost. **Ilsabet Kell**, sixty-one, is coming to a room in the fifth month with her own note in her own hand. The **scope decision on the nine paragraphs is refused** with a seal and nobody's name on it, and a second request is filed over **Tamsin Rook's own signature as a roll-keeper**, naming the district of Glasswater, and is unanswered. The **limited sitting produced one thing**: Sivra Oris's sworn statement that the instrument created two named personal duties and no office, in a form with a blank box for a dead counterparty that will be empty for ever, and **it may not be cited in any other matter.** A court has recorded, with a magistrate's name at the foot of it, that it cannot find out who authorised the sealed instruction, and that a party may apply to the **Cinder Court at Auremar** in the fifth month on the record of the third of the fourth, and that this court cannot make that application. The Venn term expired on the nineteenth of the second month with nothing decided. **Aldren Venn's file cannot be made complete and is not going to be.** The Caudles have a notice with **Mike Caudle's name in a box** and a thumb over the line that says he owes nothing. A woman in Emberwatch has asked, in writing, what a person does who is owed an answer, and four letters to her lane are in a drawer unsent.

**Calendar.** The Batch 0004 calendar is canon in `state/continuity.md` and runs from the second week of the second month to the seventh of the fourth. **Chapters 41–43 sit in the fifth month before and around the second Dunn hearing on the twenty-first. Chapters 44–46 are the escalation and its cost. ### Chapter 0041

POV: Orra Dunn

Location/time: The upper seed barn and the Mosswake Roll Court, the third week of the fifth month.

Immediate goal: Get a girl of fifteen into a room on the twenty-first of the fifth month as a person who is going to be asked something, and not as a name in a surety line that a man in Greyfen has been holding for four months.

Resistance: The household has nothing left to sell and the rye is gone. Grice has a served paper, an unexpired grievance, and a lawyer, and he will use the lawyer. Ilsabet Kell is sixty-one and is going to stand in the same room and Orra cannot protect her from that. The new process **did not help her** — it is a flood-season process and her file is a barn term of the third month, and the only reason there is a date at all is that she put her name on a slate in January and would not go home. Information revealed: A creditor with a served paper is not stopped by a pending duty and is not stopped by a request on a board, and a person who has been told *a court cannot promise that nothing will be done before a date* has been told the true thing and has to plan around it. The hearing slate works in a way nobody designed: it is visible to a creditor because it is public, and Orra's visibility is the only thing that has kept Grice's paper from becoming a fact.

Action or decision: Orra writes, in her own hand and with Nessa's name on it, the four questions as a woman of fifteen would put them, and reads them to her daughter, and Nessa changes two of them. Orra then makes the decision that costs her: she does not ask the court to strike Grice's paper, because she cannot, and she asks instead that the hearing be held with the paper read out loud in the room, in front of the person it names, and that the reading be entered. She says out loud that she is asking a court to hear a creditor's paper rather than to stop him, and that she does not know why that is the right thing and is asking anyway.

Emotional change: Orra stops wanting Nessa protected from the hearing and starts wanting her heard in it, which is a harder thing to want and the only one that is available.

Power or resource change: None. A slate, a sheet, and a decision not to ask for something impossible.

Continuity fact: The four questions now exist in a second hand, in a fifteen-year-old's version, and the difference between the two versions is entered. The hearing on the twenty-first is confirmed in writing to the household, and Grice's paper is to be read aloud in the room and entered.

Ending type: Preparation with a cost.

### Chapter 0045

POV: Nell

Location/time: The Venn lane, the reading table, and the Mosswake Roll Court, the second week of the sixth month.

Immediate goal: Get twenty-three letters and eleven answers read out loud in a room where the people who wrote them can hear each other's, and stop being the person who carries paper between two towns.

Resistance: She cannot read for a person who cannot read and she cannot be in two rooms, and the lane's table is a mile from the next province and forty minutes from the court. Information revealed: A public reading is not the same act as a private reading and the difference is not the words, it is **who is in the room.** A question asked in a kitchen can be refused and the refusal is private and costs nothing. A question asked at a table in a lane in front of ninety people cannot be refused by a person who has come to be helped, because refusing is now a public act and a public act has a cost. That is a fact about the method and it is not a discovery anybody wanted. And the fourth question, put to a room of a hundred, finds a hundred different answers, and about sixty of them are *yes, and I get paid*, and about ten are *I do not know*, and about four are *nobody read it to me*, and those four are the ones that are worth a district.

Action or decision: Nell reads the eleven answers from the next province aloud, one at a time, with the writer present and named, and when she gets to the fourth one she stops and says the thing that stops the room, which is that she is going to read out the answer and is not going to say whether it is the right one. Power or resource change: None. Eleven answers read aloud, one assessment read aloud, and a statement of what the board is.

Ending type: Character beat with a method limit stated in public.

### Chapter 0046

POV: Marek

Location/time: Auremar, and the Cinder Court at the Crown Kiln, the third week of the sixth month.

Immediate goal: Put the record in front of the high court and get a named person, any named person, in a document that will be read by somebody in nine provinces in nine years.

Resistance: The Cinder Court is not a bigger version of the Mosswake court and it is not friendlier. It has a clear-hearing rule and a beneficiary rule, and both of them are about the shape of a case, and this case is thin. The reference is a **referral on the record of a provincial court**, brought in the name of that court, and the first thing the registrar does is read the caption twice. The nine paragraphs are not before it and Tamsin's four lines are not before it. The respondent is not a person: the thing that would be answering is a seat, and a seat is not a party, and the registrar has to decide what stands up to speak, and nobody in Auremar has ever had to answer that question.

Information revealed: The Cinder Court's rule is that a clear hearing cannot conceal a beneficiary, and that is the only part of the compact's own procedure that does any work here, and it works on a record that is nothing but names. The court does not decide who authorised the instruction and says so in terms. What it does is take **the public-necessity sentence into its own record as wording**, and hold that the sentence as printed is a model of general wording and not a decision of any court, and that the record of the sentence is a record of a document and not a record of a law. That is narrower than a victory and much more useful than one.

Action or decision: Marek does the thing he has been refusing to do for four months and **stops arguing the case and starts arguing the record.** He gives the court the file and asks it four questions, and three are refused and the fourth is taken, and the fourth is: *whether the sentence as printed in the amendment sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month may be used as the recorded reason for a decision in any district without a hearing of the person it names.* The court takes it. And then, because the court will not say who authorised the sealed instruction, Marek does the other thing: he puts the record of the four months in front of the court **in the form of a list of people who did things in their own names**, and the registrar, who is an ordinary official with a heavy file, reads it, and finds in it a thing nobody had been looking for — not a motive, not a person, but **a pattern of offices**: five different offices, four hundred miles apart, all of which did exactly what they were supposed to do, and one of which is still able to do it tomorrow. And that is what escalates. The **Chancery escalates the sealed First Seat instruction to the Cinder Court**, on the court's own motion, over the applicant's objection, which Marek raises and loses, and which is the volume's last surprise.

Emotional change: Marek loses the last thing he was building toward — an answer with a name on it — and finds that what the court took instead is a list of offices, and that a list of offices is a thing that can be worked on for years, and that this is a worse answer and the only one there is.

Power or resource change: None. No magic, no order of his own, and a hearing he argued badly and a question he did not ask.

Continuity fact: **No guarantor duty may attach to a person who has not been heard and has not signed**, stated in the court's words, entered, and binding on the court that said it. **The public-necessity sentence as printed in the amendment sheet of the twenty-second of the ninth month is recorded as model wording and not as a decision of any court**, and the model may not be used as the recorded reason for a decision in any district without a hearing of the person it names. **Corvin Dray's seal certificates are suspended** on the registrar's own authority and without argument, and the count of them, the number of the certificates, and the thirty-eight lines in three districts go into the record as a mark and not as a name. **The sealed First Seat instruction is escalated to the Cinder Court** on the court's own motion over the applicant's objection. **The court states, with a name at the foot of it, that it does not know and cannot compel who authorised the instruction.** **Seryn Oris is not named, is not present, and is not credited with any action.** The MC/FS series is not before the court and is not examined.

Ending type: Climax, and a bounded resolution.

Next-chapter pull: Everything after this is what a ruling is made of, and the first thing it is made of is a man putting a piece of paper on a nail in a public place and finding that four people have already copied it out.

### Chapter 0047

POV: Ivet Sarn

Location/time: Mosswake Roll Court, the embankment, and the district, the fourth week of the sixth month and the first week of the seventh.

Immediate goal: Get a decision that came from a high court into the hands of the people it names, in a district that is nine provinces from the court that made it, before the eighth month's flood season is three weeks old.

Resistance: The Cinder Court's record is a printed record and it takes six weeks to reach a district on the ordinary distribution, and this district's copy comes as a bundle of forty-one pages with a form of words on the front and nobody at the end of the distribution who has been told what to do with it. The relief rolls are in. There is no process running, because the trial is over and the review created no precedent. And the clerk who has to enter it is the same clerk whose four days in the fifth year are still a paper in a drawer in her own room.

Information revealed: **A decision has to be put somewhere a creditor will read it, and the only somewhere that works is a board at a window where a creditor is already standing.** The Mosswake court puts the Cinder Court's record on the same board as the register of duties and the hearing slate, which is the same wall as before, in the same hand, and a man in a good coat reads all three before he gets to the sill. That is the whole of the transmission mechanism and it is one clerk and one nail. And the people who copy the record out are not officials: they are the professional copyist on the Greyfen road, the two men off the Reed Cut bank, the woman in the hood, a lamp-trimmer who can write, a cooper three hundred miles down the water, and a woman in a dyers' lane who charges nothing for the first ten and is asked to stop by a man with a tray.

Action or decision: Sarn enters the record, copies it to every party on the slate, sells copies at a shilling, and then does the thing that is hers and nobody else's: she writes on the wall, in her own hand, **what the district has to do about it**, which is almost nothing, and says so. The process may be run again. The district has not decided. Four people have put their names on a board asking for a hearing. A man in Greyfen may or may not have a lawyer. **And the wall says all of that, and the wall is the only part of the distribution anybody in this district is certain to read.**

Emotional change: Sarn sees that the thing she was afraid of — being the person who runs a thing she cannot hand on — turns out to be the reason a decision reaches anybody, and that she would rather be frightening and indispensable than tidy and ignored, and that this is the first honest thing she has thought about her own job in nine years.

Power or resource change: None. A board, a nail, and a shilling a copy.

### Chapter 0048

POV: Tamsin

Location/time: Auremar, the copying alcove, and Candlewright Lane, the second and third weeks of the seventh month.

Immediate goal: Find out who has a copy of the Cinder Court's record and what happens to a record that is genuinely held by twenty-nine people, and get a man out of a cell who has been there since the eighteenth of the ninth.

Resistance: A distributed record is not a controlled record, and Tamsin has spent four months making copies she cannot recall, and this is what that looks like. The counting house on the Kiln row has the record and has had it for nine days and has not used it, which is the most frightening possible answer. Bryn Mawer is seventeen, is not a protected witness, was never on the bench, and has been copying for two years and has a line in a file and no wages. And **Corvin Dray's plea must be set, and his trial is in Volume 02, and it is set here** — refused, formally, in a room, with the four things he may not do written down.

Information revealed: Copies are not a network, and the moment a copy is in a building where somebody sells things, the copy is a **commercial fact** and not a public one, and the person who understands that fastest is the person it hurts most. The record is read in the counting house and it changes nothing on the day it arrives and may change something in six months, and there is no instrument in the world that says a person may not keep a document, and none should be invented here. Tamsin's list is now the only thing in the empire that knows where thirty of the thirty are.

Action or decision: Tamsin does the thing she has been building toward since the twelfth of the twelfth and does not want to: she **hands the list over.** Not to an office. To the four people who have proved they can hold a list — a copyist with a shop and a price, a clerk who keeps two registers, a foreman who keeps a works book, and a woman in a dyers' lane who is the only one of the four who is not a clerk. Each gets a page, not the book, and the pages disagree in one place, and the disagreement is written down, and the rule is the same one from the twenty-fourth of the twelfth: a list of who holds a copy is not a list of people who have reported. And Bryn Mawer is asked whether she wants to be examined in the copying, again, in her own words, and the answer is on the page.

Emotional change: Tamsin gives up being the person who knows, and finds out that what she gets back is a list with four people's handwriting on it and a name she does not know, and that being a person among forty who each hold a page is not a smaller version of what she had. It is a version that cannot be lost in a drawer.

Power or resource change: None. A list, four pages, a fee at a chandler's counter, and a trial date.

Continuity fact: **The copy list is now held in four places, in four hands, and the four pages are not identical and say so.** Bryn Mawer is asked again and answers. **Corvin Dray's plea is refused on the record and his trial is set for Volume 02**; he names no client, and the counting house's mark is on the record and not a name. The copy of the Cinder Court's record is in the counting house and has been for nine days.

Ending type: Handover, and a cost that is not a loss.

Next-chapter pull: A girl in a dyers' lane in another district has been putting four questions to a man in a good coat for two months and has got nowhere, and she has written to a lane, and a woman off the vats has a cart and no coach and a question that has been asked in the wrong direction for two months.

### Chapter 0049

POV: Nell

Location/time: Greyfen, the dyers' lane, and Emberwatch, the fourth week of the seventh month and the first week of the eighth.

Immediate goal: Take four hundred and forty miles in one trip with no money and no authority, to a trade that has never had a dispute in forty years, to answer a question that four people asked in writing in the fourth month.

Resistance: She is the only person who can go, because the four in Emberwatch have never been asked by anybody who was not sent to them, and Bettin Rahl is the only door and he is not going to let an uninvited woman of twenty-four past it. The journey is four days each way on a coach and it is the whole of a month's wages at a wage of four shillings a day. There is a levy to argue and an assessment to fight and a trade's own decision that eleven houses are still inside. And when she gets there she discovers that the four have not been idle: they have written to two other trades, and one of those trades has written back, and a thing that four people in a kitchen started in the fourth month is now in four buildings in three districts, and **nobody put it there.**

Information revealed: The thing that travels is not a form and not a question and not a ruling. It is a **person who turns up.** A dyers' trade with one door, told by a woman with a carriage clock and a bad hat that the door is optional, will find out in about a year that it has four doors, and that this is the whole of the institutional change available to a trade of two thousand people, and it will not cost anything, and it will not be a rule, and it will be entirely untraceable. And the person who has to say it is a twenty-year-old dye-house worker who is going to have to say it in a room with an assessor in it who has never been asked anything in nine years and does not like it.

Action or decision: Nell goes, and is made to go through the assessor because that is the rule and because going round it would prove nothing, and then spends nine days in Emberwatch **not advising anybody**, and then does the one thing she is good at, which is ask the fourth question in a room of a trade and write the answer down. The answer is *yes, and he is paid by the combine, and there is only one of him.* And then she asks Bettin Rahl the fifth question nobody has asked, which is what a house does when the assessor says no, and Rahl, who has had that question waiting for thirty-one years, cannot answer it and says so, and then writes it down.

Emotional change: Nell discovers that a lane's practice becomes a trade's practice when somebody with no authority turns up, and that the practice still does not belong to her, and that this is what she wanted and is also the end of the only thing she was ever going to be first at.

Power or resource change: None. A coach fare, nine days, and a book that belongs to somebody else.

Continuity fact: The Emberwatch house has opened its correspondence to two other trades and one has written back, and nobody put it there. The Emberwatch Dyers' Combination's rule is **still in force** and Bettin Rahl has written the unanswered question into the Combination's own book at the second leaf, next to the rule, in his own hand, on a page nobody has read for thirty-one years. **The four questions are now asked by a trade, in a furnace town, and the answer is that the one man in the building is paid to read.**

Ending type: Payoff, and the pattern is out of anybody's hands.

Next-chapter pull: A coach on the road back, a woman in a furnace town who has been eleven years in a room, and an archive in Auremar that burned twelve years ago.

### Chapter 0050

POV: Marek

Location/time: Auremar, the third gallery, the received-room shelf, and the Ashfall wrapper, the second and third weeks of the eighth month.

Immediate goal: Close the file, and put the corrected Venn ruling where two kinds of people can read it, and find out what there is in this building he has been told not to look at twice.

Resistance: The file cannot be closed, because the request of the eighteenth of the ninth is alive and the second sealed page is shut and there is a refusal against his name in a form in Examinations, and the correct end to his time at the Inheritance Desk is not a decision but a **transfer with a date on it.** The Kest file is formally reopened by the Registrar and **not cleared**, and his mother's name stays a redaction in the public roll and a copy in his father's tin, and reopening it means that anybody at the Roll Hall counter may now ask for the file, and every one of them will be a person he did not decide to tell. And he has said he would not open the second sealed page and he is not going to, and the batch ends with him standing in a room where he has decided not to look at something, which is what he has been doing since the fifth of the ninth month.

Information revealed: **A red thread runs across the collated edge of a burned archive wrapper, and it runs onto the leaf beneath it, and the leaf beneath it is not from this year.** It is a stitching mark — a thread used by a copyist to mark a passage in a bundle, going through two or more leaves at once, which is what Ilyra Kest's red-thread copies were and what nobody else's were. It is on a wrapper in the received room, dated twelve years ago, from a branch archive that burned and was never fully inventoried, and the inventory of it has a line that says *damaged, four items, uncollated*, and the four items are in a bundle that was never opened because opening it was somebody else's decision. **The thread is not a message. It is a way of marking a page, and a way of marking a page is a person who was working and not a person who was plotting, and that is a smaller and more human thing and it does not tell him who was right.** Nobody in the batch claims the stitch in the form's fold is the same thread, and nobody may.

Action or decision: Marek does three ordinary things. He posts the corrected Venn ruling in **two places** — the Roll Hall reading steps and the Mosswake court wall — in the form of a sheet with all five terms on it and the date, so that a person who has heard four of them in a lane can see the fifth. And he writes to Nell, who has not written to him since the twenty-ninth of the first month, and the letter is four lines and it is about the levy and not about the ninth month. He receives no answer inside the volume, and the receiving of no answer is the last line of the book, and it is not a cliffhanger: it is a man who has spent a volume learning to say out loud what he wants, in four lines, to a person who is entitled to answer in her own time or not at all.

Emotional change: Marek has got what he came for and the volume ends with him without a standing, without a promotion, without a decision on the request he filed, and with a bundle of four uncollated items on a shelf he has decided not to open, and with a red thread that gives him a route and not an answer, and he is not wretched, and he is not happy, and he has somebody's name at the bottom of four hundred and forty lines of public record and there is not one line of it that says he was right.
